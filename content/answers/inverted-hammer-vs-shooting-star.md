An inverted hammer and a shooting star are the same candle: a small body near the low with a long upper shadow. After a fall it is a bullish inverted hammer; after a rise it is a bearish shooting star, and in our tests they behaved very differently.

## One shape, two names

Both candles have:

- a small body in the lower part of the range;
- an upper shadow at least about twice the body;
- little or no lower shadow.

During the period, buyers pushed price well above the open, then sellers pushed it back down to close near the low.

A [shooting star](/shooting-star/) appears after a rise. Its story is easy: buyers tried to extend the rally, failed, and price closed near its low. It is the bearish mirror of the hammer.

An [inverted hammer](/inverted-hammer/) appears after a fall. Its story is less obvious. The candle closed near its low, which sounds bearish. The bullish reading is that, after a run of selling, buyers showed up at all and pushed price well above the open. If the next candle opens and closes above the inverted hammer's body, those buyers are taking over. Because the candle's own close is weak, most writers say the inverted hammer needs confirmation more than almost any other pattern.

When the body shrinks to nothing, the shape becomes a [gravestone doji](/gravestone-doji/).

## Side by side

| | Inverted hammer | Shooting star |
|---|---|---|
| Comes after | A fall | A rise |
| Reading | Bullish, tentative | Bearish |
| Story | Buyers appear, but lose the period | Buyers fail at higher prices |
| Confirmation | Usually required | Often used |
| Stop for a trade | Under the low | Above the high |
| Our test (stop 1 ATR) | Exploratory, not a tested result; held-back test under review | No evidence of an edge |

## What our tests found

First pass: daily bars for 10 US stocks and funds (SPY, QQQ, IWM, Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla, Amazon, JPMorgan, Exxon), January 2021 to October 2025, tuning period. Entry at the next open, 2R target, 20-day limit, spread paid both ways, compared with random entries in the same markets and quarters. 110 variants were tried in that pass.

| Pattern | Stop | Trades | Edge over random entry, R per trade | 95% range | Same-day exits | Label |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inverted hammer (long) | 1 ATR from close | 96 | +0.38 | +0.01 to +0.75 | 20% | Exploratory, not a tested result |
| Inverted hammer (long) | Under the low | 98 | +0.03 | -0.20 to +0.27 | 82% | No evidence of an edge |
| Shooting star (short) | 1 ATR from close | 111 | +0.07 | -0.16 to +0.30 | 18% | No evidence of an edge |
| Shooting star (short) | Above the high | 114 | -0.11 | -0.32 to +0.12 | 42% | No evidence of an edge |

The inverted hammer with the wider stop was the strongest of all 110 variants, but under 100 trades and not strong enough to pass the adjustment for testing so many. So we re-ran it on 39 markets it had not been picked on: 26 Dow Jones Industrial Average members (as of January 2021), the 11 Select Sector SPDR funds, gold and bitcoin. Same rules, still the tuning period, January 2021 to October 2025: **420 trades, edge over random entry +0.20R per trade** (95% range +0.07 to +0.33). Label: **exploratory, not a tested result**. A test on data held back from both runs has been run and its result is being reviewed before we publish it. ([How we test](/methods/).)

Three things to take from that, none of which is in either guide:

1. **The stop rule decided the result.** Under the low, 82% of inverted hammer trades ended the day they began, and the edge vanished. The inverted hammer's low is close to its close, so the next day's ordinary movement hits it.
2. **The effect shrank on new markets**, from +0.38R to +0.20R. That is normal when a result is picked as the best of many; part of the first figure was likely luck.
3. **The shooting star, the "easier" signal, did nothing** over random entry in this sample. Our test markets rose strongly over the period, which works against short trades and is worth keeping in mind.

## Worked example

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A stock falls from 30 to 24 over two weeks. Today it opens at 24.10, rallies to 25.40, and closes at 24.20. Body 0.10, upper shadow 1.20, no lower shadow. That is an inverted hammer.

A trader using our test's rules buys at the next open, 24.30, with a stop one average daily range (say 0.80) below the signal close: 23.40. Risk is 0.90. Target at 2R: 26.10.

Now put the same candle on a stock that has risen from 18 to 24. It is a shooting star. A trader sells at the next open, 24.10, with a stop one range above the signal close at 25.00. Risk 0.90; target 22.30.

## Verdict

The two candles differ only in the trend before them. The shooting star has the clearer story, but in our tuning-period test it showed no evidence of an edge. The inverted hammer, with a stop one average range away, was our most interesting candlestick result; it is still exploratory, not a tested result, and a test on held-back data is being reviewed. Either way, give the stop room.
