Two touches make a level for most traders, and a third clear test is usually taken as confirmation. After that, more touches do not simply mean a stronger level. Each test fills some of the orders waiting there, so a level tested again and again, with weaker bounces, is often close to breaking.

## Why two, why three

One turning point is just a swing. It might be a level, but nothing has repeated. Two turns near the same price show that the market reacted there twice, which is the minimum for calling it a level. Three tests show the reaction was not a coincidence, which is why the "rule of three" appears in so many trading books.

None of these numbers come from a test. They are conventions. They work as a way to decide when to start paying attention to a price.

## The problem with "more is stronger"

The common idea that every extra touch makes a level stronger misses how levels work. A level holds because orders are waiting there. Each test fills some of them.

Think of a support at 50 with buyers waiting. The first test fills the most eager buyers. The second fills more. By the fifth test, the buyers who wanted 50 have mostly bought. If sellers keep coming, the level gives way.

That is the logic of the [ascending triangle](/ascending-triangle/): flat resistance tested again and again by rising lows. The repeated tests are not a sign the resistance is strong. They are a sign buyers keep coming back to it, and the classic reading is that it eventually breaks upward.

## Getting stronger or wearing out?

Look at how each test behaves, not just how many there are.

| Sign | Level holding up | Level wearing out |
|---|---|---|
| Size of bounce | Similar or larger each time | Smaller each time |
| Time between tests | Weeks apart | Shorter and shorter |
| Depth of each poke | Wicks barely reach the zone | Wicks go further through |
| Lows on the other side | Not rising towards the level | Rising lows (into resistance) or falling highs (into support) |
| Volume on tests | Selling dries up at support | Selling grows at support |

## Worked example: two supports with five touches

**Share A.** Support at 30. Tests in January, March, May, July and September. Each bounce reached about 34. Each test barely touched 30. Five touches, spread out, same reaction each time. This level is behaving well.

**Share B.** Support at 30. Tests on five days within three weeks. Bounces reached 33, 32.20, 31.50, 31.00, 30.60. Highs falling towards the level. That is a descending triangle in shape. Five touches again, but this one looks tired.

Same count, opposite meaning. The count told you there was a level. The behaviour tells you what it is doing.

## Counting touches honestly

- **Count clear tests, not every bar near the level.** Price sitting at a level for ten bars is one test.
- **Count turns that started a move.** A pause inside a trend is weaker than a real swing. See [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/).
- **Count on one timeframe.** A level tested five times on the 5-minute chart may be one test on the daily.
- **Include both sides.** A level that was resistance twice and support once has three tests.

## When one touch is enough

Some levels matter after a single touch:

- An all-time high or a multi-year low.
- A sharp turn with a very large move away, especially on a weekly chart.
- A turn at a round number that many traders already watch.

In these cases the one reaction was large or visible enough that traders remember it. Supply and demand traders actively prefer zones with no retest yet, on the logic that unused zones still have their orders. See the [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) guide for how these views fit together.

## Verdict

Two touches make a level, three confirm it, and after that, watch the behaviour, not the count. Bounces that shrink and tests that bunch up suggest a level running out of orders. Spread-out tests with steady reactions suggest a level still in use. Counting touches is a reason to notice a level, not a reason to trust it more each time.
