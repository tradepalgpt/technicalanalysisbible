Use more than one. Weekly and daily levels are the ones most traders watch, so they tend to matter most. Your trading timeframe adds the nearer levels you need for entries and stops. A common pattern is two steps up from the chart you trade: daily and weekly for swing traders, hourly and daily for day traders.

## Why higher timeframes usually matter more

A weekly swing high is built from five days of trading. Thousands of traders on many timeframes can see it. A one-minute swing high is built from one minute, and most traders never see it. Levels matter because of the orders and attention they gather, so the more visible a level, the more likely it is to matter.

That does not make lower-timeframe levels useless. They are where your entry and stop live. But a five-minute support just under a daily resistance is not much of a support.

## A simple top-down routine

| Your style | Map levels from | Fine-tune on | Enter on |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position (weeks to months) | Monthly, weekly | Daily | Daily |
| Swing (days to weeks) | Weekly, daily | 4-hour | 4-hour or 1-hour |
| Day trading | Daily, 4-hour, plus [previous day's high and low](/previous-day-high-low/) | 1-hour | 5 to 15-minute |
| Scalping | 1-hour, session levels | 5-minute | 1-minute |

The rule of thumb is: map two steps up, enter one step down from your map. The exact steps matter less than doing it the same way each time.

## Worked example: a day trader

A day trader trades an index future on the 5-minute chart.

**Daily chart.** Resistance zone 5,180 to 5,195 (three swing highs over a month). Support zone 5,060 to 5,075 (two lows).

**Session levels.** Previous day's high 5,152, low 5,108. Overnight high 5,160.

**Hourly chart.** A swing low at 5,128 from yesterday afternoon.

**5-minute chart.** Many small swings. Only one is kept: this morning's low at 5,136.

Map for the day, from top down:

| Level | Source | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| 5,180 to 5,195 | Daily resistance | High |
| 5,160 | Overnight high | Medium |
| 5,152 | Previous day high | Medium |
| 5,136 | This morning's low | Low |
| 5,128 | Hourly swing low | Medium |
| 5,108 | Previous day low | Medium |
| 5,060 to 5,075 | Daily support | High |

Price opens at 5,145. The trader's first questions: will it clear the previous day's high at 5,152 and the overnight high at 5,160? If it does, the next level is the daily zone at 5,180, which gives room for a trade. If it fails, 5,136 and 5,128 are the nearby supports.

The 5-minute chart provides the trigger. The higher levels decide where the trade is going and whether there is room.

## How many timeframes is too many?

Three is a sensible maximum. Beyond that, levels from every timeframe pile up, and price is always near something. If your map has more than about six or seven levels for a day, delete from the lowest timeframe first.

## When timeframes conflict

A common situation: daily resistance just above, hourly support just below. Price is squeezed between them. There are two honest choices:

- Wait for price to leave the squeeze, then trade towards the next level.
- Skip the trade, because there is no room either way.

Trading inside a squeeze between opposing levels usually means small rewards and frequent stops.

## Markets with sessions

In markets that close, gaps and session highs and lows add another layer. In 24-hour markets like forex and crypto, the daily candle's close time decides your daily levels, and it differs between platforms. See the [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) guide for session levels and [key levels](/key-levels/) for how traders shortlist them.

## Verdict

There is no single best timeframe. Levels from weekly and daily charts carry the most weight because the most traders see them. Your own timeframe supplies entries and stops. Map two steps up, trade one step down, keep the map short, and skip trades squeezed between opposing levels.
