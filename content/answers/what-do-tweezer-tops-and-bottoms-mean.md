Tweezer tops are two candles with matching highs after a rise, and tweezer bottoms are two candles with matching lows after a fall: price tested the same level twice in a row and was rejected both times. They are read as reversal signals, best at a known level and with confirmation.

## What they look like

[Tweezer tops](/tweezer-tops/): after a rise, two neighbouring candles reach the same high, or very nearly. Classically the first is green, carrying the rally, and the second is red, opening near the high and falling away. The highs are the two prongs of the tweezers.

[Tweezer bottoms](/tweezer-bottoms/): after a fall, two neighbouring candles reach the same low. Classically the first is red and the second green.

The shapes of the individual candles do not matter much. One might be a shooting star, one a large body. What makes the pattern is the matching extreme.

## What they mean

Two periods in a row, price reached a level and could not get past it. That is a small version of a [double top](/double-top/) or double bottom, compressed into two candles. The level held twice; the side pushing into it ran out of strength.

In price-action terms, matching highs or lows are also [equal highs and lows](/equal-highs-lows/), which many traders watch as places where stop orders collect. That gives a second, opposite reading: an obvious double level can attract a later push through it, to trigger those stops. A tweezer top today can become the level that is swept tomorrow.

## How "matching" is decided

This is where tweezers differ most between platforms. "The same high" is rarely exact to the cent. Rules include:

- highs within a fixed number of ticks or pips;
- highs within a small percentage of each other, such as 0.1%;
- highs within a fraction of the average daily range, such as 5%.

A loose tolerance finds many tweezers; a strict one finds few. Two screeners can disagree completely on the same chart. Whatever rule you use, write it down and keep it.

## What our tests found

First pass: daily bars for 10 US stocks and funds (SPY, QQQ, IWM, Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla, Amazon, JPMorgan, Exxon), January 2021 to October 2025, tuning period. Entry at the next open, 2R target, 20-day limit, costs both ways, compared with random entries in the same markets and quarters. 110 variants were tried in that pass.

| Pattern | Stop | Trades | Edge over random entry, R per trade | 95% range | Label |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tweezer tops (short) | 1 ATR from close | 59 | +0.37 | +0.03 to +0.71 | Exploratory, not a tested result |
| Tweezer tops (short) | Above the highs | 59 | +0.11 | -0.24 to +0.45 | Exploratory, not a tested result |
| Tweezer bottoms (long) | 1 ATR from close | 33 | -0.20 | -0.59 to +0.25 | No evidence of an edge |
| Tweezer bottoms (long) | Below the lows | 33 | -0.20 | -0.55 to +0.23 | No evidence of an edge |

Tweezer tops with the wider stop was the second-best of the 110 variants, on only 59 trades. A follow-up test on 39 different markets (26 Dow Jones Industrial Average members as of January 2021, 11 sector funds, gold and bitcoin), still in the tuning period, January 2021 to October 2025, gave **215 trades and an edge over random entry of -0.07R per trade** (95% range -0.24 to +0.13). Label: **no evidence of an edge**. The rules for what would count as holding up were written down before the new data was fetched. ([How we test](/methods/).)

That is the most useful thing we can tell you about tweezers, and it is not in either guide: **a result that looked strong on 59 trades in 10 markets did not carry over to 215 trades in 39 others.** Small samples flatter. It is a good reason to treat any striking pattern statistic, from us or anyone, as a question rather than an answer until it has been repeated on new data.

Note too that on the 39 markets, 30% of tweezer top trades ended on the entry day even with the wider stop.

## How traders use them

1. **At a known level.** A tweezer top that forms at last month's high, a round number or a falling trendline is a double rejection of a level that already mattered. One in open space is much weaker.
2. **With confirmation.** A close below the tweezer's lower low (for tops) confirms that sellers have followed through.
3. **With a stop above the matching highs**, plus a buffer, or one average range away. Since the highs are an obvious level, stops just above them can be swept.
4. **On daily or higher charts.** On short timeframes, neighbouring candles touch the same price all the time.

## Worked example

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A stock rises from 140 to 162 over three weeks, into resistance at 162.50 from earlier in the year.

- Day 1: opens 159, high 162.40, closes 162.
- Day 2: opens 161.80, high 162.45, closes 159.50.

Highs of 162.40 and 162.45 match within a few cents: a tweezer top at resistance. Day 3 closes at 158.60, below Day 2's low of 158.80. A trader sells at the next open, 158.50, with a stop one average range (say 3.00) above the signal close, at 161.60. Risk 3.10. Target 2R: 152.30.

Notice the stop sits below the tweezer highs. A trader who wants it above them, at 162.70, risks 4.20 instead.

## Verdict

Tweezer tops and bottoms mean a level was tested twice in a row and held. That is a clear story, but in our tests it did not hold up: tweezer tops looked promising on 59 trades and showed no evidence of an edge on 215 trades across 39 further markets, and tweezer bottoms showed no evidence of an edge from the start. Use tweezers to mark levels, not as signals on their own.
