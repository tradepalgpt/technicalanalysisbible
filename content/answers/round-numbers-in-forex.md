Yes, often. Big figures such as 1.1000 in EUR/USD attract clusters of take-profit and stop orders, and research on bank order books found exactly that clustering. Round numbers are not walls, though. Price often reacts a few pips early, or pokes through to trigger stops before turning.

## The evidence

Most claims about round numbers are trader lore. One is backed by research. Carol Osler studied the stop-loss and take-profit orders a large bank held for currency clients. Her 2003 paper in the *Journal of Finance* found that the orders clustered at round numbers, and that take-profit orders and stop orders clustered in different ways around them. That gives a mechanical reason why round numbers can act as levels in forex: the orders are really there.

We are not quoting figures from the paper. Two limits are worth stating. It looked at one bank's client orders, in a specific period. And it explains why reactions can happen near round numbers, not that they always will.

## Which round numbers

Forex dealers have names for the levels.

| Level | Example (EUR/USD) | Example (USD/JPY) | Usual weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Big figure | 1.1000, 1.2000 | 150.00, 151.00 | Highest |
| Half figure | 1.1050 | 150.50 | Medium |
| Quarter levels | 1.1025, 1.1075 | 150.25, 150.75 | Low |
| Major "century" levels | 1.0000 (parity), 1.5000 | 100.00, 150.00 | Very high, rare |

Parity in EUR/USD and levels like 100.00 or 150.00 in USD/JPY are the most watched of all. Headlines are written about them. Options are often struck there.

The more levels you add, the less each means. If you mark every 25 pips, price is always near one.

## The level before the level

Because many orders sit right at the round number, some traders place theirs just before it to get filled first.

- A buyer wanting 1.1000 puts a limit at 1.1005 or 1.1010.
- A trader taking profit on a long into 1.1000 sells at 1.0990 to 1.0995.

So reactions often start a few pips before the round number. On the other side, stops tend to sit just beyond the round number. If price pushes through, it can trigger them in a burst, then turn back once they are filled. That is a [liquidity sweep](/liquidity-sweep/) in newer language. Osler's work found stop orders and take-profit orders were placed differently around round numbers, which fits this picture.

## Worked example

EUR/USD is falling towards 1.1000. A trader wants to buy if it holds.

**Entry.** Not a limit at exactly 1.1000. Instead, wait for a reaction in the 1.0990 to 1.1015 band, then buy on a close back above 1.1010 on the hourly chart. Say the entry is 1.1018.

**Stop.** Not at 1.0995. That is where everyone's stops are. The current hourly ATR is 15 pips. Put the stop at 1.0970: 30 pips below the round number, giving room for a sweep. Risk 48 pips (1R).

**Target.** The next level above is a swing high at 1.1090, also near the half figure 1.1100 below it. Target 1.1085, just before it. Reward 67 pips, about 1.4R.

That is a thin reward. The trader might take it, size down, or skip it. Round numbers give the entry area; they do not change the arithmetic.

## When round numbers matter most

- **At new territory.** When a pair reaches a level it has not seen for years, round numbers are the main reference.
- **On higher timeframes.** Big figures on the daily chart matter more than quarter levels on the 5-minute chart.
- **Near events.** Options expiries and central bank decisions tend to focus attention on big figures.

When a round number sits on top of an old swing level, it usually matters more than either alone. See the [round numbers](/round-numbers/) guide and the [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) guide for how to combine them.

## Common mistakes

- Limit orders exactly at the round number, which may not fill.
- Stops one or two pips beyond the round number, which are easy to hit.
- Treating every quarter level as important.
- Forgetting that JPY pairs and others quote to a different number of decimals.

## Verdict

Round numbers matter in forex for a real reason: orders cluster there, as bank order data showed. Use big figures and a few major levels, not every quarter level. Expect reactions slightly before the number and sweeps slightly beyond it. Place entries a little early and stops a little further away than feels natural.
