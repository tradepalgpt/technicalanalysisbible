A long upper wick means buyers pushed price well above the open during the period, but sellers pushed it back down before the close. At resistance after a rise it shows higher prices being rejected; after a fall, or in a quiet range, it can mean something quite different.

## What the wick records

A candle's upper wick (or shadow) runs from the top of the body to the high of the period. A long one means price spent part of the period much higher than where it finished. Somebody was willing to buy up there, and then sellers took over.

That is the whole fact. Everything else is interpretation, and the interpretation depends on three things: where the candle is, what came before it, and how long "long" is.

## How long is long?

There is no single rule. Common guides:

- **Compared with the body:** an upper wick two or more times the body, the usual rule for the [shooting star](/shooting-star/) and [inverted hammer](/inverted-hammer/).
- **Compared with the range:** the upper wick is more than half of the whole candle's range.
- **Compared with recent candles:** the wick alone is larger than the average range of the last 10 to 20 candles.

The third is the most useful. A wick that is long compared with its own body but tiny compared with a normal day's range is not saying much.

## Four places, four meanings

| Where it appears | Common name | Usual reading |
|---|---|---|
| After a rise, at resistance | Shooting star, gravestone doji | Higher prices rejected; bearish warning |
| After a fall | Inverted hammer | Buyers appeared; tentatively bullish, needs confirmation |
| Just above a prior high, closing back below | Sweep, failed breakout | Stops above the high triggered, then reversed |
| In a quiet range | No special name | Little meaning |

The third row comes from newer price-action language. When price briefly trades above an obvious level, such as the [previous day's high](/previous-day-high-low/), and closes back below it, traders call it a [liquidity sweep](/liquidity-sweep/): stop orders above the high were triggered, filled by sellers, and price fell back. The candle that records this has a long upper wick poking above the level.

The second row surprises many people. A long upper wick after a fall is read as bullish by some candlestick writers, because buyers were strong enough to push price up at all. It is the less intuitive reading and the one that most needs confirmation.

## What our test found

We tested the two named patterns built on a long upper wick, using daily bars for 10 US stocks and funds (SPY, QQQ, IWM, Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla, Amazon, JPMorgan, Exxon) over the tuning period, January 2021 to October 2025, with a stop one average daily range from the signal close, a 2R target, a 20-day limit, and random entries as the comparison.

- **Shooting star (sell after a rise):** 111 trades, edge over random entry +0.07R per trade (95% range -0.16 to +0.30). **No evidence of an edge.**
- **Inverted hammer (buy after a fall):** 96 trades, edge +0.38R per trade (95% range +0.01 to +0.75). **Exploratory, not a tested result.** On 39 further markets over the same tuning period it gave 420 trades and an edge of +0.20R. A follow-up test is under way.
- **Gravestone doji:** 18 trades, too few to report.

([How we test](/methods/).)

The surprising part, which neither guide has: in this sample, the "obvious" bearish reading of a long upper wick did nothing over random entry, while the less obvious bullish reading after a fall was the more interesting result. Our test markets rose strongly over the period, which works against short trades, so treat that contrast with care.

## How to judge a long upper wick

1. **Check the level.** Did the wick reach a known resistance, prior high, round number or the upper edge of a band? A wick into empty space is weaker.
2. **Check the close.** The lower the close in the range, the stronger the rejection.
3. **Check the next candle.** A follow-through close below the wick candle's low confirms the rejection. A close above its high cancels it.
4. **Check the timeframe.** On 1 to 5-minute charts, long wicks appear whenever a large order passes through. On daily charts they reflect a full session.
5. **Check the news.** A spike on a headline that reverses within minutes is a reaction, not necessarily a turn.

## Worked example

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A currency pair rallies from 1.0800 to 1.0950 over a week. Yesterday's high was 1.0960. Today it rises to 1.0985, then falls back to close at 1.0935, below the open at 1.0945. Upper wick: 0.0040 from the open to the high, four times the 0.0010 body, and larger than the 0.0030 average daily range.

That candle has a long upper wick, at a level (above yesterday's high), after a rise, closing below the level. Three readings line up: shooting star, failed breakout, sweep of yesterday's high.

A trader selling at the next open of 1.0933 with a stop one average range above the signal close (1.0965) risks 0.0032. A 2R target sits at 1.0869.

## Verdict

A long upper wick means higher prices were tried and rejected within the period. It is most meaningful after a rise, at a level, on a daily or higher chart. On its own, the shooting star version showed no evidence of an edge in our tuning-period test; the inverted hammer version, after a fall, was more interesting and is still exploratory.
