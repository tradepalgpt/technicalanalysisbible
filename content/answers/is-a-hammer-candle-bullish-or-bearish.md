A hammer candle is read as bullish: it shows sellers pushed price down and buyers drove it back up to close near the high. But it is only a hammer after a fall; the same shape after a rise is a bearish hanging man, and on its own the hammer showed no evidence of an edge in our test.

## Why the hammer is read as bullish

A [hammer](/hammer/) has a small body near the top of its range, a long lower shadow at least about twice the body, and little or no upper shadow. The story is simple. During the period, sellers pushed price well below the open. Then buyers took over and pushed it back up, so that by the close most of the drop had been erased.

After a decline, that is a change in behaviour. For days or weeks, every push lower held. Now a push lower was rejected inside a single period. Traders read that as the first sign that selling is running out and buyers are willing to pay higher prices.

The colour of the body matters less than the shadow. A green hammer (close above open) is often called slightly stronger than a red one, because buyers took price above where it started. But both have the same basic shape and the same story.

## Why the answer depends on what came before

The word "hammer" includes its context. The candle shape alone is neutral; the name assumes a prior fall. The same shape after a rise is called a [hanging man](/hanging-man/), and it is read as a bearish warning: sellers managed to push price a long way down during the period, even though it recovered by the close.

So the honest answer has three parts:

| Where the shape appears | Name | Usual reading |
|---|---|---|
| After a clear fall | Hammer | Bullish reversal signal |
| After a clear rise | Hanging man | Bearish warning |
| In a sideways range | No special name | Close to meaningless |

The third row is the one most people forget. A hammer-shaped candle in the middle of a quiet range is just a day when price dipped and came back. It tells you very little.

## What our test found

We ran a hammer detector on daily bars for 10 large US stocks and funds (SPY, QQQ, IWM, Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla, Amazon, JPMorgan and Exxon) over the tuning period, January 2021 to October 2025. Each hammer was bought at the next day's open, with a stop one average daily range from the signal close, a target of twice the risk (2R), and a 20-day time limit. Every trade was compared with random entries on the same markets in the same quarters.

Result: **191 trades, edge over random entry +0.02R per trade** (95% range -0.18 to +0.24). Label: **no evidence of an edge**. With the stop just under the hammer's low instead, 195 trades gave an edge of +0.04R, also no evidence of an edge, and 40% of those trades were stopped out on the day they were entered. ([How we test](/methods/).)

That figure is what the guide does not tell you. Mechanically, "buy every hammer after a 10-bar dip" did about as well as buying on random days. The bullish reading of the hammer is a description of the candle, not a reliable prediction.

Two cautions on that result. First, our "after a fall" rule was a simple 10-bar direction, so many test hammers came after shallow dips. Second, the test ignores location entirely. A hammer at a twice-tested support level is a different thing from a hammer in open space, and our detector cannot see the difference.

## What makes a hammer worth acting on

Traders who use hammers well tend to add three filters:

1. **A real fall first.** Several days of lower highs and lower lows, or price well below a short moving average.
2. **A level.** The hammer's low tests a known [support level](/support-and-resistance/), a round number, the prior swing low or a Fibonacci retracement.
3. **Confirmation.** The next candle closes above the hammer's high, or at least above its close.

Above-average volume on the hammer itself adds weight: more traders took part in rejecting the lower prices.

## Worked example

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A stock falls from 80 to 66 over four weeks. It previously bottomed at 65 in the spring. Today it opens at 67, drops to 64.50, and closes at 67.40. Body: 0.40. Lower shadow: 2.50, more than six times the body. Upper shadow: none. Volume is 1.6 times its 20-day average.

- Clear prior fall: yes.
- At a level: yes, the low dipped under the spring low at 65 and recovered.
- Volume: above normal.

Tomorrow it closes at 68.10, above the hammer's high. A trader using next-bar confirmation buys at the following open, 68.20, with a stop under the hammer's low at 64.40. Risk is 3.80 per share. A 2R target sits at 75.80, below the 80 starting point of the fall.

Compare that with a hammer of the same shape on a stock that has drifted between 64 and 70 for two months. Same candle, no fall, no level. A mechanical scan would treat them the same. A trader should not.

## Verdict

The hammer is a bullish candle in the sense that it describes buyers winning the period after a fall. It is not a bullish signal on its own: in our tuning-period test of 191 trades it did no better than random entry. Treat it as a reason to look closer at a level you already care about, not as a reason to buy. For how it compares with its bearish twin, see [What is the difference between a hammer and a hanging man?](/answers/difference-between-hammer-and-hanging-man/), and for the full family of patterns, the [candlestick patterns guide](/guide/candlestick-patterns/).
