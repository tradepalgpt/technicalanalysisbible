The 200-day moving average works well as a summary of the long-term trend: whether the past year has been up or down. As a buy and sell signal it is slow, it whipsaws when price goes sideways near it, and the historical evidence for it as a timing rule is mixed.

## What the 200-day actually is

It is the [simple moving average](/simple-moving-average/) of the last 200 daily closes. With around 252 trading days in a year, that covers roughly ten months. It is the most widely quoted average in markets. When a report says a stock "broke below its 200-day", this is the line.

Because it averages so many days, it moves slowly. A single big day barely shifts it. That is its strength as context and its weakness as a signal.

## Two different questions

"Does it work?" usually means one of two things.

1. **Does it describe the long-term trend?** Yes, almost by definition. If price is above a rising 200-day average, the past ten months have been mostly up. That is useful context for anyone choosing which way to trade.
2. **Does crossing it predict what happens next?** That is the harder question. The honest answer is: sometimes in some markets and periods, and not reliably enough to assume.

## The common rule and its costs

The classic rule is simple: be in the market while price closes above the 200-day, be out (or short) when it closes below. It is a [trend-following](/trend-following/) rule. It aims to miss the worst of long declines, in exchange for being late into recoveries.

What it costs:

| Situation | What the rule does | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Long steady uptrend | Stays in for months | Rides most of it |
| Sharp crash | Exits after price has already fallen some way | Avoids part of the decline, not all |
| V-shaped recovery | Re-enters well above the low | Misses the first part of the rebound |
| Long flat market near the line | Crosses in and out repeatedly | Many small losses and costs |

The fourth row is the problem. Markets often spend months going sideways around their 200-day. A close-above / close-below rule can trade many times in that period, each time losing a little.

### A worked whipsaw

An index is flat around 4,000 for four months. Its 200-day average is also near 4,000 and almost flat.

- Week 1: close 4,030, above the line. Rule enters long.
- Week 3: close 3,970, below. Rule exits. Loss about 60 points plus costs.
- Week 5: close 4,040, above. Rule re-enters, 70 points higher than the exit.
- Week 8: close 3,960, below. Rule exits. Loss about 80 points.

Nothing dramatic happened in the market, but the rule gave up about 140 points plus costs. A common fix is to require price to close a set distance beyond the line (say 1% to 3%), or to stay beyond it for several days. These filters cut whipsaws and also make every real signal later.

## What the research says

The best-known study is Brock, Lakonishok and LeBaron (1992), "Simple Technical Trading Rules and the Stochastic Properties of Stock Returns" in the Journal of Finance. They tested several moving-average rules, including long ones, on a long history of the Dow Jones Industrial Average. In their sample, returns after buy signals differed from returns after sell signals more than chance would suggest.

Two important caveats came later. Sullivan, Timmermann and White (1999), also in the Journal of Finance, showed that testing many rules on one history makes some look good by chance, and that the best rules did less well in the years after the original sample. And costs matter: a rule that trades in and out of an index during flat periods pays spread and commission each time.

So the fair summary is: a long moving-average rule has some historical support on some data, with serious questions about how much of it was luck and whether it still holds. We have not tested 200-day rules ourselves yet. When we do, the results will be on our [methods page](/methods/).

## How the 200-day is more often used

Most traders do not trade the 200-day cross mechanically. More common uses:

- **Context filter.** Only look for long setups when price is above a rising 200-day, short setups when below a falling one.
- **Exit for long-term holdings.** A close below the 200-day as a point to review the position, not an automatic sale. See [moving-average exit](/moving-average-exit/).
- **Zone to watch.** In strong trends, deep pullbacks sometimes stall near the 200-day. Treat it as a zone, not a line.

## Slope matters more than the cross

Price crossing a flat 200-day is a weak event: the line is flat because the market has gone nowhere. Price crossing a 200-day that is clearly falling, after months below it, is a bigger change. A simple check before reading any cross: is the line itself rising, falling or flat?

## Verdict

As a summary of the long-term trend, the 200-day average does its job well. As a timing signal, it is late and prone to whipsaws in flat markets, and the evidence that it adds an edge after costs is mixed. It is most useful as a filter for other decisions.
