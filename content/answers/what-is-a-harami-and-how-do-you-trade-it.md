A harami is a two-candle pattern where a small body sits entirely inside the previous candle's long body, showing that the move has suddenly stalled. It is usually traded only after a third candle confirms the turn, with a stop beyond the first candle's extreme.

## What a harami is

"Harami" is Japanese for pregnant. The first candle is a long body, the mother. The second is a small body, the baby, contained within the first body.

- A [bullish harami](/bullish-harami/) comes after a fall: a long red candle, then a small candle (any colour, often green) whose body sits inside the red body.
- A [bearish harami](/bearish-harami/) comes after a rise: a long green candle, then a small body inside it.
- A [harami cross](/harami-cross/) is either version where the second candle is a doji. It is often called the stronger version, since the stall is more complete.

The harami is often confused with the [inside bar](/inside-bar/) from price-action trading. The difference is what must be inside: a harami needs the second *body* inside the first *body*; an inside bar needs the second candle's whole *range*, wicks included, inside the first candle's range. Every inside bar with a small body is close to a harami, but many haramis are not inside bars.

## What it means

A harami says something different from most reversal patterns. An [engulfing pattern](/answers/bullish-engulfing-vs-piercing-line/) says "the other side took over". A harami says only "the move stopped". The long first candle shows the trend in full force. The small second candle shows that force suddenly gone: a much narrower range, and no follow-through.

That is information, but it is not yet a reversal. A stall can be followed by a turn, by a sideways pause, or by the trend resuming. That is why most writers treat the harami as needing confirmation, and why the three inside up and three inside down patterns exist: they are a harami plus a third candle closing beyond the first candle's body.

## How to trade it

A common approach for a bullish harami:

1. **Check the context.** A real fall before it, ideally into a support level.
2. **Wait for confirmation.** The third candle closes above the harami's high, or above the first candle's open (which makes it a three inside up).
3. **Enter** at the next open after confirmation, or on a break of the confirming candle's high.
4. **Stop** below the low of the two-candle pattern. Because the first candle is long, this is often a wide stop; check it against the average daily range.
5. **Target** the next resistance, or a fixed multiple of risk such as 2R.

The bearish harami is the mirror.

What this adds to the harami guides:

- **The bullish harami looked better than random entry, but the sample is small.** 58 trades is well under the 100 our rules need for a headline, and the ranges include zero. It is a pattern we would want to see on more markets before saying more.
- **The bearish harami did worse with a stop just beyond the pattern.** 41% of those trades exited on the entry day. Our 10 markets rose strongly over the period, which works against short trades in general.
- **The harami cross and the three inside patterns, often called the stronger versions, were too rare** to measure on these markets.

## Worked example

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A fund falls from 210 to 192 over two weeks. Day 1: opens 198, closes 192, a long red body. Day 2: opens 193, closes 194.50, high 195, low 192.50. The body (193 to 194.50) sits inside Day 1's body (192 to 198). That is a bullish harami at a support level near 191.

Day 3 closes at 198.40, above Day 1's open of 198: a three inside up. A trader buys at the next open, 198.60, with a stop below the two-candle low (Day 1's low, say 191.20) at 191.00. Risk 7.60. The average daily range is 4.00, so the stop is well outside the noise, but the risk is large. With 300 at risk, that is about 39 units. A 2R target is 213.80, above the 210 start of the fall; a trader might settle for 210 instead, which is 1.5R.

## Verdict

A harami shows a stall, not a reversal, so trade it with confirmation and a stop beyond the pattern's extreme. In our tuning-period test the bullish harami was exploratory, not a tested result, on only 58 trades; the bearish harami showed no evidence of an edge; and the harami cross was too rare to measure. Treat the pattern as a reason to watch the next candle closely. See [Bullish engulfing vs piercing line](/answers/bullish-engulfing-vs-piercing-line/) for the patterns that show a takeover rather than a stall, and the [candlestick patterns guide](/guide/candlestick-patterns/) for the rest.
