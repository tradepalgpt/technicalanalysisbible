A trend makes steady progress in one direction, with swings stepping higher or lower; a range goes sideways, with swings overlapping and returning to the same highs and lows. The most reliable test is the swing sequence, backed by a measure of net progress versus total movement.

## Three tests you can write down

| Test | Trend | Range |
|---|---|---|
| Swing sequence | HH and HL (or LH and LL) in a row | Mixed labels; highs and lows near the same levels |
| Overlap | Pullbacks stay above the prior swing low | Each swing overlaps much of the last |
| Efficiency | Net move is a large share of the total path | Net move is small compared with the path |

The first test is [market structure](/market-structure/). The second asks whether price keeps revisiting the same zone. The third can be measured: divide the net change over N bars by the sum of the absolute bar-to-bar changes. This is Perry Kaufman's efficiency ratio. Values near 1 mean a straight line; values near 0 mean noise. The [choppiness index](/choppiness-index/) is a related indicator built on the same idea of path versus distance.

## Worked comparison

Two 20-bar stretches of a share, closing prices only:

| | Stretch A | Stretch B |
|---|---|---|
| Start close | 50.00 | 50.00 |
| End close | 56.00 | 50.80 |
| Net change | 6.00 | 0.80 |
| Sum of absolute daily changes | 10.00 | 12.00 |
| Efficiency ratio | 0.60 | 0.07 |
| Swing labels | HL, HH, HL, HH | HH, LL, HL, LH |
| Reading | Trend | Range |

Stretch B moved more in total (12.00) but went almost nowhere. Stretch A moved less in total but most of it was progress. A bar count alone could not separate them; the ratio and the swings do.

There is no standard threshold. Some traders call above about 0.3 trending and below about 0.1 ranging on daily data, but those cut-offs are choices, not tested facts, and they shift by market and timeframe.

## Transitions are the hard part

Markets rarely switch cleanly. A typical sequence is trend, then a lower high (in an uptrend), then a break of the last higher low, then a sideways period, and only then a new trend one way or the other. During the transition, both a [trend-following](/trend-following/) and a [range trading](/range-trading/) approach will struggle. Many traders treat "unclear" as its own state and simply wait.

## Why the label changes the trade

The same entry looks different depending on state.

- In a trend, a long near the last higher low with a stop under it, target at or beyond the last high. 1R is fill to that stop times size.
- In a range, a long near the range low with a stop just under it, target near the range high, not beyond.

If a long fills at 50.30 with a stop at 49.80 and 400 shares, 1R = (50.30 - 49.80) x 400 = 200. In a range with a top near 51.30 the target is about +2R on price; in a trend the trader might trail instead. The plan, not the entry, is what the trend-or-range call decides.

## Common mistakes

- Deciding the state from the last three bars.
- Using one timeframe's label for another. A daily range can contain a strong hourly trend.
- Calling a range "accumulation" or "distribution" before it resolves. That is a guess about the future, not a description.
- Changing the label after a losing trade so the trade looks like a misread rather than a normal loss.

## Verdict

Tell a trend from a range with swing labels, overlap and an efficiency measure, all on a fixed timeframe. Trends show progress; ranges show movement without progress. When the tests disagree, the market is in transition, and "no clear state" is a valid answer that keeps you out of the hardest conditions to trade.
