It depends on what you want to give up. Waiting for a retest usually gives a better entry and a tighter stop, so each trade is worth more in R. The cost is that you miss the breakouts that never come back, which can include the strongest ones. Neither choice is free.

## What a retest is

A [breakout retest](/breakout-retest/) happens when price breaks through a line, moves away, then comes back to touch or test that line from the other side. A broken resistance that now holds as support (or the reverse) is called [role reversal](/role-reversal/).

Retests happen for ordinary reasons. Early breakout buyers take quick profits. Traders who missed the break wait for a better price and buy the pullback. Sellers who were trapped above the line use the return to get out near break-even.

## The trade-off

| | Enter on the break | Wait for the retest |
|---|---|---|
| Entry price | Worse (further from the line) | Better (near the line) |
| Stop | Wider, usually at a swing inside the pattern | Tighter, just beyond the retest low |
| R multiple to target | Smaller | Larger |
| Trades taken | Every break that meets your rule | Only breaks that come back and hold |
| Misses | Nothing (but takes every false break) | Breaks that run away without retesting |
| Gets caught by | False breakouts | Retests that keep going back into the pattern |
| Hardest part | Sitting through the retest | Defining what "holding" means |

There is no published figure we trust on how often breakouts retest, and we have not tested it ourselves. It varies by market, timeframe and how you define a retest. What is clear from any chart is that some do and some do not.

## What "holding" means

A retest entry needs a rule, or it turns into hoping. Common choices:

1. **A rejection bar.** Price touches the line and the bar closes back in the breakout direction, ideally with a long wick into the line.
2. **A close that stays on the right side.** Price may poke through the line intraday but closes beyond it.
3. **A lower-timeframe break.** On a shorter chart, price makes a small pattern at the line and breaks out of it in the original direction.
4. **A time rule.** If price has not retested within a set number of bars, the setup is cancelled.

Without one of these, a "retest" can quietly become a full failure.

## Worked example in R

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A share has been capped at 100 for three months. Inside the range, the last swing low is 94. The measured target is 112.

**Day 1:** closes at 101.20.

**Trader A, the break.** Buys at the next open, 101.40. Stop below the last swing low, 93.80. Risk 7.60. Target reward 10.60. **1.4R.**

**Day 4:** price drifts back to 100.30 and makes a bar with a long lower wick, closing at 101.00.

**Trader B, the retest.** Buys at the next open, 101.10. Stop below the retest low, 99.70. Risk 1.40. Target reward 10.90. **7.8R.**

On this chart, Trader B's trade is far better. Now imagine two other versions:

- **No retest.** Price runs straight from 101 to 112 without looking back. Trader A makes 1.4R. Trader B has no trade.
- **Deep retest.** Price falls back to 97, holds, then rallies. Trader A sits through it and still makes 1.4R. Trader B's stop at 99.70 never came into play because there was no rejection bar at 100; B either had no trade or, without a rule, was stopped out.

Over many trades, which style does better depends on how often each version happens. That is something you can only learn from a record of your own market and rules.

## A split approach

Some traders do both: half the position on the break with a structural stop, half on a retest with a tight stop. If no retest comes, they still have a position. If one does, the average entry improves. It adds complexity and two sets of costs, but it removes the all-or-nothing choice.

## When retests matter most

- **Clean, widely watched levels.** A flat top that has held for months is a level many traders know. Retests of those are common.
- **Breaks in quiet conditions.** Breaks on low volume or in thin sessions are more likely to come back to the line.
- **Shorter timeframes.** Intraday breaks retest often, simply because there is more noise.

Breaks driven by big news, gapping well clear of the level on heavy volume, are the ones most likely never to look back.

## Verdict

Waiting for a retest is a trade-off, not an upgrade. You get a tighter stop and a larger R multiple, and you give up the breakouts that leave without you. Entering on the break catches everything, including the failures. Pick one approach, define what counts as a hold, and judge it on a record of trades in R, not on the best example.
