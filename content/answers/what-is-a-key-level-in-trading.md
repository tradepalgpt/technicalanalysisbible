A key level is a price area that many traders are watching, so a reaction there is more likely than at a random price. Common examples are major swing highs and lows, round numbers, the previous day's high and low, the weekly open and old levels that changed sides. "Key" means widely watched, not certain to hold.

## What makes a level key

The word gets used loosely. A useful working definition: a level is key if you would expect most traders in that market, on most timeframes, to have it on their chart. The [key levels](/key-levels/) guide lists the common types. This post is about shortlisting them.

## The common types

| Type | Why it is watched | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Major swing high or low | Big, visible turn on weekly or daily | Last year's high |
| All-time or multi-year high or low | No history beyond; headline price | A share at a record |
| Round number | Orders and headlines cluster | 1.1000 in EUR/USD; 100 in a share |
| [Previous day high and low](/previous-day-high-low/) | Objective; every day trader sees it | Yesterday's range |
| Weekly and monthly open | Divides up and down periods | This week's opening price |
| Old level that changed sides | Trapped traders on both sides | Broken resistance, now support |
| Gap edge | Last price before a jump | Pre-earnings close |
| Session or anchored VWAP | Institutional benchmark | Today's VWAP |

## How to shortlist

The danger with key levels is that every type above produces a level, and soon the chart is full. A simple shortlist rule many traders use:

1. Take the nearest level above and below price from the weekly or daily chart.
2. Add any [round number](/round-numbers/) within that space.
3. Add the previous day's high and low if you trade intraday.
4. Mark where two or more of these sit together. Those are your key levels.
5. Note the rest faintly, or delete them.

## Worked example

A share trades at 97.40.

- Weekly: last year's high at 102.80; a major low at 88.50.
- Daily: a swing high at 99.70 to 100.20; a swing low at 94.60 to 95.10.
- Round numbers: 100 and 95.
- Previous day: high 98.30, low 96.90.
- Weekly open: 96.10.

Where do they cluster?

| Area | What lines up | Shortlist? |
|---|---|---|
| 99.70 to 100.20 | Daily swing high + round 100 | Yes: key resistance |
| 98.30 | Previous day high | Yes, intraday only |
| 96.90 | Previous day low | Yes, intraday only |
| 96.10 | Weekly open | Faint |
| 94.60 to 95.10 | Daily swing low + round 95 | Yes: key support |
| 102.80 | Last year's high | Faint; far away |
| 88.50 | Major low | Faint; far away |

The trader's chart now has two key swing levels and, for the day, the previous day's range. That is a usable map. The area around 100 is the one most traders will be watching; it combines a visible swing high with the most obvious round number.

## Key does not mean certain

A key level is one where more traders are paying attention. That raises the chance of a reaction. It also raises the chance of a stop run, because everyone's stops are in the same place. Expect key levels to produce both clean bounces and sharp false breaks. Put stops beyond the zone with a buffer, and want a close, not a touch, before calling a break.

## Key level vs any level

| | Any level | Key level |
|---|---|---|
| Seen by | You | Most traders in the market |
| Timeframe | Any | Usually daily or higher, or a session extreme |
| Number on chart | Many | A few |
| Use | Fine-tuning entries | Planning the trade |

## Verdict

A key level is a widely watched price area: a major swing, a round number, yesterday's range, a period open or an old level that changed sides. The best ones are where several of these line up. Keep the list short, treat each as a zone, and expect both reactions and stop runs there. The [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) guide covers how to trade them.
