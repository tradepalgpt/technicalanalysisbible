There is no best timeframe for Fibonacci. The calculation is identical on a 5-minute chart and a weekly chart; the timeframe only decides which swing you measure. Use the timeframe that matches how long you plan to hold a trade, and expect levels from larger swings to be watched by more traders.

## The maths does not change

A [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) needs two prices: a swing low and a swing high. It has no setting for bar length. What changes between [timeframes](/time-frames/) is which swings are visible:

- On a daily chart, a swing might run for weeks.
- On an hourly chart, the same period contains many smaller swings.
- On a 5-minute chart, swings last minutes and are often just noise.

So the question is really "which swing size suits my trading?" rather than "which timeframe is best?".

## Worked example: same market, two swings

On a daily chart, a rise runs from 100 to 200. Inside the last part of that rise, an hourly chart shows a smaller swing from 180 to 200.

| Level | Daily swing 100 to 200 | Hourly swing 180 to 200 |
|---|---|---|
| 23.6% | 176.4 | 195.3 |
| 38.2% | 161.8 | 192.4 |
| 50% | 150.0 | 190.0 |
| 61.8% | 138.2 | 187.6 |
| 78.6% | 121.4 | 184.3 |

Both sets are "correct". A short-term trader watches the hourly levels; a position trader watches the daily ones. Price could fall through every hourly level and still be above the daily 23.6% at 176.4. Each set answers a different question about a different move.

Notice also the effect on risk. A long at the hourly 61.8% (187.6) with a stop below the hourly swing low (just under 180) risks about 8 points. A long at the daily 61.8% (138.2) with a stop below 100 risks about 38. The R arithmetic is the same, but position size must shrink as the stop widens if 1R is to stay the same in money.

## How timeframe changes reliability

| Factor | Higher timeframes | Lower timeframes |
|---|---|---|
| Swing choice | Fewer swings, less room to pick | Many swings, easy to pick one that fits |
| Audience | More traders watching the same levels | Fewer traders share any one level |
| Costs relative to 1R | Spread and commission small next to the stop | Costs can be a large part of 1R |
| Noise | One spike rarely defines a swing | Spikes often define swings |
| Speed of feedback | Slow; few examples per year | Fast; many examples |

None of these factors makes one timeframe work and another fail. They change how easy it is to fool yourself and how much costs eat into each trade.

## Combining timeframes

Many traders use [multiple time frame analysis](/multiple-time-frame-analysis/): draw Fibonacci on the higher timeframe to find the zone, then use the lower timeframe to time an entry inside it. If a daily 61.8% level sits near an hourly 50% level, some traders treat that overlap as a stronger zone. That idea is covered in our answer on [Fibonacci confluence](/answers/what-is-fibonacci-confluence/), along with its main weakness: with enough timeframes, overlaps are common.

## Common mistakes

- Switching timeframes until a level lines up with a turn you already saw.
- Using a 5-minute swing to set a target for a trade meant to last weeks.
- Ignoring costs on low timeframes, where the spread can be a sizeable part of 1R.
- Treating levels from a higher timeframe as stronger without testing that idea.

## Verdict

No timeframe is best. Match the swing to your holding period, fix your timeframe before you look for levels, and remember that levels from larger swings are more widely watched but give fewer examples to judge them by.
