A head and shoulders is read as bearish: it marks a possible top after a rise. The inverse head and shoulders, its mirror image after a fall, is read as bullish. Either way, the pattern only counts once price closes through the neckline; before that, it is just three peaks.

## Why the standard pattern is bearish

A [head and shoulders](/head-and-shoulders/) forms after an uptrend. Price makes a peak (the left shoulder), pulls back, makes a higher peak (the head), pulls back again, then rallies to a lower peak (the right shoulder). The line joining the two pullback lows is the neckline.

The story is a trend running out of buyers. Up to the head, every rally made a new high. The right shoulder is the first rally that failed to do so. When price then closes below the neckline, the last pullback low has gone too. A run of higher highs and higher lows has become a lower high and a lower low. That is the textbook definition of a trend change, and it is why the pattern is read as bearish.

## Why the inverse is bullish

The [inverse head and shoulders](/inverse-head-and-shoulders/) turns everything upside down. After a fall, price makes a trough, a lower trough, then a higher trough. The neckline joins the two peaks between them. A close above it completes the pattern. The story is sellers running out of steam: the last push lower failed to reach a new low, and the last bounce high has now been cleared.

## It is about the trend, not the shape

The shape alone is neutral. The name assumes what came before. Three peaks with the middle one highest, sitting in the middle of a sideways range, are not a meaningful top. There is nothing to reverse.

| Where the shape appears | Name | Usual reading |
|---|---|---|
| After a clear rise, neckline broken | Head and shoulders top | Bearish: possible trend change |
| After a clear rise, neckline not broken | Possible head and shoulders | Nothing yet |
| After a clear fall, neckline broken | Inverse head and shoulders | Bullish: possible trend change |
| Inside a sideways range | No special name | Close to meaningless |
| Short bounce inside a downtrend | Often a continuation, not a top | The downtrend is still in charge |

The second row is the one traders forget most. A large share of shapes that look like tops never break their necklines. Price holds the neckline, rallies again, and the "right shoulder" turns out to be the start of a new leg up. Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, stressed that the pattern is incomplete until the neckline goes.

## Things that change the reading

- **Neckline slope.** A neckline that slopes down (each pullback low lower) is often read as a sign that buyers were already weakening. One that slopes up delays the break and is sometimes read as less bearish.
- **The right shoulder.** A right shoulder well below the left one shows a weaker final rally. One that almost reaches the head starts to look like a double top or a continuing range.
- **Volume.** The textbook picture has lighter volume on the right shoulder and heavier volume on the neckline break. In stocks and futures that is worth checking. In spot forex, platform volume is tick volume, so it is a rougher guide.
- **A bigger level nearby.** A neckline that sits just above a major weekly support level may break and then stall. The pattern does not cancel the level.

## Worked example in R

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A share rises from 60 to 95 over several months. It makes a left shoulder at 92, pulls back to 84, makes a head at 98, pulls back to 84, and makes a right shoulder at 91. The neckline is flat at 84. Pattern height: 98 − 84 = 14. Measured target: 84 − 14 = 70.

Price closes at 83.40. A trader sells at the next open, 83.30, with a stop just above the right shoulder at 91.50.

- Risk (1R): 91.50 − 83.30 = 8.20.
- Reward to target: 83.30 − 70 = 13.30.
- Target in R: 13.30 / 8.20 = 1.6R.

Now the inverse case, also invented. A share falls from 60 to 32. It makes troughs at 36, 30 and 35, with a flat neckline at 42. Height: 12. Target: 54. Buying at 42.30 after the break with a stop under the right shoulder at 34.60 risks 7.70 for a reward of 11.70: about 1.5R.

Both trades sit around 1.5R with a structural stop. That is fairly typical for the pattern traded this way. A tighter stop just beyond the neckline makes the R look larger but sits right where neckline retests tend to happen. The answer on [what a neckline is](/answers/what-is-a-neckline-in-trading/) covers that trade-off.

## What we do not know yet

We have not tested the head and shoulders ourselves, so there is no figure of our own on this page. Thomas Bulkowski's *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* is the best-known attempt to measure how the pattern behaves in US stocks; it uses its own drawing rules, which matter a lot for a pattern this open to judgement. Lo, Mamaysky and Wang (2000) included the head and shoulders among the patterns they detected by computer, and found some information in the patterns they studied, without claiming a tradable edge.

## Verdict

A head and shoulders top is a bearish pattern, and the inverse is a bullish one, in the sense that both describe a trend that has stopped making progress and has now broken its last swing. Neither is a forecast on its own. The neckline break is the event that matters, the trend before it decides whether the name applies at all, and a nearby [support or resistance](/support-and-resistance/) level can stop the move well short of the measured target.
