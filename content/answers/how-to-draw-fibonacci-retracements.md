You draw a Fibonacci retracement from the start of a completed swing to its end: from the swing low to the swing high in a rise, and from the swing high to the swing low in a fall. The tool then marks the standard ratios of that move (23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8% and 78.6%) as possible pullback levels.

## What the tool actually does

A [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) is just arithmetic on two prices. It takes the size of a move and marks fixed fractions of it, measured back from the end of the move. The ratios come from the Fibonacci sequence and the [golden ratio](/golden-ratio/) (0.618 is 1 divided by 1.618), except 50%, which is a convention rather than a Fibonacci ratio.

Because it is only arithmetic, "correct" drawing means one thing: choosing the two anchor points in a consistent, rule-based way. Everything else follows.

## Step by step

1. **Pick a completed swing.** Look for a clear [swing low and swing high](/swing-highs-lows/) where price has turned. A move still in progress has no fixed end, so its levels keep moving.
2. **Decide the direction.** In a rise, click the swing low first and drag to the swing high. In a fall, click the swing high first and drag to the swing low. Most platforms expect this order.
3. **Decide wicks or closes before you draw.** Most textbooks use the extreme high and low (wicks). Whatever you choose, use the same rule every time.
4. **Check the labels.** Platforms differ in where they put 0% and 100%. On some, 0% sits at the end of the move; on others, at the start. Read the price beside each line rather than trusting the label.
5. **Keep the standard levels.** 23.6%, 38.2%, 50%, 61.8% and 78.6%. Adding more levels makes price "react" to something on almost every bar.
6. **Write down why you chose those anchors.** If you cannot state the rule, you will be tempted to move the tool later to fit what price did.

## Worked example: a rise and a fall

Take a rise from a swing low at 100 to a swing high at 200. The move is 100. Each retracement level is the high minus the ratio times the move:

Level = high - ratio x (high - low)

For a fall from 200 to 100, the levels are measured up from the low instead:

Level = low + ratio x (high - low)

| Ratio | Rise 100 to 200 (pullback level) | Fall 200 to 100 (bounce level) |
|---|---|---|
| 23.6% | 176.4 | 123.6 |
| 38.2% | 161.8 | 138.2 |
| 50% | 150.0 | 150.0 |
| 61.8% | 138.2 | 161.8 |
| 78.6% | 121.4 | 178.6 |

Notice that the 38.2% and 61.8% prices swap places between the two columns. That is the most common drawing error: dragging the tool the wrong way and reading a 38.2% pullback as a 61.8% one.

## Common drawing mistakes

- **Anchoring to a swing that is not finished.** The levels slide as the move extends.
- **Picking the swing after the bounce.** If you choose the anchors because price has already turned at a level, the drawing shows nothing.
- **Mixing wicks and closes.** Our answer on [wicks or closes](/answers/fibonacci-wicks-or-closes/) shows how much this moves the levels.
- **Switching to a different swing when price ignores your levels.** With enough swings, some level always lines up.
- **Reading labels, not prices.** Platforms disagree on whether the 0% line is at the start or the end of the move.

## How the levels are usually read

A retracement level is a place to watch, not a signal. Traders look for price to slow or turn near a level, often alongside other evidence such as a prior high or low or a round number. Which levels get the most attention is covered in [which Fibonacci levels matter most](/answers/which-fibonacci-levels-matter-most/).

## Verdict

Drawing a Fibonacci retracement correctly is about the anchors, not the tool. Use a finished swing, drag from start to end in the direction of the move, apply one rule for wicks or closes, and keep to the five standard levels. The levels are only as objective as the swing you chose, so choose it before you look at what happened next.
