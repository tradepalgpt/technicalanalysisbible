Most textbooks and charting defaults draw Fibonacci levels from the wick extremes, the true high and low of the swing. Drawing from closing prices is also a recognised rule. Neither is more correct; what matters is choosing one rule before you draw and using it every time.

## The case for wicks

The wick is the furthest price actually traded. If you are measuring how far a move went, the extreme is the honest measure. It is also what most [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) examples in books use, so levels drawn from wicks are the ones most other traders see.

The weakness: one spike, a news print or a thin overnight session can stretch a wick far beyond where most trading happened. The levels then inherit that one moment.

## The case for closes

A close is the price the market settled on for the period. Drawing from closes ignores brief spikes and focuses on where buyers and sellers agreed. Some traders argue this gives cleaner levels on markets that spike often.

The weakness: the close depends on the timeframe and, for markets that trade around the clock, on which time zone the platform uses to end the day. Two traders on different settings can get different closes for the "same" daily bar.

## Worked example: how far the levels move

A rise has a swing low bar that closed at 100 with a wick down to 98, and a swing high bar that closed at 200 with a wick up to 205. Using closes the move is 100; using wicks it is 107.

| Level | From closes (100 to 200) | From wicks (98 to 205) | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 23.6% | 176.4 | 179.7 | 3.3 |
| 38.2% | 161.8 | 164.1 | 2.3 |
| 50% | 150.0 | 151.5 | 1.5 |
| 61.8% | 138.2 | 138.9 | 0.7 |
| 78.6% | 121.4 | 120.9 | 0.5 |

On a move of 100, the two rules disagree by up to 3.3 points. That is enough to change whether price "held" a level or broke it, and enough to move a stop placed just beyond one. If a trader's 1R is only a few points, the choice of anchor can matter as much as the level itself.

Note that the gap is largest near the end of the move (23.6%) here, because the high wick is bigger than the low wick. With other wick sizes the pattern changes.

## Which to use where

| Situation | Common choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Liquid markets, few spikes | Wicks | Standard, matches most published examples |
| Markets with frequent spikes or thin sessions | Closes, or wicks with a stated filter | Avoids one-off prints setting the levels |
| Testing a rule on past data | Whichever you fix in advance | A rule chosen afterwards fits the past too well |
| Comparing with other traders' levels | Wicks | That is what most people draw |

## The real risk: switching

The biggest error is not choosing the "wrong" rule. It is choosing the anchor after seeing the result: using wicks on one chart because the level lines up, then closes on the next for the same reason. That makes Fibonacci look accurate on every chart you check and tells you nothing.

Write your rule down with your [swing high and low](/swing-highs-lows/) definition, such as "extreme high and low of a swing confirmed by two lower highs on each side", and apply it the same way every time.

## Verdict

Wicks are the textbook default and what most traders see; closes are a defensible alternative on spiky markets. The difference between them can be a few percent of the move, which is large relative to a tight stop. Pick one rule before you draw and do not switch to suit the chart.
