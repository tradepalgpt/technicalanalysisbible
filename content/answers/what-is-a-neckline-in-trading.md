A neckline is the trigger line of a reversal pattern. In a head and shoulders it joins the two lows either side of the head; in a double top it runs through the low between the peaks. A close through the neckline completes the pattern, and the neckline often acts as a level afterwards.

## Where the name comes from

In a [head and shoulders](/head-and-shoulders/), the three peaks look like a head between two shoulders. The line joining the two pullback lows sits where the neck would be. The term has spread to other patterns that have a similar trigger line, especially the [double top](/double-top/) and double bottom.

## Necklines by pattern

| Pattern | Where the neckline runs | Break that completes it |
|---|---|---|
| Head and shoulders | Through the two lows either side of the head | Close below |
| Inverse head and shoulders | Through the two highs either side of the lowest trough | Close above |
| Double top | Through the low between the two peaks (flat line) | Close below |
| Double bottom | Through the high between the two troughs (flat line) | Close above |
| Triple top | Through the lows between the peaks, often the lower one | Close below |
| Triple bottom | Through the highs between the troughs, often the higher one | Close above |

## How to draw one

1. **Find the swings.** The neckline joins [swing lows](/swing-highs-lows/) (or highs) that are part of the pattern. Use the same swing rule you use everywhere, so you are not choosing points to suit the shape.
2. **Choose wicks or closes.** A line through the lowest wick points and a line through the lowest closes can differ. Either is fine. Using one consistently is what matters.
3. **Let it slope if it slopes.** In a head and shoulders, the two lows are rarely equal, so the neckline is often tilted. Do not force it flat.
4. **Extend it to the right.** The neckline is useful beyond the pattern: after the break, it is the level to watch on a retest.

## What the slope tells you

| Neckline slope (head and shoulders top) | What it shows | Traditional reading |
|---|---|---|
| Sloping down | Each pullback low is lower | Buyers already weakening; break comes sooner |
| Flat | Pullbacks stop at the same price | Classic shape; a clear support level |
| Sloping up | Each pullback low is higher | Buyers still stepping in; break comes later |

For an inverse pattern, the readings flip. These are traditional interpretations, not tested results.

## Close, not touch

A neckline is "broken" when price closes beyond it on your chosen timeframe. Intraday pokes through necklines are common and often reverse. Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, treated the pattern as incomplete until the neckline was broken. A large share of shapes that look like a head and shoulders never break the neckline at all.

Some traders add a filter: a close beyond the line by a set amount, or two closes in a row. Each filter costs a worse entry.

## After the break: the retest

Once the neckline breaks, it often becomes a level. The old support becomes resistance: this is [role reversal](/role-reversal/). Price frequently comes back to test the neckline from the other side before moving away. Some traders use that retest as their entry, because it allows a tight stop just beyond the neckline.

## Worked example in R

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A share makes a head and shoulders top: left shoulder 62, head 68, right shoulder 61.50. The two lows are 57 (between left shoulder and head) and 56 (between head and right shoulder). The neckline slopes down from 57 to 56, and if extended, it sits at about 55.70 a week later.

Price closes at 55.20, below the neckline. The head is 68; the neckline directly beneath it is about 56.50, so the height is 11.50. The target is about 55.70 − 11.50 = 44.20.

**Entry on the break:** short at 55.10, stop above the right shoulder at 61.70. Risk 6.60. Reward 10.90. **1.7R.**

**Entry on the retest:** three days later, price rallies to 55.60, just under the extended neckline, and turns down. Short at 55.40 with a stop at 56.40, above the neckline plus a buffer. Risk 1.00. Reward 11.20. **11.2R** on paper.

The second trade looks far better, but two things are hidden. First, many breaks never retest, so a trader who only takes retests will miss those. Second, a stop just above the neckline will be hit if the retest pushes slightly further. The R multiple is large partly because the stop is easy to reach.

## Common neckline mistakes

- **Redrawing after a failed break.** If price breaks and recovers, it is tempting to redraw the neckline lower so the "real" break is still to come. That is fitting the line to your view.
- **Treating the neckline as exact.** It is a zone, a little wide, not a single price.
- **Ignoring the slope when projecting.** Measure the height to the neckline directly below the head, not to the lowest point.

## Verdict

A neckline is the line that turns a shape into a pattern. Draw it through the swing lows (or highs) that define the pattern, let it slope if it slopes, and treat a close through it as the event that matters. After the break, it often becomes a level for a retest. It is a zone, not a laser line, so place stops with a buffer. See the [chart patterns guide](/guide/chart-patterns/) for necklines in context.
