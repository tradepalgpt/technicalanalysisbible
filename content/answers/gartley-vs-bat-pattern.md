The difference is depth. In a Gartley, B retraces 61.8% of the XA leg and D completes at 78.6% of XA. In a bat, B is shallower, at 38.2% to 50% of XA, and D is deeper, at 88.6% of XA, which puts the bat's reversal zone much closer to X.

## Where each comes from

The [Gartley](/gartley-pattern/) is named after H.M. Gartley's 1935 book, with Fibonacci ratios added later by Larry Pesavento and Scott Carney. The [bat](/bat-pattern/) was introduced by Scott Carney himself, who has said he identified it in 2001, and it appears in his *Harmonic Trading* books. Both belong to the wider [harmonic patterns](/harmonic-patterns/) family.

## Ratio comparison (Carney's published rules)

| Rule | Gartley | Bat |
|---|---|---|
| B retracement of XA | 0.618 | 0.382 to 0.50 |
| C retracement of AB | 0.382 to 0.886 | 0.382 to 0.886 |
| CD projection of BC | 1.272 to 1.618 | 1.618 to 2.618 |
| D retracement of XA | 0.786 | 0.886 |
| Where D sits | About four fifths of the way back to X | Close to X |

The 0.886 level is the fourth root of 0.618; it is used mainly in harmonic work.

## Worked example: same XA, two patterns

Both patterns start from X = 100 and A = 200, an XA leg of 100. In each case C is placed at a 0.618 retracement of AB.

| Point | Gartley | Bat |
|---|---|---|
| B | 200 - 0.618 x 100 = 138.2 | 200 - 0.50 x 100 = 150.0 |
| C | 138.2 + 0.618 x 61.8 = 176.4 | 150.0 + 0.618 x 50 = 180.9 |
| D | 200 - 0.786 x 100 = 121.4 | 200 - 0.886 x 100 = 111.4 |
| BC | 38.2 | 30.9 |
| CD | 55.0 | 69.5 |
| CD / BC | 1.44 (inside 1.272 to 1.618) | 2.25 (inside 1.618 to 2.618) |

So on the same XA leg, the bat's D is 10 points deeper than the Gartley's.

## What the depth means for R

Both patterns commonly use a stop beyond X, here at 99.50. With a fill a little above D:

| | Gartley | Bat |
|---|---|---|
| Fill (ask) | 121.60 | 111.60 |
| First stop | 99.50 | 99.50 |
| 1R per unit | 22.10 | 12.10 |
| Target: 38.2% of AD | 151.4 | 145.2 |
| Distance to target | +1.35R | +2.78R |

The bat's deeper D gives a tighter stop and a larger R multiple to the same kind of target. The trade-off is that price must travel further before D is reached, so fewer swings complete a bat, and if price goes through X the pattern fails. A clean stop is exactly -1R in either case, with costs and any gap on top.

## How to tell them apart on a chart

1. Measure B as a fraction of XA. At about 0.618 it is a Gartley candidate; at 0.382 to 0.50 it is a bat candidate.
2. Project D for the candidate pattern (0.786 or 0.886 of XA).
3. Check that the CD/BC ratio falls inside that pattern's band.
4. If B lands between 0.50 and 0.618, neither pattern fits cleanly under Carney's rules. Software that "finds" one there is using looser bands.

## Verdict

The bat is a deeper relative of the Gartley: shallower B, deeper D at 88.6% of XA, and a longer CD leg. That gives a tighter stop beyond X and larger R multiples, but fewer completed patterns. Neither pattern's predictive value is established, so the useful difference is in the trade plan, not in which one "works better".
