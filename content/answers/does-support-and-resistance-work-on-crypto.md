The idea applies, because crypto traders watch the same prices and leave orders there like any other market. A few things differ: trading never stops, prices and wicks vary between exchanges, round numbers like 50,000 get heavy attention, and sharp stop runs through obvious levels are common. Wider zones and stops help.

## What carries over

Every reason levels can matter applies to crypto:

- Traders leave limit orders at obvious prices.
- Traders who bought at a high want out at break-even.
- Big [round numbers](/round-numbers/) attract attention and headlines.
- Busy prices from the past show where both sides accepted value.

The drawing routine is the same as in the [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) guide: higher timeframes first, clear swing points, zones not lines.

## What is different

| Feature | Shares and futures | Crypto |
|---|---|---|
| Trading hours | Sessions, closes, weekends off | 24/7 |
| Daily candle | Set by the exchange session | Set by the platform, often midnight UTC |
| Gaps | Common between sessions | Rare on spot; possible on futures (some exchanges close) |
| Price source | One main exchange or consolidated feed | Many exchanges, prices differ slightly |
| Wicks | Generally consistent | Can differ a lot between exchanges |
| Leverage and liquidations | Limited | High leverage common; liquidations cluster |
| Round numbers | Matter | Matter a lot (10,000s in Bitcoin) |

## Daily levels depend on your platform

Because crypto never closes, "the daily high" depends on when your platform starts the day. Most use midnight UTC, but not all. A trader using a different time zone can see different daily candles, different swing points and different previous-day levels. For daily and weekly levels, using UTC candles is the closest thing to a shared standard.

## Wicks and exchanges

A sharp move on one exchange can print a wick that others do not show, or show less. Derivatives exchanges add liquidation cascades: when price hits a level where many leveraged positions are forced to close, the selling or buying can push price well beyond the level for a moment.

Practical consequences:

- Draw levels from an exchange with deep trading, or an index price, rather than a thin one.
- Give more weight to bodies than to single wicks when defining the level.
- Put stops well beyond the zone, as wicks through obvious levels are common. That is the [liquidity sweep](/liquidity-sweep/) idea, and crypto shows it often.

## Worked example

Bitcoin has risen from 41,000 to 49,200, failed twice between 49,000 and 49,600, and is now at 47,800. The daily ATR is about 1,800.

- Resistance zone: 49,000 to 49,600 (two failures), just under the round number 50,000.
- Support: a daily swing low at 45,800 to 46,300.

A trader wants to short the next test of resistance. Where should the stop go?

- Above the zone: 49,600.
- Above the round number, where stops cluster: 50,000.
- Plus half an ATR buffer: about 50,900.

So a short entered at 49,200 has a stop at 50,900, risk 1,700. Target the support at 46,300, reward 2,900, about 1.7R. A stop at 49,700 would look like a better ratio but sits exactly where a sweep towards 50,000 would take it.

## Round numbers in crypto

Crypto has very round numbers, and very visible ones: 10,000, 20,000, 50,000, 100,000 in Bitcoin; 1,000 or 5,000 in Ether. They draw news and attention, which gives them weight. They also attract stops just beyond them and are frequent targets for sweeps.

## Volatility and zone width

Crypto often moves several percent a day. Zones that would be sensible in a share are too narrow. Size zones with ATR on the timeframe you use; a quarter to half an ATR is a reasonable starting width, as with any market.

## Verdict

Support and resistance works in crypto in the same limited sense it works elsewhere: levels mark places where reactions are more likely. The differences are practical. Use UTC daily candles, prefer deep exchanges or index prices, give bodies more weight than single wicks, respect big round numbers, and put stops well beyond the zone, because sweeps and liquidations are common.
