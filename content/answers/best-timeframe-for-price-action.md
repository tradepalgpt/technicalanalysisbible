No timeframe is best for everyone, but for [price action](/price-action/) higher timeframes, such as the 4-hour and daily charts, give price action patterns more room relative to trading costs and random noise. Lower timeframes give more signals, with costs taking a much bigger share of each trade.

## Why costs matter more on low timeframes

On a lower timeframe, swings are smaller, so stops are tighter. The spread and commission stay the same size. That means costs take a bigger bite of every trade.

The spread is already inside 1R, because a long fills at the ask and 1R runs from that fill to the stop. But the spread still has to be paid: a tight stop leaves less room once the spread is taken out of it. Commission sits on top of the result.

## Worked table: costs against stop size

A forex pair with a 1-pip spread and a round-trip commission of 7 per standard lot (where 1 pip is worth 10 per lot).

| Typical chart | Stop distance | Spread as share of stop | Commission as share of 1R |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5-minute | 10 pips | 10% | 7% |
| 1-hour | 30 pips | 3.3% | 2.3% |
| Daily | 100 pips | 1% | 0.7% |

On the 10-pip stop, 1R per lot is 10 x 10 = 100, and commission of 7 makes a clean stop-out -1.07R net. On the 100-pip stop, 1R per lot is 1,000, and the same 7 is under 0.01R. A plan that wins +1.5R gross on the 5-minute chart keeps about +1.43R after commission; on the daily it keeps almost all of it.

These figures are arithmetic, not results. The stop distances are illustrations; real swings vary.

## Noise

Short bars contain more random movement relative to their size. A pin bar on a 1-minute chart can be one large order or a spread widening for a few seconds. On a daily chart, the same shape reflects a whole session. That does not make daily patterns reliable; it makes them less dominated by tiny, random events.

## What each range of timeframes suits

| Timeframe | Signals | Main advantage | Main drawback |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 to 5 minutes | Many | Fast feedback, small stops | Costs and noise, screen time |
| 15 minutes to 1 hour | Moderate | Intraday trades, workable costs | Session effects, news spikes |
| 4 hours to daily | Few | Costs small in R, clearer swings | Overnight and weekend gaps |
| Weekly | Very few | Big picture | Very wide stops, small sizes |

The gap risk in the third row is real. A daily-chart stop can be jumped by an opening gap, so a stop-out can be worse than -1R. See [stop loss when the market gaps](/answers/stop-loss-when-market-gaps/).

## Using more than one

Many price action traders read structure on a higher chart and time entries on a lower one, which is [multiple time frame analysis](/multiple-time-frame-analysis/). That gets some of the higher chart's clarity with a lower chart's tighter stop. The tighter stop changes the size, not the money at risk: [position sizing](/position-sizing/) keeps 1R the same whichever chart sets the stop.

Our post on [the best timeframe for candlestick patterns](/answers/best-timeframe-for-candlestick-patterns/) makes similar points for single candles.

## How to choose

1. Start from the time you can watch the screen. A daily chart needs minutes a day; a 5-minute chart needs hours.
2. Work out costs as a share of 1R for your typical stop. If costs exceed a few percent of 1R, the plan needs a larger edge to survive.
3. Test on the timeframe you will trade. Results do not transfer cleanly between timeframes.

## Common mistakes

- Dropping to a lower timeframe to "tighten the stop" without counting the higher cost share.
- Holding a low-timeframe trade overnight and ignoring gap risk.
- Switching timeframes after a loss.

## Verdict

No single timeframe is best for price action. Higher timeframes keep costs and noise small in R but bring gap risk and fewer trades; lower ones give more trades at a much higher cost share. Choose from your available time and your cost per 1R, then test on that timeframe.
