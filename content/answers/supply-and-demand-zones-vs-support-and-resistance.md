Both mark price areas where a reaction is more likely. Classic support and resistance usually comes from repeated turning points and treats more tests as more notice. Supply and demand zones come from a short base that price left sharply, and prefer zones that have not been retested. They overlap more than either camp admits.

## Two ways of drawing the same idea

| | Classic support and resistance | Supply and demand zones |
|---|---|---|
| What defines it | Repeated turns near one price | A short base before a sharp move away |
| Preferred zone | Tested two or three times | Fresh: not yet revisited |
| View of retests | Confirm the level | Use up the orders |
| Zone drawing | Bodies to wicks of the turns | The base candles, edge to edge |
| Language | Buyers and sellers remember the level | Unfilled institutional orders remain |
| Main weakness | Clutter; too many lines | Hindsight; many candidate bases |

## Where they agree

Both say a reaction is more likely at some prices than others. Both draw zones, not lines (at least in good practice). Both put the stop beyond the zone. Both look for the next zone as the target.

And both have the same underlying story: orders. Classic levels say orders cluster where traders remember turns. [Supply and demand zones](/supply-and-demand-zones/) say orders were left unfilled where price moved away too fast.

## Where they differ

**Retests.** This is the real disagreement. Classic charting tends to treat a level tested three times as confirmed. Supply and demand traders treat each retest as using up the zone. Both are partly right. Our answer on [how many touches make a level](/answers/how-many-touches-make-a-support-level/) explains why the count alone says little; how each test behaves says more.

**Where the zone comes from.** A supply and demand zone can exist after a single event. A rally base followed by a big drop marks a supply zone even if price has never returned. Classic [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) needs repeated turns to call it a level, except for major highs and lows.

**How the move away looks.** Supply and demand traders care a lot about how fast price left. Several large-range bars in a row away from a small base is the ideal. Classic charting cares less about the departure and more about the turn.

## Worked example: one chart, two readings

A share trades sideways between 41.80 and 42.20 for three days, then rallies on wide bars to 46. It pulls back to 44, rallies to 47, and is now falling again at 43.50.

**Supply and demand reading.** The three-day base, 41.80 to 42.20, is a demand zone. It has not been revisited. A trader might place a limit buy at the top of the zone, 42.20, with a stop below 41.80 plus a buffer, at 41.50. Risk 0.70.

**Classic reading.** The obvious level is the pullback low at 44, which held once. Below that, the base at 41.80 to 42.20 is a level only because price turned up from it. A classic trader might watch 44 first (a pullback low in an uptrend) and only consider the lower zone if 44 fails.

**What a careful trader does.** Uses both. The 44 area and the 41.80 to 42.20 area are both candidates. The lower zone has the stronger departure. The higher one is closer. The decision depends on whether the trader expects a shallow or deep pullback, which a level alone cannot answer.

## And order blocks?

An [order block](/order-block/) is usually defined as the last opposite-coloured candle before a strong move. It is close to a demand or supply zone drawn from a single candle instead of a base. The same strengths and weaknesses apply. The extra precision of "one candle" can make zones narrow, which means tighter stops and more stop-outs from ordinary noise.

## The hindsight problem

Both approaches share a trap, but supply and demand zones fall into it more easily. On any chart, after the fact, every sharp move starts somewhere. That gives many candidate zones. The ones that held are easy to remember; the ones that failed are easy to forget. If you test either method, draw zones only with information available at the time, and count the failures. Our [methods page](/methods/) describes the approach.

## Verdict

Supply and demand zones and classic levels are two ways of drawing the same idea, with one real disagreement: whether retests strengthen or weaken a zone. The supply and demand emphasis on fast departures and fresh zones is useful. The classic emphasis on repeated, visible turns is also useful. Use whichever drawing rule you can apply consistently, and judge each zone by what price did there, not by its label.
