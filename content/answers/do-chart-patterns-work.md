Chart patterns work well as a way to organise a chart and plan a trade: they give you a level, a stop and a rough target. As forecasts, the evidence is thin and hard to judge, because patterns are drawn by eye, famous examples are chosen after the fact, and few tests use only information available at the time.

## Two different questions

"Do chart patterns work?" usually means one of two things.

1. **Do they help you plan a trade?** Yes. A pattern gives you a trigger line, a place where you are clearly wrong, and a measured target to compare with your risk. That structure is useful whether or not the pattern predicts anything.
2. **Do they predict where price goes next, better than chance?** That is the hard question, and the honest answer is that nobody has settled it in a way that applies to your market, your timeframe and your drawing rules.

Most arguments about chart patterns are two people answering different questions.

## What the research says

A few well-known sources are worth naming.

- **Edwards and Magee**, *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends* (1948), set out the patterns most people use. It is a careful description, built on examples, not a statistical test.
- **Thomas Bulkowski**, *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* (first edition 2000), counts how patterns behaved in a large set of US stocks using his own drawing rules. It is the best-known attempt at measurement. Its figures depend on his definitions and sample, and do not include a comparison with random entries of the kind we use.
- **Lo, Mamaysky and Wang**, "Foundations of Technical Analysis", *Journal of Finance* (2000), detected patterns by computer, so the drawing was not a matter of opinion. They reported that several patterns seemed to carry some information about later returns in US stocks. That is not the same as a pattern being tradable at a gain after costs.

We have not tested chart patterns ourselves yet. When we do, the results will follow our [testing rules](/methods/), including a comparison with random entries.

## Why they are hard to judge

| Problem | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Subjective drawing | Two traders mark different swings and lines | "The" pattern does not exist; only your version does |
| Hindsight | Examples are chosen after the move | The failures are not in the book |
| Late confirmation | A swing high is only known bars later | Tests that use those bars cheat by accident |
| Rarity | Big patterns appear a few times a year per market | Small samples, noisy results |
| Many settings | Swing size, tolerance, filter, stop, target | Try enough and one looks good by chance |
| Costs | Spread and slippage on every trade | Small patterns are hit hardest |

Each of these makes chart patterns look better than they are. None of them proves patterns are useless.

## What probably helps

Experienced traders tend to use patterns with extra conditions. These are reasonable ideas, not tested results.

- **Location.** A double bottom at a long-standing [support level](/support-and-resistance/) is a different thing from a double bottom in open space.
- **Trend.** A flag in a strong trend makes sense. A "flag" in a range is just noise.
- **Confirmation.** A close through the line, not a touch.
- **Risk in R.** A pattern that offers less than about 1R to its measured target with a structural stop may not be worth taking, however clean it looks.

## A worked example of planning, not predicting

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A share falls from 70 to 50 and makes an inverse [head and shoulders](/head-and-shoulders/): troughs at 52, 48 and 51.50, with a neckline at 56. Height: 8. Measured target: 64.

Without the pattern, a trader would have a vague view that the share "looks like it is bottoming". With the pattern, they have a plan:

- **Trigger:** a close above 56.
- **Entry:** next open, say 56.30.
- **Stop:** below the right shoulder, 51.30. Risk: 5.00.
- **Target:** 64. Reward: 7.70. **1.5R.**
- **Wrong if:** price closes back below the right shoulder.

Whether the share actually rises is unknown. But the trader knows exactly what they are risking, what they hope to gain and when they are wrong. Even if the pattern predicts nothing, that discipline has value.

## How to test it yourself

1. Pick one pattern and write a precise definition: swing size, tolerance, trigger, stop, target, time limit.
2. Go back through past charts in order, without looking ahead, and mark every case, including the ugly ones.
3. Record each trade in R, with costs.
4. Compare with random entries on the same market and period, using the same stop and target rules.
5. Keep the most recent year aside and check it once, at the end.

That is roughly what our [methods page](/methods/) describes. It is slow by hand, but it is the only way to find out whether the pattern works for you. The [breakout trading](/breakout-trading/) guide covers the trade rules most patterns share.

## Verdict

Chart patterns work as a planning tool: they turn a vague view into a trigger, a stop and a target in R. Whether they predict better than chance is unsettled. Published work is limited, drawing rules vary, and most examples are chosen after the fact. Use them as structure, add context such as trend and location, and test your own rules before trusting them. For the full picture, see the [chart patterns guide](/guide/chart-patterns/).
