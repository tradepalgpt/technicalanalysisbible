Mostly, yes. Nearly every smart money concepts (SMC) term maps onto an older idea from Dow theory, Wyckoff, supply and demand or classic price action. What SMC adds is a shared vocabulary, a few tighter definitions such as the fair value gap, and a story about institutions that the chart itself cannot confirm.

## Term by term

| SMC term | What it means | Older idea it relabels | Can it be defined objectively? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Break of structure | New swing extreme with the trend | Dow theory higher highs, lower lows | Yes, with a swing and close rule |
| Change of character | First break against the trend | Trend violation, failed higher low | Yes, with the same rules |
| Order block | Last opposite candle before a strong move | Demand or supply zone base | Partly; "strong move" needs a rule |
| Fair value gap | Three-bar non-overlap | Gaps; Wyckoff's wide-spread bars | Yes, exactly |
| Liquidity sweep | Brief trade past a high or low, then back | Stop run, false break, Wyckoff spring | Yes, with a distance and bar limit |
| Equal highs and lows | Swings at nearly the same price | Double top or bottom, resistance or support | Yes, with a tolerance |
| Premium and discount | Above or below a range's 50% | Range trading, 50% retracement | Yes, once the range is fixed |
| Inducement | A minor swing that "lures" early entries | Minor swing before the real move | Hard: intent is not measurable |
| Displacement | A large, fast bar | Wide-range bar, momentum bar | Yes, with a size threshold |
| Mitigation | Price returning to a zone | Retest | Yes |

[Smart money concepts](/smart-money-concepts/) as a body of teaching spread through online courses and video from the late 2010s. Its roots are visible: Richard Wyckoff's [method](/wyckoff-method/) from the early twentieth century already described composite operators, springs and tests; [supply and demand zones](/supply-and-demand-zones/) were popular well before; and [Dow theory](/dow-theory/) defined trends by swings.

## What is genuinely different

Being a relabel is not automatically bad. Three things are worth crediting.

1. **Tighter definitions in places.** The fair value gap is a precise three-bar rule. Older "imbalance" talk was vaguer. Precise rules are easier to test.
2. **A combined workflow.** SMC chains the parts: structure break, then a pullback into an order block or gap, in discount, after a sweep. Older books described each part separately.
3. **A focus on where stops sit.** Thinking about where other traders' stops are clustered is a useful habit, whatever the reason price reacts there.

## What is weaker

- **The institutional story.** Claims that banks engineer moves to take retail stops are presented as fact in many courses. Price data does not record who traded or why. Some markets do have large participants with real influence; that is not the same as showing a specific candle was their doing.
- **Loose terms.** "Inducement" and "smart money" itself are defined by intent, which cannot be tested.
- **Hindsight charts.** Many teaching examples are labelled after the move. On a finished chart, there is always an order block and a sweep that "explain" the result.
- **Evidence.** We know of no independent, peer-reviewed study showing that SMC rules beat random entries after costs, and we have not tested them ourselves yet. Absence of a study is not proof they fail; it means the claims are untested.

## How to use the vocabulary sensibly

Treat each term as a definition, not a revelation. Write the exact rule (which swing, wick or close, minimum size), apply it the same way every time, and measure results in R against a random-entry baseline. A trade from any SMC setup still has the same plan: fill, first stop, 1R = fill to stop times size, a clean stop-out at exactly -1R, costs on top, and stops that only tighten.

## Verdict

Smart money concepts is mostly older price action, Wyckoff and supply-demand reading under new names, plus a few sharper definitions and a strong narrative. The definitions can be useful; the narrative is untested. Judge any SMC rule the way you would judge any other: written down, tested against random entries, and measured in R after costs.
