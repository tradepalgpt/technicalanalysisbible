A double top is only as reliable as its confirmation. Two peaks at the same level happen constantly and often lead nowhere; the pattern only exists once price closes below the trough between them. Even then, there is no trustworthy published figure that applies to your market and your drawing rules.

## Two questions hiding in one

"How reliable is a double top?" can mean two things.

1. **How often do two equal peaks lead to a fall?** Not very often. Most pairs of similar highs are just part of a range or a pullback in a rising market. Price tests a level, pulls back, tests it again, then breaks through and keeps rising.
2. **How often does a confirmed double top lead to a fall?** That is the useful question. A [double top](/double-top/) is confirmed when price closes below the trough between the peaks. At that point, the market has failed twice at a level and has broken its last swing low.

Much of the confusion about reliability comes from mixing the two. Count every pair of equal highs and the pattern looks poor. Count only confirmed breaks and it looks better, but you have also given up part of the move to wait for the break.

## What the published research says

Thomas Bulkowski's *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* counts how double tops behaved across a large set of US stocks, using his own definitions. It is the best-known source, and we do not quote its numbers here because they depend on his drawing rules and sample. We have not yet tested the double top ourselves, so there is no figure of our own on this page either.

The honest summary is that nobody can tell you a single reliability number that applies to your chart. The result depends on the market, the timeframe, how equal the peaks must be, how far apart they are, how deep the trough is, and how the break is confirmed.

## A checklist that separates real tops from noise

| Check | Weaker double top | Stronger double top |
|---|---|---|
| Trend before | Short bounce or range | Clear, extended rise |
| Time between peaks | A few bars | Several weeks on a daily chart |
| Depth of the trough | Shallow | A real pullback, so the second rally is a test |
| Second peak | Well above the first (a new high) | Close to the first, or just below |
| Break | Intraday poke below the trough | Close below the trough |
| Volume (stocks, futures) | Normal on the break | Above average on the break |
| Bigger picture | Strong support just below | Room below to the next level |

None of these makes a double top certain. Each removes a common way for the pattern to fail.

## How double tops usually fail

- **The second peak breaks out.** Price pushes above the first peak and keeps going. This is not a failed double top; it was never one.
- **The break below the trough fails.** Price closes under the trough, then rallies back above it within a few bars. This is a [false breakout](/false-breakout/), and it is common because many stop orders sit just under an obvious swing low.
- **The fall stops short.** Price breaks, falls a little, and finds support well before the measured target. The pattern "worked" but the trade did not pay.

## Worked example in R

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

An index fund rises from 380 to 420. It pulls back to 400, rallies to 419, then falls and closes at 398.50, below the 400 trough.

- Pattern height: 420 − 400 = 20.
- Measured target: 400 − 20 = 380.
- Entry: short at the next open, 398.20.
- Stop above the second peak: 420.50. Risk: 22.30.
- Reward to target: 18.20.
- Target in R: 0.8R.

That is the double top's common problem. With a stop above the whole pattern, the measured target is often worth less than 1R. Traders deal with this in three ways:

1. **Wait for a retest.** If price rallies back to 400 and stalls, a short there with a stop at 404 risks 4 for a reward of 20: 5R. But many breaks never retest.
2. **Use a nearer stop.** A stop above the last small swing high after the break, say 406, risks 7.80 for 2.3R. It is more likely to be hit by noise.
3. **Pass.** If the trade is not worth it in R terms with a stop that makes structural sense, skipping it is a valid choice.

## The double bottom

Everything above applies in reverse to the [double bottom](/double-bottom/): two troughs at a similar level, confirmed by a close above the peak between them. The same checks apply, and the same R problem appears. Our answer on [double bottom vs triple bottom](/answers/double-bottom-vs-triple-bottom/) compares the two.

## Verdict

A double top is a clear, easy-to-define pattern, which is its real strength. Its reliability is not well established: there is no figure that applies to every market and drawing rule, and two equal peaks on their own mean little. Treat the close below the trough as the signal, check the trend before and the room below, and work out the trade in R before you take it. If the numbers do not work with a sensible stop, that is useful information too. For the wider context, see the [chart patterns guide](/guide/chart-patterns/).
