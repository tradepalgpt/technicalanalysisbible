There is no correct depth. In strong trends, pullbacks are often shallow, a third or less of the previous leg; in weaker trends they run deeper, half or more. A pullback that retraces the whole leg and breaks the last higher low is no longer a pullback in the usual reading.

## Common depth bands

Depth is measured as the share of the previous leg that price gives back. In an uptrend from a swing low A to a swing high B, a pullback to price P has a depth of (B - P) / (B - A).

| Depth | Common reading | Trade-off for an entry |
|---|---|---|
| Under about 25% | Very strong trend, buyers impatient | Fills often, but stop is far in R terms |
| About 33 to 50% | Normal pullback in a healthy trend | Middle ground |
| About 50 to 80% | Deep pullback, trend weakening or testing | Better R, fills less often |
| 100% or more | Leg fully retraced; the higher low is gone | Usually read as a trend change, not a pullback |

These bands are conventions from trading literature, not tested thresholds. Dow theory writers described secondary reactions of about a third to two-thirds of the prior move. Fibonacci retracement levels (38.2%, 50%, 61.8%) are a popular way of marking the same range; that topic has its own pillar, and we only use the numbers here as reference points.

## Worked example: depth versus R

A share rallies from 100 to 120. The trader plans to buy the pullback, with a stop at 99.50, under the start of the leg, and a target back at the 120 high.

| Depth | Entry (long fill) | Fill to stop | Fill to target | Target in R |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 38.2% | 112.36 | 12.86 | 7.64 | +0.59R |
| 50% | 110.00 | 10.50 | 10.00 | +0.95R |
| 61.8% | 107.64 | 8.14 | 12.36 | +1.52R |
| 78.6% | 104.28 | 4.78 | 15.72 | +3.29R |

With a fixed stop under the leg's start, deeper entries look much better in R. But each deeper level is reached less often, and the deeper price goes, the more the trend itself is in doubt. The table cannot show those two costs. In every row, a clean stop-out at 99.50 is exactly -1R, with costs on top.

Many traders therefore use a nearer stop for shallow entries, under the pullback's own low once it forms, rather than under the leg's start. That shrinks 1R, and the size grows to keep the money at risk the same.

## Shape matters as much as depth

- **Two-legged pullbacks.** A pullback that moves down, bounces, then makes one more push lower is common enough to have its own name. See [two-legged pullback](/two-legged-pullback/).
- **Slow, overlapping pullbacks** with small bars tend to be read as healthy pauses.
- **Fast, wide-range drops** against the trend are read as stronger selling, even if shallow.

Our [pullback trading](/pullback-trading/) guide covers entries in more detail, and [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/) explains how the leg's start and end are confirmed.

## How to pick a depth rule

1. Decide which trend strength you are trading. A trend-following plan will miss most trades if it waits for 61.8%.
2. Write the depth rule and the stop rule together, because they change 1R together.
3. Record every signal, including orders that never filled. Missed trades are part of the result.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring from a different swing each time so the pullback "fits" the level.
- Waiting for a deep pullback in a strong trend and then chasing after it leaves.
- Lowering the stop when price goes deeper than planned. Stops only tighten after entry.

## Verdict

No pullback depth is correct. Shallow pullbacks fit strong trends; deep ones give better R but fill less and hint at a weaker trend. Pick a depth band that matches the trend you trade, fix the stop rule with it, and judge the plan on all signals, including the ones that never filled.
