A higher high is a swing high that peaks above the previous swing high, and a higher low is a swing low that bottoms above the previous swing low. A run of both is the textbook definition of an uptrend; the mirror image, lower highs and lower lows, defines a downtrend.

## The four labels

Every confirmed swing can be given one of four labels by comparing it with the previous swing of the same kind.

| Label | Short | Compared with | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Higher high | HH | Previous swing high | Buyers pushed further |
| Higher low | HL | Previous swing low | Sellers could not push as far |
| Lower high | LH | Previous swing high | Buyers could not push as far |
| Lower low | LL | Previous swing low | Sellers pushed further |

The idea is old. [Dow theory](/dow-theory/), drawn from Charles Dow's writing around 1900, defined an uptrend as successive higher peaks and troughs. Modern [market structure](/market-structure/) reading uses the same definition.

## What counts as a swing?

A higher high only means something if the swing is real. A common mechanical rule: a swing high is a bar whose high is above the highs of the two bars on each side. That is the fractal rule; wider rules (five bars each side) give fewer, larger swings. The [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/) guide compares the options. Whichever rule you use, a swing is only confirmed after the bars on its right side have closed. Until then, the "latest higher high" might still be extended.

## Worked example

Daily swings on an index, using a two-bar fractal rule:

| Swing | Price | Label | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 4,200 | Start | |
| High | 4,350 | Start | |
| Low | 4,260 | HL | 4,260 is above 4,200 |
| High | 4,410 | HH | 4,410 is above 4,350 |
| Low | 4,330 | HL | 4,330 is above 4,260 |
| High | 4,395 | LH | Below 4,410: first warning |

The pullback from 4,410 to 4,330 was 80 points, about 53% of the 150-point leg from 4,260. That depth is ordinary for a pullback; it would not by itself say the trend had ended. The lower high at 4,395 is a warning. Many readers would treat the uptrend as intact until price closes below 4,330, the last higher low.

## Why the higher low matters most for stops

The last higher low is where the uptrend's definition fails. That makes it a natural place for a [structure stop](/structure-stop/).

Example: long 50 contracts of a CFD at 4,345 (filled at the ask), stop at 4,325, just under the 4,330 higher low. 1R = (4,345 - 4,325) x 50 = 1,000. If the stop is hit cleanly, the loss is exactly -1R, plus commission on top. If price makes a new higher low at 4,380 later, the stop can be raised under it, say to 4,375. The stop only ever moves up for a long.

## Common pitfalls

- **Counting unconfirmed swings.** The current bar is not a swing high yet.
- **Ignoring size.** A higher high of 1 tick and one of 100 points get the same label. Some traders add a minimum size, such as half an ATR, before a swing counts.
- **Mixing timeframes.** A lower low on the 15-minute chart can sit inside a higher low on the daily.
- **Reading ranges as trends.** In a sideways market labels alternate randomly. See [how do you tell a trend from a range?](/answers/trend-or-range-how-to-tell/).

## Verdict

Higher highs and higher lows are the simplest objective definition of an uptrend, and they have been in use for over a century. They describe what has happened, not what will. Their most practical use is defining where a trend's story breaks, the last higher low, and placing the stop there.
