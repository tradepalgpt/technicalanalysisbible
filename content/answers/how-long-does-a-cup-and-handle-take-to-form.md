On a daily chart, a cup and handle usually takes several weeks to several months, and the larger published examples run longer. The handle should be much shorter than the cup, often one to a few weeks. On intraday charts the same shape forms in hours or days, with more noise.

## Where the timing rules come from

The [cup and handle](/cup-and-handle/) was popularised by William O'Neil, founder of *Investor's Business Daily*, in *How to Make Money in Stocks*. His descriptions are for growth stocks on daily and weekly charts, and the timings below are in that spirit. They are guidelines from the people who named the pattern, not tested rules, and different authors give different ranges. We have not tested the pattern ourselves.

## The parts and their usual timing

| Part | What it is | Typical length (daily chart) | What to look for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prior rise | The trend before the cup | Months | A clear advance; the cup is a rest inside it |
| Left side of the cup | The fall from the prior high | Weeks | An orderly decline, not a crash |
| Bottom of the cup | The rounded base | Weeks | Rounded, not V-shaped; volume often quiet |
| Right side of the cup | The climb back towards the high | Weeks | Back near the prior high |
| Handle | A small pullback near the rim | About one to a few weeks | Shallow, drifting down, light volume |
| Breakout | Close above the handle high or rim | One bar | Volume above average in stocks |

Put together, the cup alone is often described as lasting from around seven weeks to several months on a daily chart, with long bases running much longer. The handle is a fraction of that. These figures come from the traditional descriptions and vary between writers, so treat them as a rough shape, not a rule.

## Why time matters

**A cup that forms too fast is usually a V.** If price falls and recovers in a couple of weeks, the base has no time to shake out sellers. The textbook argument is that a rounded base shows supply being absorbed slowly. A sharp V shows a quick bounce that may not hold. See the [V reversal](/v-reversal/) guide.

**A handle that lasts too long is a new pattern.** If the handle drags on for as long as the cup, it is no longer a short shakeout. It may be turning into a rectangle, a double top, or a failed rally.

**A cup that lasts too long loses its context.** A base that takes a year or more may still be valid on a weekly chart, but the prior rise is now a long way back. Many traders would treat it as a [rounding bottom](/rounding-bottom/) instead, which is a reversal pattern rather than a continuation.

## Depth matters too

Time and depth go together. The traditional descriptions favour a cup that retraces a moderate share of the prior rise, not one that gives back nearly all of it. A very deep cup looks more like a failed trend than a rest. The handle should form in the upper part of the cup, close to the rim, and should be shallow.

## Worked example in R

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

A share rises from 40 to 80 over a year. It then:

- falls from 80 to 62 over five weeks (left side);
- spends four weeks basing between 62 and 66 (bottom);
- climbs back to 79 over five weeks (right side);
- pulls back to 75 over eight days (handle);
- closes at 79.60, above the handle high of 79.

Total cup: about 14 weeks. Handle: under two weeks, and much shallower than the cup. That fits the traditional shape.

- Cup depth: 80 − 62 = 18. Measured target: 79 + 18 = 97.
- Entry: next open, 79.80.
- Stop below the handle low: 74.80. Risk: 5.00.
- Reward to target: 17.20. **3.4R.**
- A nearer target at the prior high plus half the depth, 89, would be **1.8R.**

Now a version where the cup forms in two weeks, 80 down to 62 and back to 79. The same handle and break give the same numbers in R, but the shape is a V, and the traditional reading would be weaker. The arithmetic does not tell you that; the timing does.

## The same shape on other timeframes

| Chart | Typical cup length | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | Months to a year or more | Fewer patterns, often on long-term charts of stocks |
| Daily | Weeks to months | Where most of the published descriptions sit |
| Hourly | Days | More frequent, more false breaks |
| 5-minute | Hours | Mostly noise; spread can be a large share of R |

The proportions are what matter across timeframes: a rounded cup, a short shallow handle, and a prior rise. Our answer on the [best timeframe for chart patterns](/answers/best-timeframe-for-chart-patterns/) covers the trade-offs.

## The inverse version

The [inverse cup and handle](/inverse-cup-and-handle/) is the bearish mirror: a rounded top after a fall, a small bounce (the handle), and a break below. The same timing ideas apply in reverse.

## Verdict

On a daily chart, think in weeks to months for the cup and one to a few weeks for the handle. The exact numbers vary by author and none of them are tested rules. What matters more is the shape that the timing creates: a rounded, unhurried base, a short shallow handle close to the rim, and a prior rise for the cup to rest inside. If the cup is a fast V or the handle drags on, the pattern is weaker or has become something else. See the [chart patterns guide](/guide/chart-patterns/) for the continuation family in full.
