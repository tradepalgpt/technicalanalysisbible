There is some evidence that it does, in a modest form. Research on currency markets found that trends stopped at published levels more often than at random prices, and that client orders cluster at round numbers. That supports levels as places where reactions are more likely. It does not support the idea that a drawn line will hold.

## What the research says

Two papers by Carol Osler are the most cited.

- **"Support for Resistance: Technical Analysis and Intraday Exchange Rates"** (Federal Reserve Bank of New York *Economic Policy Review*, 2000). She took support and resistance levels that firms published for currency traders and compared them with randomly chosen levels. Intraday trends were interrupted at the published levels more often than at the random ones. The effect varied between the firms whose levels she studied.
- **"Currency Orders and Exchange Rate Dynamics"** (*Journal of Finance*, 2003). Using a bank's actual client orders, she found that stop-loss and take-profit orders clustered at round numbers. That gives a mechanical reason why round numbers can act as levels.

We are not quoting figures from either. The honest reading: in one large market, levels that many traders saw did matter more than chance, and there is a plausible order-based reason why. That is all.

## What the research does not say

- That any line a trader draws will hold.
- That buying support and selling resistance makes money after costs.
- That the effect is the same in shares, crypto or futures, or the same today as in the 1990s data.

## Why levels can work

| Reason | How it works |
|---|---|
| Resting orders | Limit orders cluster near obvious prices and absorb movement |
| Memory and regret | Traders who bought at a level sell at break-even when price returns |
| Attention | Many traders watching one price makes reactions there more likely |
| Value | Prices with heavy past trading were accepted by both sides |

## Why levels fail

| Reason | What happens |
|---|---|
| Orders get filled | Repeated tests use up the buyers or sellers at the level |
| Stop runs | Obvious levels attract stops just beyond; price pokes through to trigger them |
| News | Fundamentals change, and old reference prices stop mattering |
| Too many levels | If you draw enough, price is always at one, so "it worked" means nothing |

## A thought experiment on the last point

Imagine a trader draws twelve lines across a chart spanning 40 to 60, roughly every point and a half. Price is now always within about 0.75 of a level. Every turn in the market will look like a level holding. Every break will look like a level breaking. The trader feels the levels work. But a random set of twelve lines would look just as good.

That is why a fair test compares reactions at levels with reactions at random prices, as Osler did. Without that comparison, any level method looks like it works.

## Worked example: a fair personal test

A trader wants to know whether their own levels help. A simple honest test:

1. Pick 50 charts. For each, draw levels using only data up to a fixed date. Write them down.
2. For each chart, also pick the same number of random prices in the same range.
3. Look at the next month. Count how often price reversed (say, by at least one ATR) within a small band of a level, and how often within the same band of a random price.
4. Compare the two counts.

If levels show reactions clearly more often than random prices, the drawing method has something. If not, it may be drawing lines where price was going to wobble anyway. Our [methods page](/methods/) sets out the stricter rules we follow before publishing any figure. We have not yet run a level test of our own.

## What this means in practice

- Treat levels as places where a reaction is more likely, not certain.
- Keep the stop beyond the zone; the level can always fail.
- Prefer levels that many traders can see: weekly and daily swings, [round numbers](/round-numbers/), the previous day's range.
- Expect [false breakouts](/false-breakout/) at obvious levels.
- Keep the chart clean, so a "working" level means something.

## Verdict

Support and resistance has more evidence behind it than most of technical analysis: published levels in currency markets did interrupt trends more often than chance, and orders do cluster at round numbers. But the evidence is for a modest effect at levels many traders see. Use levels to decide where to look and where your risk ends, and test your own drawing against random prices before trusting it.
