Most traders need one to three moving averages: one for the trend you trade, one slower line for context, and perhaps one faster line for timing. Beyond that, extra lines mostly repeat the same information, because they are all averages of the same closes.

## Why more lines add little

Every moving average on a chart is built from the same prices. A 20, 30 and 40 EMA will rise together, fall together and cross price at similar times. Adding the 30 between the 20 and 40 does not tell you anything the other two did not. It just fills the gap.

The same goes for indicators built from averages. [MACD](/macd/) is the 12 EMA minus the 26 EMA. If your chart already has a 12 and 26 EMA, MACD shows you the gap between two lines you can already see.

The cost of extra lines is real: clutter, more crossings that look like signals, and a temptation to pick whichever line "explains" each move after the fact.

## What each extra line adds

| Lines | What you can read | What is added |
|---|---|---|
| One | Trend direction (slope) and price above or below | The basic picture |
| Two (fast + slow) | Above plus: crossovers, alignment, a pullback zone between them | Timing and a zone |
| Three (fast + medium + slow) | Above plus: full stack order, deeper pullback levels | Context from a longer trend |
| Four to six | Little new; finer steps between existing lines | Mostly repetition |
| Ribbon (6 to 15) | Trend strength at a glance: fanned or tangled | A visual summary, no new data |

A [moving-average ribbon](/moving-average-ribbon/) is the exception that proves the rule. It deliberately uses many lines, but it is read as a single shape, not as separate signals.

## A simple way to choose

Start from the job each line does.

1. **The trend line.** The average that matches your holding time. For swing trades on a daily chart, a 20 EMA or 50 SMA is common.
2. **The context line.** A slower average that tells you which side of the bigger trend you are on. On a daily chart, the 200 SMA is the usual one.
3. **The timing line (optional).** A faster average for entries inside the trend, such as a 9 EMA.

If a line does not have a job you can name, remove it.

### A worked example

A swing trader holds trades for one to four weeks.

- **Trend line:** 20 EMA, daily. Pullbacks to it are watched.
- **Context line:** 200 SMA, daily. Only long trades when price is above it and it is rising.
- **Timing line:** none. Entry comes from the price bar at the 20 EMA.

The chart has two averages. The trader can answer the three questions that matter: what is the big trend (200), what is my trend (20), where is my entry zone (20). A 50 could be added as a deeper pullback zone, and that would be a reasonable third line. A 100 between the 50 and 200 would add nothing that changes a decision.

## Using other timeframes instead of more lines

Instead of adding a slow line to a fast chart, many traders look at a higher timeframe. That is [multiple time frame analysis](/multiple-time-frame-analysis/). A 20 EMA on a weekly chart shows roughly the same trend as a 100 EMA on a daily chart, and the weekly chart is cleaner.

| Instead of this on a daily chart | Consider this |
|---|---|
| 100 EMA | 20 EMA on the weekly chart |
| 200 SMA | 40 SMA on the weekly chart |
| 9 EMA on an hourly chart for timing | 9 EMA on the daily chart for a slower view |

The rough conversion is: daily period ÷ 5 ≈ weekly period, for markets that trade five days a week. The lines will not match exactly, but they describe a similar span.

## Signs a chart has too many averages

- You cannot say which line matters before the bar closes.
- You find yourself explaining each turn by pointing at whichever line was nearest.
- Signals from different lines disagree most of the time.
- You added lines after losing trades to "see what you missed".

Each of these is a sign that the lines are doing the work of hindsight, not analysis.

## Verdict

One to three moving averages, each with a named job, covers almost every use. More lines repeat the same information and make it easier to fool yourself after the fact. If you want a longer-term view, a higher timeframe usually beats another slow line. More in [the moving averages guide](/guide/moving-averages/).
