Use both. The wicks show the extreme prices where orders were actually filled; the bodies show where buyers and sellers settled. Drawing the zone from the bodies to the wicks captures both. If you must pick one line, bodies are less noisy and wicks are better for stop placement.

## What each one shows

| | Wicks (shadows) | Bodies (open and close) |
|---|---|---|
| Shows | The most extreme price traded | Where the period settled |
| Strength | Real fills happened there | Ignores brief spikes |
| Weakness | One news spike can distort it | Hides how far price actually went |
| Useful for | Stop placement, liquidity | Spotting where the market accepted price |
| Changes with | Data feed, especially in forex | Daily close time |

## Why the argument exists

Traders who draw on wicks say: price traded there, so orders were filled there, so that is where the level is. Traders who draw on bodies say: the close is the market's decision for the period, and wicks are often just noise or stop runs.

Both are partly right. That is why a zone from bodies to wicks is the common compromise. The [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) guide calls this the close-to-wick method.

## Worked example

Three swing highs on a daily chart of a currency pair:

| Swing | High (wick) | Close | Open |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0985 | 1.0952 | 1.0940 |
| 2 | 1.1012 | 1.0961 | 1.0975 |
| 3 | 1.0978 | 1.0958 | 1.0949 |

**Wicks only.** A line at 1.1012 (highest wick) or 1.0992 (average). The second swing's wick was a news spike, which pulls the level up.

**Bodies only.** The highest body tops are 1.0952, 1.0975 and 1.0958. A line around 1.0960 to 1.0975.

**Zone, bodies to wicks.** 1.0952 to 1.0985, ignoring the single spike to 1.1012, or 1.0952 to 1.1012 if you include it. Most traders would use 1.0952 to 1.0990 and note the spike as an outlier.

The trader shorting this zone would put the stop above 1.1012, the spike, plus a buffer. The wick matters for the stop even if it does not define the level.

## When wicks deserve more weight

- **Repeated wicks at the same price.** Several bars poking to the same high and closing lower show sellers acting there again and again. That is strong evidence of a level.
- **Markets with real exchange data.** On a centralised exchange, the wick is a real trade. In spot forex, wicks can differ between brokers.
- **Stop placement.** Your stop should be beyond the wicks, not the bodies.

## When bodies deserve more weight

- **Single spikes.** One long wick from a news release is often not a level.
- **Thin trading.** Wicks in quiet hours may reflect a few small trades.
- **Different data feeds.** Bodies (especially closes) tend to agree more between platforms than extreme wicks do.

## Lines through bodies

A level that cuts through many bodies is usually not a level. If price spent weeks opening and closing on both sides of a line, it was not acting as support or resistance in that period. Good levels sit at the edges of the bodies, with wicks poking into them.

## Gaps

On charts with gaps, the gap edge is often the level, not a wick or a close. A gap's edge is the last price before the jump. See [price gaps](/price-gaps/).

## What about line charts?

Some traders draw levels on a closing-price line chart to remove wicks completely. It gives clean levels and hides spikes. It also hides the information in the wicks. A reasonable routine is to find levels on the line chart, then switch to candles to set the zone edges and stops.

## Verdict

Draw the zone from bodies to wicks. Bodies show where the market settled; wicks show where orders were filled. Ignore single spike wicks when defining the level, but put your stop beyond them. Levels that cut through many bodies are not levels. For finding the turns in the first place, see [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/).
