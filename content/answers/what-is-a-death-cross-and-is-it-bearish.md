A death cross is when the 50-day moving average crosses below the 200-day. It describes a decline that has already lasted for months, so it is bearish about the past. As a forecast it is weak: sometimes more selling followed, and sometimes the cross came close to the low.

## How it forms

The [death cross](/death-cross/) is the mirror image of the [golden cross](/golden-cross/). Both use the 50-day and 200-day simple moving averages on a daily chart. In a market that has been rising, the 50 sits above the 200. When price falls for long enough, the 50 drops faster than the 200 and eventually crosses below it.

Because both averages lag, the cross usually comes weeks or months after the peak. The 50-day average describes roughly the last ten weeks, and the 200-day roughly the last ten months. For the shorter one to fall below the longer, the recent ten weeks must have been lower, on average, than the past ten months. That takes a sustained decline.

## Why the name sounds worse than the signal

"Death cross" is a newsroom name, and it gets attention because it sounds alarming. It is the same signal as any slow [trend-following](/trend-following/) crossover, with the same lag. The name does not add information.

## What can happen after a death cross

There are three broad outcomes. None is a rule.

| Outcome | What it looks like | Why it happens |
|---|---|---|
| Continued decline | Price keeps falling, the averages fan apart | The cross confirmed a trend that was still running |
| Cross near the low | Price turns up within weeks of the cross; a golden cross may follow months later | The cross came late, after most of the fall |
| Sideways churn | Price ranges near both averages; the lines flatten and may cross back | The trend ended, but no new one started |

In a sharp sell-off followed by a fast recovery, the death cross can arrive near the bottom, because it takes so long to form. In a slow, grinding bear market, it can arrive early enough to stay relevant for months. You cannot tell which one you are in from the cross itself.

## A worked example of lateness

A stock falls from 150 to 105 over four months. Most of the drop happens in the first two months.

- Peak: price 150, 50-day 145, 200-day 132.
- Two months later: price 115, 50-day 131, 200-day 133. The 50 is falling fast.
- Ten weeks after the peak: price 110, 50-day 128.8, 200-day 129.0. The cross happens.
- Four months after the peak: price 105, the low.

The numbers are made up, but the shape is typical. The death cross came with price already down about 27% from the peak, and only a few percent above the low. A trader who sold on the cross got out near the bottom of this decline. In a longer bear market the same trader might have avoided a much bigger fall. That uncertainty is the honest answer to "is it bearish?".

## How traders use it

- **As a regime flag.** Some traders stop taking long trades in a market after a death cross, or reduce size, until the 50 is back above the 200.
- **As context for shorts.** A failed rally up to a falling 50-day average after a death cross is read by some as a short setup. The cross is the background, not the trigger.
- **As a review point for long-term holdings.** Not an automatic sale, but a reason to check whether the reason for holding still stands.

## The whipsaw problem

When a market goes sideways for months, the 50 and 200 flatten and drift close together. They can then cross down, up, and down again. Each cross gets headlines; each reverses the last. A simple filter is to look at the slope of the 200-day: a cross while the 200 is still rising is a weaker event than one while it is clearly falling.

## Is it bearish?

It is bearish about the past: it confirms that the medium-term average price has fallen below the long-term one. It is not reliably bearish about the future. The evidence on long moving-average rules is mixed (see [does the 200-day moving average work](/answers/does-the-200-day-moving-average-work/)), and a single famous cross is not a sample.

## Verdict

A death cross confirms a decline that has lasted months. It does not tell you whether the decline will continue. In sharp sell-offs it often comes near the low; in long bear markets it can come early enough to matter. It is best used as a filter or a review point, not a sell signal on its own.
