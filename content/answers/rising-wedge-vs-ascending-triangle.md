The difference is the top line. An ascending triangle has a flat top and rising lows, and is read as bullish. A rising wedge has rising highs and rising lows that converge, and is read as bearish. Both have rising lows, so the top line decides which one you are looking at.

## Two patterns with the same bottom

Both patterns have a rising lower [trendline](/trendline/): each pullback stops at a higher price than the last. That is why they get confused. The story changes completely with the upper line.

**[Ascending triangle](/ascending-triangle/).** The highs stop at the same level again and again. Sellers are defending one price. Buyers keep stepping in at higher prices. Pressure builds against a fixed ceiling. The textbook reading is that buyers eventually break through, so the pattern is bullish. The trigger is a close above the flat top.

**[Rising wedge](/rising-wedge/).** The highs keep rising, but each rise is smaller than the rise in the lows. The upper line is less steep than the lower one, and the two converge. Buyers are still making new highs, but each push gains less ground. The textbook reading is that the rally is tiring, so the pattern is bearish. The trigger is a close below the lower line.

## Side by side

| | Ascending triangle | Rising wedge |
|---|---|---|
| Upper line | Flat | Rising (less steeply than the lower) |
| Lower line | Rising | Rising |
| Highs | Equal | Higher, by smaller amounts |
| Story | Buyers pressing on a fixed ceiling | Rally losing momentum |
| Textbook bias | Bullish | Bearish |
| Trigger | Close above the flat top | Close below the lower line |
| Measured target | Widest part, projected up from the break | Back towards where the wedge began |
| Usual stop | Below the last swing low inside the pattern | Above the last swing high inside the pattern |
| Where it often appears | Mid-uptrend, as a pause | End of a rally, or a bounce in a downtrend |
| Common failure | Breaks down through the rising line | Breaks up through the upper line and keeps going |

## How to tell them apart on a real chart

On a clean chart it is obvious. On a real one, highs are rarely perfectly flat. Some practical checks:

1. **Measure the highs.** If the last two or three highs are within a small fraction of the average daily range of each other, treat the top as flat. If each is clearly higher than the last, it is rising.
2. **Compare slopes.** In a wedge, both lines rise and the lower one is steeper. If the upper line is rising faster than the lower one, the pattern is widening, not narrowing, and is neither of these.
3. **Count touches.** Each line needs at least two touches, and ideally three. With fewer, you are drawing a shape through a handful of points.
4. **Look at what came before.** A rising wedge after a long rally is the classic reversal setting. A rising wedge after a sharp fall, as a slow bounce, is the classic bearish continuation. An ascending triangle is most often described as a pause in an uptrend.

If after all that you still cannot decide, the honest answer is that the chart does not show either pattern clearly enough to act on. Wait for the break and let price decide.

## Momentum is the real difference

The shapes differ, but what they say about momentum differs more. In an ascending triangle, buyers are not making progress at the top, but they are getting more eager at the bottom. In a rising wedge, buyers are making progress at the top, but less each time. Many traders check this with a momentum indicator: a rising wedge often comes with [divergence](/divergence/), where price makes higher highs and an oscillator such as RSI makes lower highs.

## Worked example in R

This is an illustration with round numbers, not a real trade.

**Ascending triangle.** A share tops out at 50 three times over six weeks. The lows rise: 44, 46, 47.50. It closes at 50.60.

- Height at the widest point: 50 − 44 = 6. Target: 50 + 6 = 56.
- Entry at next open: 50.70. Stop below the last low: 47.40. Risk: 3.30.
- Reward: 5.30. **1.6R.**

**Rising wedge.** A share rises over six weeks with highs at 46, 49 and 50.50, and lows at 42, 46 and 48.50. The lower line breaks with a close at 48.20.

- Target: back to where the wedge began, 42.
- Entry at next open: 48.10. Stop above the last high: 50.70. Risk: 2.60.
- Reward: 6.10. **2.3R.**

In both cases the stop is structural: if price goes back beyond the last swing inside the pattern, the story is wrong.

## What we do not know yet

We have not tested either pattern, so there are no figures of our own here. Thomas Bulkowski's *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns* covers both in US stocks, using his own definitions. The textbook biases above are the traditional readings, not tested results.

## Verdict

Look at the top line first. Flat top with rising lows: ascending triangle, traditionally bullish, triggered by a close above the top. Rising top with steeper rising lows: rising wedge, traditionally bearish, triggered by a close below the bottom line. Both can break the other way, so wait for the close and keep the stop beyond the last swing. For triangles and wedges in full, see the [chart patterns guide](/guide/chart-patterns/).
