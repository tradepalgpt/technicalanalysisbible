The Square of Nine is a number spiral that traders linked to the work of W. D. Gann and use to find possible support and resistance prices. Numbers are laid out in a spiral around a centre, and prices on the same angle lines are treated as related. It belongs to trader education, and it has no academic evidence base.

## What is the Square of Nine?

William Delbert Gann was an American trader who wrote and sold trading courses in the first half of the 20th century. His work uses geometry, angles and number relationships, and many claims about how he traded were made after his death. Gann's own writing is often obscure, and later authors have built several different methods on it. This makes it hard to say which version is "the" Square of Nine.

The common modern version starts with 1 in the centre and writes the numbers in a spiral outward: 2, 3, 4 and so on, in a square grid. The middle row and column, and the diagonals, then hold numbers that line up on straight lines through the centre. Traders say these lines represent angles, such as 45, 90 and 180 degrees, and that prices on those lines are related.

For practical use, the calculation can be done without the grid, using square roots. One full turn around the spiral is a 360 degree move, and it adds 2 to the square root of the number. So a half turn (180 degrees) adds 1, a quarter turn (90 degrees) adds 0.5, and an eighth of a turn (45 degrees) adds 0.25.

Treat it neutrally. It is a set of mathematical relationships that some traders watch, and no published statistical test has found that its levels predict price better than other reference levels. If many traders watch the same levels, they may matter for that reason alone.

## How it is identified

The square-root method:

1. Choose a starting price, often a major high or low.
2. Take its square root.
3. Add or subtract the step for the angle you want: 0.25 (45 degrees), 0.5 (90 degrees), 1.0 (180 degrees) or 2.0 (360 degrees).
4. Square the result to get the level.

**Worked example.** Starting price 100. Square root = 10.
- Add 0.5 (90 degrees): 10.5 squared = **110.25**
- Add 1 (180 degrees): 11 squared = **121**
- Subtract 1 (180 degrees): 9 squared = **81**
- Subtract 0.5 (90 degrees): 9.5 squared = **90.25**

These become a set of horizontal lines above and below the starting price.

Different sources use different markers. Some use only the 90 and 180 degree steps, some add 45 degree steps, and some treat the cardinal and diagonal crosses as stronger. For instruments with very different prices, such as an index at 20,000 or a currency at 1.10, traders scale the price by powers of ten first, since the square root method depends on the size of the number. There is no single agreed way to do this.

## How to read it

**Levels as support and resistance.** The levels above and below the start price are watched as places where price may pause or turn. A level that also matches a swing high or low, or a round number, is considered more interesting.

**Cardinal and diagonal crosses.** Some Gann followers treat the 90 and 180 degree levels as stronger than the 45 degree ones.

**Time and price.** Gann writing also links angles to time, so that a price level and a date might line up. Those claims are harder to test and are outside the scope of this guide.

**Breaks.** As with any level, a close beyond it suggests that the next level in the sequence may become the target.

**Confluence.** Traders often check whether a Gann level lines up with Fibonacci levels, previous highs and lows or round numbers. With many levels on the chart, some will always line up by chance, so judge this with care.

## Best market states

**Reversals (main use).** The levels are used as areas to watch for a turn, along with a price signal such as a reversal candle.

**Ranges.** A pair of levels around the start price can frame a range, but any set of levels can.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong trend, price passes through the levels with little reaction.

**Breakouts.** A break through a level is read as a move toward the next one, but this is a rule of thumb.

**Volatility.** Because levels are spaced by square roots, they get further apart at higher prices, and they do not adapt to changes in volatility.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Starting point | Use an objective high or low and keep to the same rule each time |
| Angle set | Choose which steps you use (for example 90 and 180) before looking at the chart |
| Candle signal | Wait for a reversal candle or rejection at the level |
| Confluence | A level near an older support or resistance has more reason to matter |
| Stop | Beyond the level, with room for noise |

Because the levels are so many and so closely spaced, it is easy to find a level that "worked" after the fact. Fix your rules before looking at the chart.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the levels as exact. Price rarely turns precisely on a calculated number.
- Picking the starting price after seeing where price turned.
- Drawing so many levels that every price is near one, which makes any outcome look like a hit.
- Assuming claims about Gann's results are proven. They are not backed by independent records.
- Mixing different versions of the method in one chart without saying so.
- Ignoring price scaling, so that levels on indices and currencies look nonsensical.

## FAQ

**What is the Gann Square of Nine?**
It is a spiral arrangement of numbers, linked to W. D. Gann, that traders use to calculate possible support and resistance prices. Prices on the same angle lines are treated as related. It is a trader-education idea with no academic evidence base.

**How do you calculate Square of Nine levels?**
Take the square root of a starting price, add or subtract 0.25, 0.5 or 1 for 45, 90 or 180 degrees, then square the result. For a start of 100, adding 1 to the square root gives 11, which squares to 121.

**Did W. D. Gann use the Square of Nine?**
Gann's writing is hard to interpret, and many modern methods are later reconstructions. Authors disagree about what he actually used, so it is safer to say the method is attributed to him than to claim it is his.

**Does the Square of Nine work?**
There is no published statistical evidence that it predicts turning points better than other levels. Some traders find it useful as a set of reference prices, and any reaction may simply reflect other traders watching the same numbers.

**What is the difference between the Square of Nine and a Gann fan?**
The Square of Nine gives horizontal price levels from a number spiral. A Gann fan draws sloping lines from a pivot at set angles, such as 1x1. Both come from the Gann tradition, but they work differently.
