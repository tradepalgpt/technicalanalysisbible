Wyckoff accumulation is a schematic for a trading range that forms after a decline, where large buyers are said to be building positions before a rise. It moves through phases A to E, with set events such as the selling climax, the spring and the sign of strength. It is a reading framework, not a guarantee, and real charts rarely match it exactly.

## What is Wyckoff accumulation?

In the Wyckoff view, a market that has fallen for some time may stop falling because large buyers begin to take over supply from weaker holders. They buy slowly, so price does not jump. The result is a sideways range. When the buyers have enough, supply is used up and price moves out of the range upwards, which is the start of the markup phase.

Richard D. Wyckoff described this as the work of a single imagined operator, the Composite Man. The term is a thinking aid. See [Wyckoff Method](/wyckoff-method/) for the three laws and the Composite Man idea. The accumulation schematic is not a rule of the market. It is a template that readers compare against a chart, and the match is often loose.

## How it is identified

The range follows a decline. Phases A to E describe what happens inside and just after it.

**Phase A: stopping the decline.**
- **PS (preliminary support):** the first sign of buying after the fall, often on rising volume. Price usually falls further afterwards.
- **SC (selling climax):** a sharp drop on very heavy volume with wide bars, then a bounce. It often marks the low of the range.
- **AR (automatic rally):** the bounce after the climax. Its high sets the top of the range.
- **ST (secondary test):** price returns toward the climax low on lower volume. Lower volume suggests less selling pressure.

**Phase B: building the cause.** Price swings between the range edges for a time, possibly with several secondary tests. Volume tends to shrink as supply is absorbed.

**Phase C: the test.**
- **Spring:** a brief move below the range low that reverses quickly, designed to shake out weak holders and test whether sellers remain. See [Spring and upthrust (Wyckoff)](/wyckoff-spring-upthrust/).
- **Test:** a retest of the spring area, ideally on low volume, which shows supply has dried up.
Not every range has a spring. Some show a "shakeout" or no clear test at all.

**Phase D: strength appears.**
- **SOS (sign of strength):** a rally from the low area up toward or beyond the top of the range, on rising volume and wide bars.
- **LPS (last point of support):** a pullback that holds above the earlier low, on lower volume. There may be more than one.

**Phase E: leaving the range.**
- **BU/JAC (back-up and jump across the creek):** price crosses the top of the range (the "creek"), then pulls back to test it as support before the markup runs.

**Worked example.** A stock falls from 80 to 50. A selling climax at 50 on heavy volume is followed by an automatic rally to 60. The range is 50 to 60. Price retests 50.5 on lower volume (ST), then drifts inside the range for weeks (phase B). A spring dips to 48 and closes back at 51 the same day (phase C), followed by a quiet retest at 50.5 (test). Price then closes at 62 on rising volume (SOS), pulls back to 58 on low volume (LPS), and later holds the 60 level on a retest (BU). The range height is 10, so a rough point-and-figure style count suggests a possible 10-point move above the break, to around 70. That count is a guide only.

## How to read it

**The range edges.** The climax low and the automatic rally high set support and resistance. Price staying inside them is the base of the reading.

**Volume at the lows.** Heavy volume at the climax and lighter volume on the later tests suggests selling is fading. If volume rises again on each push to the low, the reading is weaker.

**The spring.** A fast recovery after a dip below the low is the key phase C signal. A dip that holds below the low and keeps falling is not a spring. It is a breakdown, and the idea is wrong.

**Sign of strength.** A move up that has both wide bars and rising volume is read as demand taking control. A weak, low-volume rally into the top of the range is less convincing.

**Last point of support.** A higher low on lower volume after the SOS shows that sellers are not returning. Some traders enter here, with a stop below it.

**Jump across the creek.** A close above the range top, then a pullback that holds it as support, is the final sign that markup may be starting.

## Best market states

**Ranges after a decline (best).** The schematic only applies where a fall has stopped and price is moving sideways.

**Reversals.** The aim is to catch the turn from a downtrend into an uptrend.

**Trends (weak).** A decline that never forms a range, or a range that is only a brief pause in a larger fall, may look like accumulation but is just continuation. It then breaks lower.

**Low volume markets.** The volume reading is not reliable where volume data is poor or fragmented.

**Time frames.** The schematic is used on daily, weekly and intraday charts. Very small time frames often show noise rather than clean phases.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior decline | A clear fall before the range |
| Climax | SC on heavy volume with a clear AR bounce |
| Tests | Secondary tests on lower volume |
| Phase C | A spring or shakeout that reverses quickly, with a quiet retest |
| Strength | SOS on wide bars and rising volume |
| Support | LPS holds above the earlier lows |
| Exit | A close above the creek, with a back-up that holds |
| Stop | Below the spring low or the last point of support |

## Common mistakes

- Calling every range after a fall "accumulation". Many ranges are only a rest before more decline.
- Buying the first volume spike. A selling climax is clear only after the bounce.
- Expecting a spring in every range. It is a common event, not a required one.
- Treating a break below the range that keeps going as a spring.
- Ignoring volume and using price shape alone.
- Entering without a stop at the point where the idea is proven wrong.

## FAQ

**What is Wyckoff accumulation?**
It is a schematic for a range that forms after a decline, where large buyers are thought to build positions before price rises. It moves through phases A to E and ends with price leaving the range upward.

**What are the main Wyckoff accumulation events?**
They are PS (preliminary support), SC (selling climax), AR (automatic rally), ST (secondary test), Spring, Test, SOS (sign of strength), LPS (last point of support) and BU/JAC (back-up and jump across the creek).

**What is a Wyckoff spring?**
A spring is a short move below the range low that quickly reverses back into the range. It tests whether sellers remain. If price holds below the low and keeps falling, it was a real breakdown, not a spring.

**Does accumulation always lead to a rise?**
No. Ranges can break either way. A range that looks like accumulation can fail and turn into a fall. That is why traders look for confirmation, such as a sign of strength and a successful back-up, and use a stop.

**How is accumulation different from distribution?**
Accumulation forms after a decline and aims to end with a rise. [Distribution](/wyckoff-distribution/) forms after an advance and aims to end with a fall. They are mirror images, with the same phases and matching events on the opposite side.
