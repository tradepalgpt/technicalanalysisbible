A trendline is a straight line drawn on a chart through a series of rising lows (an uptrend) or falling highs (a downtrend). It shows the direction and slope of a trend. Traders use it as a moving support or resistance line, as a place to enter, and as a signal that the trend may be changing when it breaks.

## What is a trendline?

An uptrend is a series of higher highs and higher lows. A line joining the lows slopes upward and is called a rising trendline. It works like a sloping floor: price pulls back to it and, while the trend lasts, bounces. In a downtrend the line joins the falling highs and works like a sloping ceiling.

Two points are enough to draw a line, but two points prove nothing. A third touch that holds is the usual test that the line is real. The more touches and the longer the line has lasted, the more attention it usually gets, and the bigger the reaction when it finally breaks.

The tool is one of the oldest in charting and is covered in every standard textbook. There is no single inventor. Trendlines are subjective, since two people can draw slightly different lines on the same chart, which is both their strength (flexibility) and their weakness (hindsight).

## How it is drawn

1. **Find the swing points.** In an uptrend, mark two clear swing lows. See [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/).
2. **Join them.** Draw a straight line through the two lows and extend it to the right.
3. **Test it.** A third low that touches or comes close to the line, then bounces, confirms it.
4. **Redraw when needed.** If price breaks the line and the line was drawn badly, adjust it using the new swings, but be honest about why.

Most traders draw from wick lows or from candle bodies and keep to that choice. Using a logarithmic scale on long-term charts keeps the slope fair when prices change by a large percentage.

**Worked example.** An uptrend has swing lows at 100 (on bar 10) and 106 (on bar 20). The slope is 6 points over 10 bars, or 0.6 per bar. By bar 30 the line sits at 112. A pullback to about 112 that bounces is a third touch. A close at 109 would be a break below the line.

## How to read it

**Bounce.** Price falls to the line and turns. In an uptrend, traders may buy the bounce with a stop just beneath the line.

**Break.** A close beyond the line, preferably with volume, is read as a warning that the trend has changed or paused. It is not proof of a reversal: price often goes sideways first.

**Retest.** After a break, price often returns to the old line from the other side, which may now act as resistance. See [role reversal](/role-reversal/).

**Slope.** A steep line is hard to maintain and often breaks, after which price may simply move to a gentler slope. A gentle line is more durable.

**Number of touches.** More touches add weight, but each touch also tests the line, and many traders know it is there.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The tool exists to describe a trend and give pullback entries within it.

**Reversals (good).** A break of a long-held trendline is one of the classic first signs of a change, often combined with a [divergence](/divergence/) or a failed new high.

**Breakouts (good).** A trendline break can signal the end of a pullback in a larger trend, and the start of a new leg.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways price has no slope to follow, so lines drawn there are random.

**Volatility.** In volatile markets, wicks overshoot the line. Using closes and a small buffer based on [ATR](/atr/) helps avoid being stopped out by noise.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Points | At least two swing points, and a third touch that holds |
| Spacing | Touches well spread out, not clustered together |
| Slope | Moderate, not near-vertical |
| Break | Close beyond the line, not just a wick |
| Volume | Rising on the break |
| Retest | A return to the line that fails to reclaim it |

## Common mistakes

- Forcing a line through points that do not really line up.
- Redrawing the line after each new bar until it fits, which makes it useless as a test.
- Drawing steep lines that break at the first pause.
- Reading a break as a certain reversal. Many breaks lead to sideways moves or failed breaks.
- Using wicks on some points and closes on others.
- Acting on the break of a line drawn from only two points.

## Pairs well with

- **[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/)**: a trendline that meets a horizontal level creates a stronger area.
- **[Volume](/volume/)**: a trendline break on rising volume is more convincing than one on thin trade.
- **[RSI](/rsi/)**: divergence at the same time as a trendline break strengthens the warning.
- **[ATR](/atr/)**: sets a buffer below the line for the stop.

## FAQ

**How many points do you need to draw a trendline?**
Two points are needed to draw it, and a third touch that holds is generally used to confirm it. With only two points, the line is just a guess. More touches usually mean more traders are watching it.

**What happens when a trendline breaks?**
It is read as a warning that the trend may be pausing or changing. Price may reverse, go sideways or bounce back above the line. Many traders wait for a close beyond the line and a failed retest before acting.

**Should I draw trendlines on wicks or closes?**
Both are used. Wicks show the extremes and closes show where price settled. Choose one method and use it consistently. Using a logarithmic scale for long-term charts is also common.

**Are trendlines reliable?**
They are useful but subjective, and they fail often. Because lines are drawn by eye, results depend on the person. Testing a clear set of drawing rules on your own market is the only way to know how well they work for you.

**What is the difference between a trendline and a trend channel?**
A trendline is a single line along the lows or highs. A [trend channel](/trend-channel/) adds a second, parallel line on the other side of price, showing the trend's range as well as its direction.
