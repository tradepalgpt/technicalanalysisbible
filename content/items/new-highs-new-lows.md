New Highs - New Lows counts how many stocks in an exchange or index closed at a new 52-week high, minus how many closed at a new 52-week low. A positive, rising figure shows broad strength; a falling or negative figure shows broad weakness. Traders use it to confirm index trends and to catch divergences.

## What is New Highs - New Lows?

Every day, some stocks close at their highest price in 52 weeks and others at their lowest. The number of each is published for major exchanges. Subtracting lows from highs gives a single net figure for the market.

In a healthy rise, new highs should be plentiful and new lows few. When an index keeps rising but fewer stocks make new highs, the advance is narrowing. When an index falls and new lows keep expanding, the decline is spreading.

The measure has no single inventor and appears in standard technical analysis textbooks, including Murphy's, as a core breadth tool. Versions differ in how they are scaled, smoothed and displayed.

## Formula

Net new highs = number of stocks at new 52-week highs - number at new 52-week lows

**Worked example.** On one day, 150 stocks close at new 52-week highs and 50 close at new 52-week lows.
Net new highs = 150 - 50 = **100**

On another day, 40 highs and 120 lows give 40 - 120 = **-80**.

**Variants.**
- **Smoothed.** Many traders average the net figure over 10 days to cut noise.
- **Cumulative.** Some add the daily net figures into a running line, like the advance-decline line.
- **Percentage.** Some divide by the total number of issues so the scale is comparable over time. The number of listed stocks changes, which affects raw counts.
- **Window length.** 52 weeks is standard, but some charts use 26 weeks or other periods.

## How to read it

**Direction.** Net new highs rising with the index confirms the rise. Net new highs falling while the index rises is a warning.

**Zero line.** Positive means more highs than lows; negative means the reverse. A move from negative to positive is often read as an improvement in breadth.

**Divergence.** If the index makes a new high but net new highs are below their earlier peak, participation has narrowed. This is not a timing signal.

**Expansion of new lows.** A sudden jump in new lows while the index is still near its highs can show stress under the surface. It is one of the conditions in some versions of the [Hindenburg Omen](/hindenburg-omen/).

**Washouts.** A very large count of new lows during a decline is sometimes read as a sign of heavy selling that may be near exhaustion, though it can also keep growing.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** Confirmation of a rise or fall by wide participation is the main use.

**Reversals (useful).** Divergences and extreme lows can mark turns, with price confirmation.

**Ranges (weak).** In sideways markets, highs and lows are both small and the figure hovers near zero.

**Volatility.** Sharp sell-offs can produce huge counts of new lows in a few days, so use smoothing and context.

## Settings

| Setting | Choice | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Window | 52 weeks | The standard definition of a new high or low |
| Smoothing | 10-day average | Common; reduces daily noise |
| Scale | Raw count or percentage of issues | Percentage is more comparable over decades |
| Market | NYSE, Nasdaq, or a specific index | Results differ between markets |

The exchange matters. The NYSE and Nasdaq include different types of stocks, so their counts can disagree.

## Common mistakes

- Comparing raw counts across decades without adjusting for the number of listed stocks.
- Treating one day's spike as a signal. Smooth or watch for persistence.
- Using it on its own as a timing tool.
- Ignoring that a stock can make a new low simply because its price has been falling for a year.
- Mixing exchanges, so the numbers disagree with the index being traded.

## FAQ

**What does New Highs - New Lows show?**
It shows how many stocks are at new 52-week highs minus how many are at new 52-week lows. A rising figure shows broad strength, and a falling one shows broad weakness. It helps check whether an index move has wide support.

**What is a good reading?**
There is no fixed level. Positive and rising is healthier than negative and falling. Compare current readings with the series' own history and with the index, rather than with a number from a book.

**Does it work as a leading indicator?**
It can give early warning when an index rises while new highs shrink, but the count is built from past prices. Most traders treat it as confirmation and use price signals for timing.

**Why use a 10-day average?**
Daily counts are noisy. A 10-day average smooths them so the trend is easier to see. The 10-day length is a common choice, not a rule.

**Is it only for stocks?**
Mostly yes. The idea can apply to any group of instruments with enough members, such as ETFs or futures, but published data are best for stock exchanges.
