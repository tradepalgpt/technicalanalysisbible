Multiple time frame analysis means looking at the same market on two or more chart time frames before trading. A higher time frame sets the direction, and a lower one gives the entry. The aim is to trade with the larger move rather than against it.

## What is multiple time frame analysis?

A chart looks different on each time frame. A downtrend on the hourly chart can be a small pullback inside an uptrend on the daily chart. If you only look at one, you can easily trade against a larger move that is stronger than the one you see.

The idea of checking larger time frames is old and common across technical analysis. Its best-known formal version is Alexander Elder's Triple Screen system, described in *Trading for a Living* (Wiley, 1993). Elder used three time frames, each about five times longer than the one below, and different tools on each. He said the trader should use the longest time frame for the main trend and the shortest for the entry.

The method does not predict anything. It is a filter: it reduces trades against the larger direction and gives a sense of where the nearest major levels are.

## How it is identified

Think of it as a procedure, not a pattern. A simple three-step version:

1. **Pick a trading time frame.** The one on which you place trades, for example the hourly chart.
2. **Pick a higher time frame about four to six times longer.** For the hourly chart, the four-hour or daily chart. This gives direction.
3. **Pick a lower time frame for timing.** For example 15 minutes, to refine the entry.

**Elder's Triple Screen (summary).** First screen: use a trend tool on the longer chart, for example the slope of the MACD histogram or of a moving average, to set direction. Second screen: on the middle chart, use an oscillator to find pullbacks against that direction. Third screen: on the shortest chart, use a trailing entry order beyond the previous bar to join the move.

**Worked example.** The daily chart shows price above a rising 50-day average, so the bias is long. On the hourly chart, price pulls back to a support level at 100 while RSI dips below 40. On the 15-minute chart, price breaks above the last small swing high at 101. The trader buys at 101 with a stop at 99. If the daily chart had shown a falling 50-day average, the same hourly setup would have been skipped or taken with less size.

## How to read it

**Higher time frame sets bias.** Take only trades in that direction, or reduce size on trades against it.

**Levels carry down.** Support and resistance from the daily or weekly chart matter more than those drawn on a 5-minute chart. Mark them first.

**Agreement.** When all time frames point the same way, the case is stronger. When they conflict, many traders stay out.

**Pullbacks.** A move against the higher trend that shows up on the lower chart is often just a pullback. See [pullback trading](/pullback-trading/).

**Where the trade ends.** A trade on the lower time frame can be stopped out by noise that is only a candle on the higher time frame. Know which time frame your stop lives on.

## Best market states

**Trends (good).** The method finds pullbacks within a larger trend. This is its most common use.

**Ranges (good).** The higher time frame shows where the range edges are, and the lower time frame times the entry near them.

**Reversals.** A divergence or break of structure on a lower time frame may be an early warning, but the higher time frame decides whether it matters.

**Volatility.** In choppy markets all time frames can look messy. Analysis then adds confusion, not clarity.

**Limits.** More time frames do not give more certainty. Beyond three, the benefit tends to drop and conflicting signals multiply.

## Settings

| Trading style | Higher | Middle | Lower |
|---|---|---|---|
| Position | Monthly | Weekly | Daily |
| Swing | Weekly | Daily | Four-hour |
| Day trading | Daily | One-hour | 15-minute |
| Scalping | One-hour | 15-minute | One-minute |

These are common examples. The factor of about five between steps follows Elder. Other traders use factors of three to six. Keep to one scheme and apply it consistently.

## Common mistakes

- Using too many time frames and finding a reason for any trade.
- Looking at time frames that are too close together, such as the 5-minute and 10-minute, which show nearly the same picture.
- Searching the lower time frames for confirmation after you have already decided to trade.
- Ignoring the higher time frame because the lower one "looks perfect".
- Setting a stop on a short time frame for a trade that depends on a long-time-frame idea.
- Changing the time frames after a loss to fit the outcome.

## FAQ

**What is multiple time frame analysis?**
It is checking the same market on more than one chart time frame, usually a higher one for direction and a lower one for the entry. It helps avoid trading against the bigger picture, though it does not predict price.

**How many time frames should I use?**
Two or three is common. Elder's Triple Screen uses three. More than that tends to add conflicting signals without much extra insight. Pick a set that fits your holding period and keep it fixed.

**What is Elder's Triple Screen?**
It is a method from Alexander Elder's 1993 book *Trading for a Living*. The first screen sets the trend on the longer chart, the second finds a counter-trend pullback with an oscillator, and the third times the entry on the shortest chart.

**What if the time frames disagree?**
Many traders stay out, or take only the trade in the higher time frame direction with reduced size. A disagreement means the picture is unclear, and waiting costs nothing.

**Which time frame should come first?**
Start with the longest time frame you use, to find the main trend and key levels. Then move down to your trading chart, and last to the entry chart. Working from the top down keeps the larger picture in view.
