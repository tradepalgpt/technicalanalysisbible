A rounding bottom, also called a saucer, is a slow, U-shaped reversal pattern at the end of a downtrend. Price falls gradually, flattens out, then rises gradually, tracing a bowl. It shows selling pressure fading and buying slowly taking over. A close above the left rim of the bowl is the usual entry signal.

## What is a rounding bottom?

Most reversal patterns, such as a [double bottom](/double-bottom/) or [inverse head and shoulders](/inverse-head-and-shoulders/), show sharp turning points. A rounding bottom is different: the turn is slow. Selling eases off over weeks or months, price drifts sideways at the lows, and then buying builds gradually.

Chart readers often explain it as a hand-over from sellers to buyers. Early on, sellers dominate. At the base, the two sides are balanced. Later, buyers dominate. The curve reflects this smooth shift in the balance, without an event that triggers a sudden reversal. Wyckoff-style accumulation has a similar idea of gradual buying at the lows, though the two are different frameworks.

The pattern is described in classic chart-pattern literature, including Edwards and Magee's *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, and in later reference works. No single person is credited with it. It is related to the [cup and handle](/cup-and-handle/), which is a rounded base with a short pullback before the breakout.

It is a slow, subjective pattern. Exactly how round is round enough is a matter of opinion, which makes it harder to test than patterns with clear lines.

## How it is identified

1. **A prior downtrend.** Price has fallen for some time before the bowl begins.
2. **A gradual decline.** Lower lows that come with shallower falls, forming the left side of the bowl.
3. **A flat base.** Price moves in a narrow area near the lows for a while.
4. **A gradual rise.** Higher lows and higher highs form the right side, ending near the starting level.
5. **Breakout.** A close above the rim, which is the high at the start of the decline.

**Measured target.** Measure the depth of the bowl, from the rim to the lowest point, and project it up from the breakout.

**Worked example.** Price falls from a rim of 50 to a low of 40 over four months, flattens around 40 to 42 for two months, then rises gradually back to 50. Depth is 50 - 40 = 10.

A close at 51 above the rim confirms the pattern. Target = 51 + 10 = **61**. A stop might sit below the most recent swing low on the right side, such as 47, which is a modest risk compared with the rim breakout.

Real bowls are messy, with small bumps along the curve. A smooth line drawn through the lows can help, or a line chart of closing prices, which hides some noise. Even then, the shape is a judgement.

## How to read it

**Slow shift in control.** The more even the curve and the longer the base, the more it is read as a genuine change in the balance of supply and demand.

**Volume.** Classic teaching expects volume to be high on the left side, shrink at the base, and pick up as price climbs the right side. Rising volume on the breakout adds weight.

**The rim as resistance.** The level at the start of the decline is where earlier sellers were active. A close above it, and a hold on a retest, is the key signal. See [Role reversal](/role-reversal/).

**Patience needed.** The pattern can take months or years on a weekly or monthly chart. Entering inside the bowl, before the breakout, is guesswork.

**Failure.** If price rises to the rim and is rejected, forming a lower high, the bowl may turn into a larger range or resume the downtrend.

## Best market states

**Reversals after a long downtrend (best).** The pattern needs a decline to reverse and a visible slowing of selling.

**Trends (not applicable).** It does not describe a continuing trend. A gentle dip in an up-trend is better described as a pullback or a cup.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, curved shapes can be found almost anywhere. Without a prior downtrend, the label means little.

**Volatility.** Volatility often falls toward the base and rises on the breakout. Highly volatile markets rarely give clean bowls.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear, extended decline |
| Shape | Gradual, roughly symmetrical, not a sharp V |
| Duration | Long enough that the curve is meaningful for the chart |
| Volume | Lower at the base, rising toward and through the rim |
| Break | A close above the rim, not just an intraday spike |
| Stop | Below the right-side swing low, or below the base |

## Common mistakes

- Seeing a bowl in every sideways market after a dip.
- Buying inside the bowl before price breaks the rim.
- Forcing a sharp V-shaped recovery into a rounded shape. See [V-reversal](/v-reversal/).
- Using a stop that is far too tight for a slow, long pattern.
- Ignoring the right-side lows, where a new lower low breaks the pattern.
- Treating the measured target as certain.

## FAQ

**What is a rounding bottom?**
It is a gradual, U-shaped reversal pattern at the end of a downtrend. Price slows its decline, flattens, then slowly rises. It shows selling pressure fading and buyers taking control over a long period.

**Is a rounding bottom the same as a cup and handle?**
They are close relatives. Both have a rounded base. A [cup and handle](/cup-and-handle/) adds a short pullback (the handle) near the right rim before the breakout, and it is usually found after an uptrend.

**How long does a rounding bottom take to form?**
It varies, from several weeks on a daily chart to years on a monthly chart. There is no minimum, but the pattern is slow, and a very short one is more likely to be a plain V-shaped turn.

**How do you trade a rounding bottom?**
Most traders wait for a close above the rim, often with a rise in volume, and place the stop below the recent right-side low. Some buy on a retest of the rim. Entering earlier is more of a guess.

**How reliable is the pattern?**
No pattern works every time. The shape is subjective, so results depend on how strictly you define it. Test your rules on your own market and treat the target as a rough guide.
