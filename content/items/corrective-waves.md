Corrective waves are the sideways or counter-trend phases in Elliott wave theory. They move against, or pause, the larger trend. The three main families are the zigzag (a sharp ABC), the flat (a sideways ABC) and the triangle (five overlapping legs). Traders use them to guess when a pullback is ending.

## What are corrective waves?

In [Elliott wave](/elliott-wave/) theory, price moves in two kinds of waves. Impulse waves push in the direction of the larger trend and have five parts. Corrective waves go against it, or move sideways, and are labelled with letters: A, B, C. They are harder to recognise than [impulse waves](/impulse-wave/) because they come in many forms and often blend together.

Ralph Nelson Elliott set out the wave principle in the 1930s, with *The Wave Principle* appearing in 1938. Later, A. J. Frost and Robert Prechter popularised and organised the rules in *Elliott Wave Principle* (1978).

Elliott theory is a trader-education framework. It has not been shown to have predictive power in academic studies, and two analysts often count the same chart differently. Treat these shapes as descriptions to test, not laws.

## How it is identified

**Zigzag (5-3-5).** A sharp three-wave decline in an uptrend. Wave A has five sub-waves, B is a three-wave bounce, C has five sub-waves again and usually goes beyond the end of A. It is steep and moves against the trend.

**Flat (3-3-5).** A sideways correction. A has three sub-waves, B retraces most or all of A, and C has five sub-waves ending near the end of A. In an "expanded flat", B goes past the start of A and C goes past the end of A.

**Triangle (3-3-3-3-3).** Five overlapping legs labelled A to E, each with three sub-waves, contracting inside two converging lines. It usually appears before the final move of a trend.

**Combinations.** Two or three simple corrections joined by a small connecting wave. These are often called double and triple threes and are the hardest to spot.

**Worked example.** A trend leg rises from 100 to 140, a move of 40. A zigzag pulls back in three waves: A down to 125, B up to 133, C down to 120. The total fall is 20, which is half of the 40-point move, inside the common 38% to 62% zone. A trader following the theory would look for a signal near 120 to join the uptrend again.

## How to read it

**Alternation.** Elliott's guideline is that if wave 2 is sharp, wave 4 is likely to be sideways, and the reverse.

**Depth.** Corrections in the middle of an impulse often retrace 38% to 62% of the previous wave using [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/). Wave 2 of an impulse cannot go beyond the start of wave 1 under the standard rules.

**End of correction.** The count says a correction is over when a new impulse begins in the trend direction, for example a five-wave move that breaks the C wave's trend line.

**Counting changes.** If price moves past a level the count needs it not to cross, the count is invalid and must be redone. That is a fair stop point.

## Best market states

**Ranges (good).** Flats and triangles match what traders see as consolidation, so they fit sideways phases.

**Trends (good).** Zigzags are the corrective pause inside a trend.

**Reversals (weak).** A zigzag against the trend can turn out to be the start of a new trend, and the count is then rewritten. That is a built-in weakness: the labels are often clear only after the fact.

**Volatility.** Quiet triangles often end in a sudden move, but this is a tendency, not a rule.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior impulse | A clear five-wave move before the correction |
| Internal count | A is 5 or 3 waves, B is 3 waves, C is 5 waves (zigzag, flat) |
| Depth | Wave 2 not beyond the start of wave 1 |
| Triangle | Five legs contracting between two lines |
| Resumption | A break of the C wave's line in the trend direction |
| Stop | Beyond the point that would invalidate the count |

## Common mistakes

- Forcing a count that fits the picture rather than the rules.
- Calling every three-leg move an ABC.
- Entering before the correction shows any sign of finishing.
- Treating Fibonacci depth as a rule rather than a guideline.
- Ignoring the higher timeframe, which decides whether the move is a pullback or a trend change.
- Changing the count after every bar and never admitting it is wrong.

## FAQ

**What are the three types of corrective waves?**
The three families are the zigzag (5-3-5), the flat (3-3-5) and the triangle (3-3-3-3-3). Combinations of these, often called double and triple threes, also occur. Each has its own internal wave count and typical shape.

**What is the difference between a zigzag and a flat?**
A zigzag is a sharp, steep correction where C goes beyond the end of A. A flat is sideways: B retraces most of A and C ends close to where A ended. Flats appear in strong trends, zigzags in deeper pullbacks.

**How do you know a correction is finished?**
In theory, a correction ends when a five-wave move in the trend direction begins. Traders often watch for a break of the trend line drawn through the correction. Counts can be wrong, so a stop beyond the invalidation point is standard.

**Can corrective waves be traded?**
Some traders enter at the end of C or after a triangle breakout. The method is subjective, and two analysts may count the same chart differently. There is no academic evidence it gives an edge, so test it before using real money.

**How is it different from a two-legged pullback?**
A [two-legged pullback](/two-legged-pullback/) is a loose price-action description of a pause in a trend. Corrective waves are a strict set of labelled patterns inside Elliott theory with internal rules for each leg.
