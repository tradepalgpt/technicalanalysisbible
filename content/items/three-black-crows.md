Three black crows is a pattern of three long falling candles in a row, each opening inside the previous candle's body and closing near its low. It shows steady, determined selling. After a rise, it can mark the start of a decline.

## What are three black crows?

The name comes from a row of dark birds perched in a line. "Black" refers to the falling candles, which are drawn filled or black in traditional charts. On modern charts they are often red.

Nison describes the pattern in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*, and it also appears in Morris and Bulkowski. Each day, sellers push price lower and hold the losses into the close. Three days of that in a row points to persistent supply, not a one-off dip.

It mirrors [Three White Soldiers](/three-white-soldiers/). By the time the third candle closes, price has already dropped a long way. That makes the pattern more a confirmation of a decline than an early warning of one.

## How it is identified

Three black crows has:

1. **Three consecutive falling candles**, each with a long body.
2. **Each close lower than the one before.**
3. **Each open inside the previous candle's body**, usually in its lower half. This keeps the fall orderly.
4. **Closes near the low of each candle**, so lower wicks are short.

It usually follows an advance or a stretch near highs. A common caution is that if the first candle opens well below the prior close, or if the candles are short, the shape is a weaker version.

**Worked example.** After a rise:
- Day one opens at 90.0, closes at 87.5 (low 87.4).
- Day two opens at 88.0, closes at 85.0 (low 84.9).
- Day three opens at 85.5, closes at 82.5 (low 82.4).

Each candle has a body of 2.5 to 3.0, opens within the previous body (88.0 sits inside 87.5 to 90.0, and 85.5 sits inside 85.0 to 88.0) and closes near its low. That is three black crows.

## How to read it

**Steady supply.** Long bodies and low closes show sellers winning each period, not just once.

**Where it starts.** After a rise or a top, the pattern can mark a change in control. It works as confirmation that a decline has begun.

**Where it ends.** After a long fall, it may reflect late selling near a low. The pattern says what has happened, not what comes next.

**Warning signs.** Shrinking bodies, or a third candle that closes well off its low, suggest sellers are tiring. Support just below can also halt the fall.

**Size of the move.** If the three candles have already covered a large distance, a stop above the first candle's high may be far away. Think about how much of the move is already behind you.

## Best market states

**Reversals (good).** At the end of a rise or a top, it can show a convincing change in direction.

**Trends (good).** Early in a decline it confirms that sellers are in control. Later it is less useful.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, three falling candles often mark a move to the bottom of the range and little more.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A rise, or a top |
| Shape | Three long falling bodies, each lower, each opening in the previous body |
| Wicks | Short lower wicks; closes near the lows |
| Position | Not already far extended; check nearby support |
| Volume | Steady or rising volume across the three candles adds weight |

## Common mistakes

- Calling any three falling candles crows without checking body length and closes.
- Selling after the third candle, when a large part of the move is already complete.
- Ignoring nearby support that can stop the decline.
- Overlooking shrinking bodies or a third candle that closes off its low.
- Using it after a long fall, where it can show late selling near a bottom.
- Treating it as a prediction instead of a confirmation of what has happened.

## FAQ

**Are three black crows bearish?**
Yes. Three long falling candles in a row show persistent selling. It is usually read as a bearish sign, especially after a rise. Because the fall is already well under way by the third candle, it works more as confirmation than as an early signal.

**Why are they called "black"?**
In traditional Japanese charts, falling candles were drawn black or filled. Many modern platforms colour them red instead, but the pattern name is unchanged.

**Can the pattern be a warning instead of a sell sign?**
Yes. After a long fall, three strong candles can reflect late selling near a low. Context decides how to read it, along with whether support lies just below.

**What is the opposite pattern?**
[Three White Soldiers](/three-white-soldiers/), which is three long rising candles in a row. It shows steady buying and is read as a bullish sign.

**Do the candles need to be exactly the same size?**
No, but they should all be long and reasonably similar. A shrinking body or a third candle that closes well above its low is a sign that selling is weakening.
