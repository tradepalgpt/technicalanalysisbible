An exhaustion gap is a price gap near the end of a long trend, where the last burst of buying (or selling) leaves a hole that price quickly closes. It is read as a late, emotional push by the last buyers before a pause or turn. Traders mostly use it as a warning to take profits.

## What is an exhaustion gap?

Late in an up-trend, price may suddenly gap higher on excitement. The gap looks like more of the same, but it comes when most of the available buyers have already bought. After the gap, price stalls or reverses and closes the gap within a few bars. The buying has run out of energy, which is where the name comes from.

Chart-pattern writers describe three types of trend gap. A [breakaway gap](/breakaway-gap/) starts a move, a [runaway gap](/runaway-gap/) appears part-way and an exhaustion gap appears at the end. The same logic applies in a downtrend, where a last gap lower is followed by a bounce. A downward version is sometimes tied to panic selling.

The term comes from the early technical analysis literature, including Edwards and Magee's *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends* (first edition 1948). No single person is credited.

The big weakness is that the label is known only afterwards. When a gap appears late in a trend, it may be an exhaustion gap or a runaway gap. Only the next bars show which.

## How it is identified

1. **An extended trend.** Price has already made a large move, often with several earlier gaps.
2. **A gap in the trend's direction.** It looks like a sign of strength.
3. **Very high volume.** Volume often spikes, sometimes the highest of the whole trend. See [Volume climax](/volume-climax/).
4. **A quick failure.** Price stalls, then closes back inside or below the gap, often within one to a few bars.

A common follow-up is an [island reversal](/island-reversal/), where price gaps in the opposite direction soon after, leaving a few bars isolated by gaps on both sides.

**Worked example.** A stock has risen from 60 to 100 over several months. One morning it opens at 104, leaving a gap above the prior high of 100, with volume three times the average. It reaches 105, then sells off. Two days later it closes at 99, back below the bottom of the gap.

The close back below 100 fills the gap. This is the evidence that the gap was a final push, not a fresh start. A trader holding a long position might use the close below the gap as a signal to exit or tighten the stop.

## How to read it

**Warning, not a forecast.** An exhaustion gap says the trend is tired. It does not say how far price will fall, or for how long. Prices may only pause or move sideways.

**Volume spike.** Heavy volume at the end of a long move can show that many were buying at once, perhaps out of fear of missing out. Some of the reversal that follows comes from those buyers leaving.

**Confirmation by the fill.** A close back inside the gap is the key sign. A gap that holds for several days is more likely a runaway gap.

**Use it to protect gains.** Because it is late in a trend, the pattern is mostly used for exits: tightening a trailing stop, selling part of a position, or no longer adding. Look at [Scaling out](/scaling-out/) for one way to do this.

**Bearish and bullish versions.** In an up-trend it warns of a top. In a downtrend it warns of a bottom and may be followed by a sharp bounce.

## Best market states

**Reversals after extended trends (best).** The pattern only makes sense near the end of a long move.

**Trends in progress (caution).** In a strong trend, a gap that appears late can be followed by more gains. Treating every late gap as exhaustion would mean exiting too early.

**Ranges (weak).** A gap in a range usually means nothing about trend exhaustion. See [Common gap](/common-gap/).

**Volatility.** News-driven spikes create many large gaps. Judging them by volume and by the next bars helps avoid false signals.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A long, extended move, often with earlier gaps |
| Gap | Opens beyond the prior high (or low) with a visible hole |
| Volume | A spike well above average |
| Failure | Price closes back inside the gap within a few bars |
| Momentum | Divergence on an oscillator adds weight, see [Divergence](/divergence/) |
| Action | Tighten stop or exit rather than open a new position on the label alone |

## Common mistakes

- Calling every late gap an exhaustion gap and selling a trend too soon.
- Acting on the gap before price fails to hold, when it could still be a runaway gap.
- Ignoring the news behind the gap, which can start a new trend instead of ending one.
- Treating the pattern as a short signal without confirmation.
- Expecting a deep reversal, when the pause may only be a short consolidation.
- Applying it in markets with no overnight gaps, such as the 24-hour forex market on weekdays.

## FAQ

**What is an exhaustion gap?**
It is a gap near the end of a long trend that fills quickly. It looks like a continuation but marks the last burst of buying or selling, so many traders treat it as a warning that a pause or reversal may be near.

**How do you tell an exhaustion gap from a runaway gap?**
Only afterwards. A runaway gap holds and the trend continues. An exhaustion gap is followed by a stall and a close back into the gap. Late timing and a volume spike raise the chance, but do not prove it.

**Is an exhaustion gap a sell signal?**
By itself, no. It is more a prompt to protect profits, such as moving a stop closer. Many traders wait for price to close back inside the gap before acting on a reversal idea.

**Does an exhaustion gap always lead to a reversal?**
No. Some are followed only by a sideways pause, and some are followed by more trend. Treat it as one piece of evidence along with volume, momentum and the wider chart.

**What is the link with an island reversal?**
If price gaps one way, trades for a short time, then gaps back the other way, the isolated bars form an [island reversal](/island-reversal/). An exhaustion gap can be the first half of one.
