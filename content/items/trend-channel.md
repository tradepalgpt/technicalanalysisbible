A trend channel is a pair of parallel lines drawn around a trending price. One line follows the swing lows (in an uptrend) and the other follows the swing highs. The lines mark a path for price: traders look to buy near the lower line in an uptrend, and take profit near the upper line.

## What is a trend channel?

A trend channel extends the [trendline](/trendline/). In an uptrend the trendline connects the rising lows. A second line, parallel to it, is drawn through the highest swing high between those lows. The space between the lines is the channel. Price tends to wander between the two lines in an orderly way while the trend lasts.

Channels come in three shapes. An ascending channel slopes upward, a descending channel slopes downward, and a horizontal channel is just a sideways range, which is why a flat channel is not really a trend tool. See [rectangle pattern](/rectangle-pattern/).

The idea is subjective and old. The classic books present it as an extension of the basic trendline. Calculated channels such as [Donchian channels](/donchian-channels/), [Keltner channels](/keltner-channels/) and [Bollinger Bands](/bollinger-bands/) follow price by a formula, while a drawn trend channel needs a human to choose the points.

## How it is drawn

1. **Draw the main trendline** through two or more swing lows in an uptrend, or highs in a downtrend.
2. **Find the opposite extreme.** In an uptrend, pick the swing high between the lows.
3. **Draw a parallel line** through that high, with the same slope as the trendline.
4. **Check the fit.** Price should touch or come close to both lines over time. If it does not, the channel is unreliable.

**Worked example.** Rising lows at 100 and 108 define a trendline that rises 4 per 5 bars. A swing high of 118 between them sits 6 above the line at that point. The parallel line is drawn 6 above the trendline. Price moving up inside the channel would meet the upper line near 114 to 120 and the lower line near 108 to 112, depending on the date.

## How to read it

**Buy near the lower line in an uptrend.** A bounce from the lower line with a stop just beneath it is the classic entry. The upper line is the target.

**Take profit near the upper line.** Price often reaches the upper line and pulls back. Many traders reduce position there.

**Mid-channel.** The middle of the channel is sometimes used as a rough balance point. Entries there have less room to the target.

**Break below the lower line.** A close below the lower line warns that the trend may be weakening or changing. It is not confirmation of a reversal.

**Break above the upper line.** In an uptrend, a close above the upper line can mean the trend is accelerating. It can also be a false burst that falls back inside. Some traders redraw the channel at a steeper angle, which is a judgement call. A steeper channel often breaks sooner.

**Measuring.** The width of the channel can be used to estimate a target after a break, as in a [measured move](/measured-move/).

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The channel needs an orderly trend with clean swings. Strong, steady trends give the best channels.

**Ranges (weak).** A flat channel is just a range and is better handled with [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).

**Breakouts (weak).** A channel break is the end of the channel's usefulness. After a break, the tool needs to be redrawn.

**Volatility.** Very wide, ragged swings make the upper line hard to place. Calculated channels adapt to volatility better.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Trendline | At least two touches, ideally three |
| Parallel line | Price has reached or come close to it at least once |
| Slope | Not too steep to last |
| Entry | A rejection candle or turn near the edge |
| Volume | Light on pullbacks, heavier on the trend leg |
| Failure | A close beyond the far line ends the channel |

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the second line to include a spike, so the channel is far wider than normal swings.
- Buying the lower line in a downtrend channel because "it is support".
- Ignoring the slope: a very steep channel is hard to keep.
- Redrawing the channel after every break so that it always "worked".
- Treating the upper line as a sure sell point in a strong trend, where price may ride along or above it.
- Confusing a drawn trend channel with calculated channels like Bollinger Bands.

## Pairs well with

- **[Trendline](/trendline/)**: the channel's base line, drawn first.
- **[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/)**: a horizontal level near a channel edge adds weight.
- **[RSI](/rsi/)**: overbought or oversold readings at the channel edges, and divergences, can help time entries.
- **[Volume](/volume/)**: volume that fades on a rally to the upper line suggests weaker demand.

## FAQ

**What is a trend channel?**
It is a pair of parallel lines around a trending price. One follows the swing lows and the other the swing highs. The channel shows where price has tended to move within the trend and gives traders edge levels to watch.

**How do you draw a trend channel?**
Draw a trendline through two or more swing lows (uptrend) or highs (downtrend). Then draw a parallel line through the opposite extreme swing. Adjust only if price clearly touches both lines on later swings.

**What does a channel breakout mean?**
A close beyond the far line means the trend is changing. It may be a pause, a reversal or a faster move. Many traders wait for a retest or a close outside the channel to filter out brief spikes.

**Is a trend channel the same as Bollinger Bands?**
No. A trend channel is drawn by hand from swing points and has a fixed slope. Bollinger Bands are calculated from a moving average and standard deviation and change width with volatility.

**Can I trade a channel in a sideways market?**
A flat channel is a range, so range tools apply. Buying near the bottom and selling near the top is the usual idea. The risk is a break out of the range, so stops are needed on every trade.
