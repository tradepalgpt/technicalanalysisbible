A double bottom is a reversal chart pattern that forms at the end of a downtrend. Price falls to a low, bounces, falls to about the same low and holds. A close above the high between the two lows completes the pattern and suggests the downtrend may be over.

## What is a double bottom pattern?

A double bottom shows sellers failing twice at the same price area. In a downtrend, price reaches a low and bounces. It then falls again but stops near the first low instead of breaking lower. Because the same floor has held twice, some traders conclude that selling pressure there has eased.

The pattern is shaped like the letter W. The high point between the two lows is the resistance level, sometimes called the neckline. Until price closes above it, the pattern is incomplete and price may still break to new lows.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, treat double tops and bottoms with caution: they are less common than they look, and many apparent double bottoms are only pauses in a continuing decline. A confirmed break is what turns the shape into a signal.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **A prior downtrend.** Without one, there is nothing to reverse.
2. **First trough:** a low followed by a bounce.
3. **Peak:** the high of the bounce. This is the resistance level.
4. **Second trough:** a fall back to roughly the same low as the first, which then holds.
5. **Break:** a close above the peak.

The two lows should be at similar prices. A second low well below the first is a breakdown, and one well above it is a higher low, which is a different signal. Murphy notes that the two lows usually need some time between them, so that the pattern reflects a real change in the balance of buyers and sellers rather than a brief wobble.

**Measured target.** Measure the vertical distance from the lows up to the peak. Add that distance to the breakout point.

**Worked example.** Both troughs are at 40 and the peak between them is at 50: a height of 10. If price closes above the peak at 51, the measured target is 51 + 10 = **61**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/) for the general idea.

## How to read it

**Peak break.** A close above the peak completes the pattern. Traders who buy at the second trough are guessing that it will hold, and carry more risk than those who wait.

**Retest.** After the break, price often falls back to the broken peak, which may now act as support (see [Role Reversal](/role-reversal/)). Some traders enter on that retest.

**Volume.** A common reading is that volume is lighter on the second trough than on the first, which suggests selling is fading. Rising volume on the break is taken as confirmation. These are tendencies, not rules.

**Momentum divergence.** If an oscillator makes a higher low at the second trough while price matches the first low, that is a bullish [divergence](/divergence/) and adds weight to the idea.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks above the peak and then closes back below it, or breaks below both troughs, the pattern has failed. Failures can lead to fast moves down.

## Best market states

**Reversals at the end of a clear downtrend (best).** A mature decline that stalls at a known support level is the classic setting.

**Strong downtrends (caution).** In a powerful decline, a retest of the low is often a pause before the next leg down. The W shape alone does not tell you which it is.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, price keeps touching the same lows, so double bottoms appear constantly and mean little. A range low is just support doing its job.

**Volatility.** In volatile markets the lows are rarely equal and breaks are noisy. Allow wider tolerances and wider stops.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear downtrend before the first trough |
| Troughs | At similar prices, with enough time between them |
| Break | Close above the peak, not just a wick |
| Volume | Lighter on the second trough, rising on the break |
| Momentum | Optional: higher oscillator low at the second trough |
| Stop | Below the second trough (conservative: below the lower of the two) |

## Common mistakes

- Buying at the second trough before the peak breaks. The pattern is not complete until the close above resistance.
- Calling any two lows a double bottom. Without a prior downtrend and a break, it is just two lows.
- Accepting lows that are too far apart in price, or too close together in time.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Ignoring the higher-timeframe trend. A double bottom on a short chart inside a strong downtrend is weak evidence.
- Having no plan if price closes back below the peak or breaks the lows.

## FAQ

**Is a double bottom bullish or bearish?**
It is bullish. It forms at the end of a downtrend when price fails twice at the same low, and a close above the peak between the lows signals a possible reversal upward.

**How do you calculate the double bottom target?**
Measure the height from the lows to the peak between them, then add it to the point where price closes above the peak. This is a guide only, and many patterns fall short or overshoot.

**Should the two bottoms be at exactly the same price?**
No. Real lows are rarely identical, and a small gap is normal. If the second low is clearly lower, price has broken down. If it is clearly higher, the picture is closer to a higher low.

**When is the pattern confirmed?**
Only when price closes above the peak between the two lows. Before that, it is a possible pattern. Many traders also look for a rise in volume on the break.

**Where does the stop usually go?**
Most traders put it just below the second trough, where the idea would be proven wrong. A wider stop below the lowest trough costs more per trade but gives price more room.
