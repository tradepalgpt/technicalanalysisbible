Role reversal is the idea that a broken support level often becomes resistance, and a broken resistance level often becomes support. After price closes through a level, it may return to test it from the other side. If the level holds in its new role, traders treat the retest as a possible entry or a confirmation of the break.

## What is role reversal?

Imagine a market that has stopped at 100 three times. Sellers wait there. Then price breaks above and closes at 104. Some sellers who sold at 100 are now losing and may exit if price comes back to 100. Traders who missed the break may want to buy on a pullback. Others who sold short near 100 may be hoping to leave at breakeven. These behaviours can turn the old ceiling into a floor.

The reverse happens when support breaks. Buyers who bought at the old floor are now underwater, and some may sell when price rallies back to their entry. The old floor becomes a ceiling.

This is described in the classic books as support and resistance exchanging roles. Murphy, for example, covers it in his section on support and resistance. The psychological story is a plausible explanation, but it is not proven, and in practice the effect is far from reliable.

## How it is identified

1. **Mark a level** that has been touched at least twice. See [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).
2. **Wait for a break.** Look for a close clearly beyond the level, not just a wick.
3. **Wait for a return.** Price comes back to the level from the new side.
4. **Look for a reaction.** Price stalls or turns away from the level in its new role.

**Worked example.** Resistance at 100 holds three times. Price closes at 104, then falls back to 100.5 and bounces to 106. The old resistance at 100 has acted as support. A close back below 99, however, would show the break was false and the idea has failed.

## How to read it

**Retest entry.** Buying the first pullback to a broken resistance with a stop just below it is a common plan. The risk is small compared with chasing the break.

**Confirmation.** A successful retest supports the idea that the break was real. A level that fails on retest, and price falls back through, suggests a false break. See [false breakout](/false-breakout/).

**Pattern necklines.** In a head and shoulders, the broken neckline often acts as resistance on the retest. The same applies to the middle low of a double top. See [head and shoulders](/head-and-shoulders/).

**Zone, not a line.** The retest may stop a little short of the level or go slightly past it. Treat it as a zone.

**Not every break retests.** Strong breakouts often leave without a pullback, and waiting for a retest means missing them.

## Best market states

**Breakouts (best).** The retest of a broken level is the main use of the idea. See [breakout retest](/breakout-retest/).

**Trends (good).** In an uptrend, old swing highs that price has cleared often act as support on the next pullback, giving entries in the trend direction.

**Ranges (weak).** In a range, price crosses levels repeatedly and the "reversal of roles" is just noise. Be careful claiming the pattern in a sideways market.

**Volatility.** Very volatile markets overshoot levels, so retests are messy. Wider stops, or a zone-based approach, are needed.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Original level | At least two clear touches before the break |
| Break | A close beyond the level, ideally with higher volume |
| Retest | Price returns to the level within a reasonable time |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or bounce at the level |
| Stop | Just beyond the level on the far side |
| Failure | A close back through the level cancels the idea |

## Common mistakes

- Assuming every broken level will be retested. Many are not.
- Trading a retest when the original break was weak, with low volume and a quick return.
- Using the idea in a choppy range where levels flip constantly.
- Entering on the first touch of the retest without waiting for any sign it is holding.
- Setting the stop exactly on the level instead of just beyond it.
- Treating the psychological explanation as a law. It is an idea, not a certainty.

## FAQ

**What is role reversal in trading?**
It is when a broken support level becomes resistance, or a broken resistance becomes support. After price closes through the level, it often returns to test it from the new side and may react there.

**Why does broken resistance become support?**
One explanation is that traders who missed the breakout buy the pullback, while earlier sellers exit near breakeven. Plausible, but unproven. Price may simply fall through the old level on the retest.

**Does the retest always happen?**
No. Strong breakouts often run away without looking back. If you only trade retests, you will miss some of the best moves. That is a trade-off, not a flaw, since the retest gives a closer stop.

**How do I tell a role reversal from a false breakout?**
Role reversal holds: price returns to the level and bounces. A false breakout fails: price closes back through the level and keeps going. You only know after the retest, which is why stops matter.

**Is role reversal the same as a breakout retest?**
They describe the same behaviour from two angles. Role reversal is the principle that levels switch jobs. A [breakout retest](/breakout-retest/) is the trade based on it.
