An RSI failure swing is a reversal signal that appears only on the RSI line, not on price. In a top failure swing, RSI rises above 70, falls back, rises again but stays below its earlier peak, then drops under the low of the pullback. A bottom failure swing is the mirror at 30. It was described by J. Welles Wilder Jr. in 1978.

## What is an RSI failure swing?

A "swing" is a turn in the RSI line. A "failure" is when RSI cannot beat its own previous extreme. Wilder, who created the [Relative Strength Index](/rsi/), described the failure swing as one of its most reliable-looking signals, and traders often read it as clearer than ordinary divergence because it has a defined trigger.

Unlike [divergence](/divergence/), a failure swing does not compare RSI with price. It only looks at RSI itself, which makes it easy to apply: you watch the oscillator and ignore whether price made a new high.

It is a pattern drawn on an indicator, so it inherits RSI's limits. In strong trends RSI can stay overbought for a long time, and failure swings in the trend's direction are rare while counter-trend ones tend to fail.

## How it is identified

**Top failure swing (bearish).** Four steps:

1. RSI rises above 70 (the first peak, call it A).
2. RSI falls back to a swing low (call it B).
3. RSI rises again but peaks below A (the failed peak, call it C). It may or may not go back above 70.
4. RSI then falls below B. That break is the signal.

**Bottom failure swing (bullish).** The mirror:

1. RSI falls below 30 (first trough A).
2. RSI rallies to a swing high B.
3. RSI falls again but bottoms above A (trough C).
4. RSI then rises above B. That break is the signal.

**Worked example.** RSI peaks at 76, falls to 58, rebounds to 69 (below 76) and then drops to 56. The fall below 58 completes the top failure swing. The trigger is the break of 58, not the lower peak at 69.

## How to read it

The signal is the break of the middle swing (B). Until then, the pattern is only a possibility.

**Bearish signal.** Suggests buying momentum failed to renew itself and selling momentum is taking over. Traders may treat this as a reason to exit longs or look for shorts, usually near resistance.

**Bullish signal.** Suggests selling momentum is exhausted. Traders may treat this as a reason to look for longs near support.

**Where the swing happens.** The first peak should be in the overbought zone (above 70) for a top, or the oversold zone (below 30) for a bottom. Some traders relax this rule, but doing so produces more signals and more false ones.

**Direction of the broader move.** A top failure swing after a long uptrend carries more weight to many traders than one in the middle of a range. That is a judgment, not a tested rule.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** The pattern is built to catch a change of direction after a stretched move, especially at a known level.

**Ranges (usable).** In a sideways market the swings are frequent and may line up with the range edges.

**Strong trends (weak).** In a strong trend RSI can pull back from 70, fail to make a higher peak, and break its low while price still rises. The next push then resets everything. Counter-trend failure swings fail often.

**Breakouts and volatility.** A breakout pushes RSI to extremes and may produce a failure swing that is quickly invalidated. The pattern has no volatility filter, so fast markets generate noise.

## Confirmation rules

- **A price level.** The pattern is stronger when price is at known support or resistance.
- **Trend direction.** Taking only failure swings in the direction of the larger trend reduces counter-trend traps.
- **Close, not intrabar.** Many traders wait for RSI to close below B (or above B for a bottom) on the bar, not just touch it.
- **Price trigger.** A break of the price swing low (for tops) alongside the RSI break is a common second condition.
- **Stop placement.** Keep stops on the price chart, such as beyond the high of the swing, not on RSI levels.

## Common mistakes

- Acting on the lower peak before RSI breaks the swing low.
- Ignoring the rule that the first peak must be in the overbought or oversold zone.
- Treating it as a stand-alone system with no price or trend context.
- Placing the stop at an RSI level instead of a price level.
- Trading failure swings against a strong trend.
- Changing the RSI period until the pattern fits past data.

## Pairs well with

## FAQ

**What is a failure swing in RSI?**
It is a reversal pattern on the RSI line. After a peak above 70, RSI pulls back, rises to a lower high, then breaks below the pullback low. The mirror at 30 gives a bullish signal. The trigger is the break, not the lower high.

**Who described the RSI failure swing?**
J. Welles Wilder Jr., the creator of RSI, who presented it in his 1978 book *New Concepts in Technical Trading Systems*. He considered it a primary way of reading the indicator.

**Is a failure swing the same as divergence?**
No. Divergence compares RSI with price. A failure swing looks only at RSI: a failed new peak and a break of the middle swing. They can occur together, which many traders see as stronger evidence.

**Does the second peak need to be above 70?**
Usually not. The first peak should enter the overbought zone. The second peak only needs to be lower than the first. Some traders prefer both above 70, but that is stricter than the original description.

**Do failure swings work in strong trends?**
Counter-trend ones often fail. RSI can drop under a swing low and recover while the trend continues. Use the larger trend and price levels as filters.
