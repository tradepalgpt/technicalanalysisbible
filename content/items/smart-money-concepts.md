Smart Money Concepts (SMC) is a modern trader-education framework that describes price as moves made by large market participants, the "smart money", who are said to hunt liquidity and leave footprints on the chart. It uses its own vocabulary, such as order blocks and fair value gaps. It has no academic evidence base, and its claims are hard to test.

## What is Smart Money Concepts?

Smart Money Concepts is a name for a group of ideas taught online by traders from about the 2010s. Many of them are linked to material from a teacher known as "ICT" (Inner Circle Trader), although the term is now used far beyond that source, and different teachers define the words in different ways. There is no single founding paper or book that everyone follows.

The central claim is that large participants such as banks and funds need a lot of liquidity to fill big orders. Liquidity here means resting orders, such as stops placed above recent highs and below recent lows. The framework says these players may push price to those areas to collect orders, then move price the other way. SMC readers try to mark where this may have happened and trade in the direction of the larger move.

Some of these ideas overlap with older work. Order blocks look similar to [supply and demand zones](/supply-and-demand-zones/). Liquidity sweeps look like a [false breakout](/false-breakout/) or a Wyckoff spring or upthrust. The [Wyckoff Method](/wyckoff-method/) also uses a story about large operators. SMC mostly renames and reframes these ideas, with extra rules about time of day and certain retracement zones.

The framework describes what large players are supposed to do. It cannot see their orders. Prices are shaped by many participants, and no chart can show who placed a trade or why. The claims are best treated as a way of describing price, not as proof of what any group is doing.

## How it is identified

SMC is read from price structure. The common terms are:

- **[Market structure](/market-structure/):** the sequence of swing highs and lows. Higher highs and higher lows mean an up structure. Lower highs and lower lows mean a down structure.
- **[Break of structure (BOS)](/break-of-structure/):** a close beyond the last swing point in the direction of the trend, taken as a sign the trend continues.
- **[Market structure shift (MSS)](/market-structure-shift/):** a break of structure in the opposite direction. Some teachers call it a "change of character" (CHoCH), taken as a possible reversal.
- **[Liquidity sweep](/liquidity-sweep/):** price runs through a swing high or low, taking out resting stops, then reverses. See also [equal highs and lows](/equal-highs-lows/).
- **[Order block](/order-block/):** the last opposite-colour candle before a strong move, marked as a possible area of interest on a later return.
- **[Fair value gap (FVG)](/fair-value-gap/):** a three-candle pattern where the first and third candles do not overlap, leaving a gap in the middle candle's range. Price is expected to return to fill it.
- **[Premium and discount](/premium-discount/):** the upper half of a range is "premium" (sell area) and the lower half "discount" (buy area), split at the 50 percent level.
- **[Optimal trade entry](/optimal-trade-entry/):** an entry in a retracement zone, often described as 62 to 79 percent of a swing.

Definitions differ between teachers. Check any tool or script for the exact rule it uses.

**Worked example.** A market makes a swing low at 100 and a swing high at 120, then a higher low at 110, so the structure is up. Price then closes at 122, above the old high (a BOS). The impulse leg from 110 to 122 started with a down candle at 110 to 111, which is marked as a bullish order block. A three-candle gap between 115 and 117 is marked as a fair value gap. A reader of SMC looks for price to pull back to the zone around 111 to 117 and show signs of buying, with a stop below 110. The framework does not say the pullback will come, or that it will hold.

## How to read it

**Start with the higher time frame.** Mark the structure on a larger chart to set a bias, then use a lower time frame to look for an entry. See [Multiple time frame analysis](/multiple-time-frame-analysis/).

**Look for a sweep, then a shift.** A common idea is that price sweeps a high or low and then breaks structure the other way. This is the sequence many SMC trades are built on.

**Mark zones, then wait.** Order blocks and fair value gaps are areas to watch, not signals by themselves. A reaction in the zone is needed.

**Use premium and discount.** Buying in a discount and selling in a premium is a way of keeping the entry price away from the middle of the range.

**Keep a clear stop.** Each idea gives a natural point where it would be wrong, such as below the order block or the sweep low.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** Break of structure and pullbacks into zones only make sense when there is a trend to follow.

**Reversals.** The sweep and shift sequence is used to look for turns. Many sweeps are simply continuation, so reversals are hard to call.

**Ranges (weak).** Swing highs and lows are everywhere in a range, so marks are easy to draw and hard to rely on.

**Choice of market.** The terms depend on clean, liquid price data. In thin markets or on tiny time frames, gaps and sweeps are often random noise.

**Subjectivity.** What counts as "the last swing" or "the last candle" is a choice. Two readers can mark very different zones on one chart.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Higher time frame bias | An up or down structure on the larger chart |
| Liquidity | A clear swing high or low or equal highs and lows taken out |
| Shift | A close that breaks structure the other way after the sweep |
| Zone | An order block or fair value gap left by the move |
| Location | Entry in discount for buys or premium for sells |
| Reaction | Candle behaviour in the zone, rather than entry on first touch |
| Stop | Beyond the sweep extreme or the zone |

## Common mistakes

- Treating the labels as proof of what large players are doing. The chart cannot show that.
- Marking too many zones, so every price level seems to be covered.
- Taking the first touch of a zone with no reaction.
- Ignoring the larger trend, and trading every shift as a reversal.
- Using lessons from chosen examples as a measure of success. Without a full, honest test of every signal, the results mean little.
- Mixing definitions from different teachers without noticing.

## Sources

## FAQ

**What is Smart Money Concepts?**
It is a modern trader-education framework that uses terms such as order block, fair value gap and liquidity sweep to describe price as the work of large participants. Many ideas are linked to "ICT" teaching. It has no single standard definition.

**Is Smart Money Concepts proven to work?**
There is no academic evidence base for it. Most support comes from chosen chart examples, which cannot show how often the methods work across all cases. Treat it as a way of describing price, and keep risk controls that do not depend on it being right.

**What does ICT mean?**
It stands for Inner Circle Trader, the name used by a trading teacher whose material many SMC terms are linked to. The terms are now used well beyond that source, and the definitions differ between teachers.

**How is SMC different from Wyckoff?**
Both talk about large operators and tests of ranges. The Wyckoff Method dates from the early 1900s and is built around price, volume and phases. SMC is newer, leans on price structure and named zones, and has no single reference text.

**What are the core terms in SMC?**
The main ones are market structure, break of structure, market structure shift, liquidity sweep, order block, fair value gap, and premium and discount. See the linked guides for each one.
