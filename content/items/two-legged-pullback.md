A two-legged pullback is a pause inside a trend that happens in two pushes against the trend, with a small bounce between them. In an uptrend it is a dip down, a weak bounce, then a second dip. Traders look for a signal to buy at the end of the second leg, expecting the trend to resume.

## What is a two-legged pullback?

Trends rarely move in a straight line. They advance, pause, then advance again. A pullback is the pause. A two-legged pullback is the most common shape of that pause: the market drifts against the trend (leg A), bounces briefly (leg B), then drifts against the trend again (leg C).

The reasoning is about behaviour. After the first leg, some traders who were on the right side of the trend take profits, and some counter-trend traders step in. The bounce lets the counter-trend traders believe they were right. When the second leg fails to carry far, they are trapped, and the trend traders who stayed out have a second chance to enter.

The "ABC" label borrows from Elliott wave notation, where [corrective waves](/corrective-waves/) are labelled A, B and C. Price action writers often use the name loosely and do not require strict wave counting.

## How it is identified

An uptrend version, in order:

1. **A clear trend.** A run of higher highs and higher lows.
2. **Leg A:** price pulls back from a swing high and makes a lower low in the short term.
3. **Leg B:** a bounce that fails to reach the old high, or reaches it weakly.
4. **Leg C:** price falls again, usually ending near or slightly beyond the end of leg A.
5. **Signal:** a bar closing in the trend direction (a bullish reversal bar, or a break above a small bar), ideally above the previous bar's high.

The mirror image applies in a downtrend.

**Worked example.** An uptrend peaks at 120. Leg A drops to 112. Leg B bounces to 116. Leg C falls to 111.5. The total pullback from 120 to 111.5 is 8.5 points. If the trend leg before the high started at 100, that is a 20-point run, so the pullback retraced about 42%, within the common 38% to 62% zone. A bullish signal bar at 111.5 with a stop below 111 risks about half a point against a possible move back toward 120.

## How to read it

**Leg two as a test.** The second leg tests whether sellers can push further than the first. If it ends near leg A's low, sellers failed twice and buyers are in control.

**Momentum shrinks.** Leg C often has smaller bars or shorter distance than leg A. That suggests selling is fading.

**Failure.** If leg C goes clearly below the end of leg A and keeps going, the pullback may turn into a reversal or a [range](/range-trading/). A third leg is possible, and is sometimes called a wedge pullback or [three pushes](/three-pushes/).

**Support zones.** The end of leg C is more meaningful when it lands on a prior swing low, a trend line or a Fibonacci level.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The setup assumes a trend exists. It is an entry method inside a trend, not a way to find one.

**Reversals (weak).** A deep two-legged move can be the first part of a reversal. Telling a pullback from a reversal needs context from the higher timeframe.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, two legs down is just noise inside the range. Wait for the trend to prove itself.

**Volatility.** In very fast markets legs are large and messy, and stops must be wider.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | Clear higher highs and higher lows (or the reverse) |
| Two legs | Two separate pushes against the trend with a bounce between |
| Depth | Not beyond the start of the trend leg |
| Signal | A close in the trend direction after leg two |
| Level | Leg two ends near support or a retracement level |
| Stop | Beyond the end of leg C, where the idea is proven wrong |

## Common mistakes

- Entering at the end of leg A, before a second leg has formed.
- Forcing the pattern onto sideways price action.
- Ignoring depth: a pullback that wipes out most of the trend leg is probably a reversal.
- Insisting on exactly two legs. Complex pullbacks with three legs are common.
- Entering with no signal bar, just because price reached a level.
- Placing the stop inside the pullback, where normal noise will reach it.

## FAQ

**What is a two-legged pullback?**
It is a pause in a trend made of two moves against the trend with a small bounce in between. Traders watch for a signal at the end of the second move to enter in the direction of the main trend.

**Is a two-legged pullback the same as an ABC correction?**
They look alike. An ABC correction in Elliott theory has strict internal rules and is counted as waves. Price action traders use "ABC" loosely for any two-legged pause. This page uses the looser meaning.

**How deep should a pullback be?**
There is no fixed depth. Many traders look at 38% to 62% of the prior trend leg, but shallow and deep pullbacks both occur. If it takes back the whole trend leg, the trend is in doubt.

**Does the pattern always have two legs?**
No. Some pullbacks have one leg, and many have three or more. Two legs are common, which is why the name stuck, but forcing a count onto every pullback leads to errors.

**Does it have academic support?**
The idea is a trader-education concept rather than a tested academic result. There is no standard formal definition, so results depend on how each trader defines a leg. Test your own rules before relying on them.
