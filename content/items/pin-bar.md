A pin bar is a single bar with a long tail (wick) and a small body at one end. The tail shows price moved far in one direction and was rejected. Traders look for it at support, resistance or a swing and use the bar's extreme as the stop. It is a price action version of the hammer and shooting star.

## What is a pin bar?

A pin bar tells a short story. Price pushed hard in one direction during the bar, then reversed and closed near where it opened. A bullish pin bar has a long lower tail: sellers pushed price down, and buyers took it back. A bearish pin bar has a long upper tail.

The shape is the same as the hammer and the shooting star in Japanese candlestick analysis. See [Hammer](/hammer/) and [Shooting star](/shooting-star/). The name "pin bar" (from pinocchio, for the long nose) comes from modern price action trading. There is no single inventor, and no book source defines the bar with exact ratios, so the numbers below are rules of thumb used by traders.

Al Brooks (2012) discusses bars with long tails as signs of rejection, but treats them as one clue among many, not a standalone signal.

## How it is identified

A common checklist:

1. The tail is long: at least two to three times the length of the body.
2. The body is small and sits in one end of the bar. For a bullish pin bar, the body is in the upper third.
3. The nose (the short side) is small or nonexistent.
4. The tail is longer than the neighbouring bars' ranges, so it stands out.
5. The bar forms at a level: support, resistance or a swing.

**Worked example.** A bar opens at 105, falls to 100, and closes at 104. The range is 5, the body is 1 and the lower tail is 4. The tail is four times the body and the body sits in the top fifth of the range. If 100 is a prior support level, this is a bullish pin bar.

**Entry and stop.** A common method is to buy a break above the pin bar's high, 105 in the example, with a stop below its low, 100. The risk is 5 points, measured from the trigger. Some traders enter on a retrace into the tail, which gives a tighter stop but misses trades that never retrace.

## How to read it

**Location is everything.** A pin bar at support after a pullback in an uptrend is a very different signal from the same bar in the middle of a range. See [Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).

**Tail through the level.** A tail that pokes below a support level and closes back above it shows a rejection, similar to a [false breakout](/false-breakout/) or a [liquidity sweep](/liquidity-sweep/).

**With the trend.** In an uptrend, a bullish pin bar on a pullback is a continuation signal. Against the trend it is a weaker, counter-trend one.

**Size.** A pin bar much larger than the average bar (see [ATR](/atr/)) means a big stop. Very small ones may only be noise.

**Next bar.** The follow-through matters more than the bar itself. A pin bar followed by a bar that breaks its high is stronger than one that sits still.

## Best market states

**Reversals at a level (best).** The rejection of a prior swing or zone is the classic use.

**Pullbacks in a trend (good).** A pin bar at a rising support level or moving average can mark the end of a pullback.

**Ranges (weak).** Pin bars appear in the middle of ranges all the time and mean little there. At the range edge they are more useful.

**Volatile markets.** News spikes make long tails that are not rejection, only a rush of orders.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Shape | Tail 2 to 3 times the body, body in the outer third |
| Location | At support, resistance or a significant swing |
| Close | Closes back on the right side of the level |
| Trigger | The next bar breaks the pin bar's high (or low for bearish) |
| Trend | Agrees with the higher time frame, or the trade is treated as counter-trend |
| Stop | Beyond the tail, with room for spread |

## Common mistakes

- Trading every long-wick bar. Without a level, it is just a long wick.
- Entering at the close with a stop beyond a very long tail, which gives poor reward to risk.
- Ignoring the trend and taking counter-trend pin bars in strong moves.
- Counting a long tail that formed on a news spike as a rejection.
- Setting the stop at the exact tip of the tail without a margin for noise.
- Expecting a pin bar to always reverse. It is a clue, not a rule.

## FAQ

**What is a pin bar in trading?**
A pin bar is a bar with a long tail and a small body at one end. The tail shows price was pushed one way and rejected. It is most useful at a support or resistance level or a swing high or low.

**Is a pin bar the same as a hammer?**
The shape is the same. A hammer is the Japanese candlestick name for a long lower tail after a fall, and a shooting star is its upper-tail equivalent. "Pin bar" is the modern price action name and covers both directions.

**How long should the tail of a pin bar be?**
Many traders ask for a tail at least two to three times the body, and at least two thirds of the bar's range. These are rules of thumb. There is no standard, so test your own definition.

**Where should the stop go on a pin bar trade?**
Usually just beyond the tail tip: below it for a bullish pin bar and above it for a bearish one. Allow a little room for spread. A long tail means a wide stop, so size the position to match.

**Does the colour of a pin bar matter?**
Not much. A bullish pin bar can be a red or green candle. What counts is the long rejection tail and where the bar closed. A close in the upper part of the range is stronger than one near the middle.
