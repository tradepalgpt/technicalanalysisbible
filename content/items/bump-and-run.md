The bump and run reversal is a chart pattern in which a steady uptrend suddenly steepens into a sharp, speculative advance (the bump) and then breaks back down through the original trendline. The break below that trendline is the usual signal that the advance is over. It is mainly described for tops, and a mirror version exists for bottoms.

## What is the bump and run reversal?

The pattern has three parts. First comes the **lead-in**, a period of steady, moderate advance. A trendline drawn under it shows a gentle slope. Second comes the **bump**, a phase where price accelerates and rises much more steeply than the lead-in. This is often described as speculative buying. Third comes the **run**, the fall back through the lead-in trendline once the bump ends.

The idea is that a sudden surge unsupported by the earlier pace is hard to sustain. When it ends, price tends to give back the whole bump and move to the level where the surge began or below.

The pattern is credited to Thomas Bulkowski, who described it in his *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns*, though the exact year of first use is not firmly documented. Like most chart patterns, it has no strong academic evidence base. Its reliability depends on how strictly it is defined and the markets and periods tested.

## How it is identified

1. **Lead-in phase.** A steady uptrend, with a trendline drawn under the lows. The slope is moderate.
2. **Measure the lead-in height.** Find the distance from the lead-in trendline to the highs in that phase. The bump is expected to be a multiple of this height, often about twice as high.
3. **Bump phase.** Price rises well above the lead-in trendline. A second, steeper trendline can be drawn under the bump.
4. **Peak.** Price tops out at the end of the bump.
5. **Run.** Price falls and closes below the original lead-in trendline. This completes the pattern.

**Worked example.** During the lead-in, price rises along a trendline, and the highs sit about 5 points above that line. In the bump, price climbs to 12 points above the original trendline, which is more than twice the lead-in distance. The pattern then completes when price closes below the original trendline, say at 100.

**Measured target.** A common guide is that price falls back to the level where the bump began, which is roughly where the lead-in ended. If that was at 100, then the target is near that level or lower. This is a guide, not a promise.

## How to read it

**The bump matters.** Without a sharp acceleration well above the lead-in line, it is just a normal uptrend. The steeper and more extended the bump, the more it resembles the pattern.

**The break.** A close below the original lead-in trendline completes the pattern. A break of the steeper bump trendline is a warning but is not the signal.

**Retest.** Price often rallies back to the broken trendline and may fail there. Some traders prefer to enter on that retest.

**Volume.** Volume is often described as rising during the bump and fading near the peak. This is a general observation and not a rule.

**Failure.** If price breaks the lead-in line and then moves back above it, the pattern has failed.

**Bottom version.** The mirror image appears at the end of a downtrend, with a steep acceleration down and a break back up through the lead-in line.

## Best market states

**Reversal after steep advance (best).** The pattern needs a sharp run-up to be meaningful.

**Strong trends (caution).** Some strong trends include a steep leg that does not end in a reversal.

**Range (weak).** A sideways market has no bump to measure.

**Breakout.** A fast bump may be mistaken for a breakout. The pattern assumes it is a late, overextended move.

**Volatility.** Highly volatile markets can break the trendline many times, so the line may need to be drawn more loosely.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Lead-in | A steady trend with a clear trendline |
| Bump | Price well above the line, about twice the lead-in height or more |
| Peak | A visible top after the acceleration |
| Break | Close below the original lead-in trendline |
| Volume | Heavy during the bump, lower at the peak |
| Stop | Above the bump high |

## Common mistakes

- Calling every sharp rally a bump. Without a clear lead-in trendline and a break, it is only a rally.
- Drawing the trendline with hindsight, so it fits the break.
- Shorting at the peak instead of waiting for the lead-in trendline to break.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Ignoring the bigger trend. A pattern in a strong long-term uptrend may fail.
- Using it on thin, illiquid charts where a few trades create the bump.

## FAQ

**What is a bump and run reversal?**
It is a chart pattern in which a steady uptrend turns into a steep, speculative advance called the bump, and then price falls back through the original trendline. The break of that line is read as the end of the move.

**How do you draw the bump and run pattern?**
Draw a trendline under the lead-in phase, then watch for price to climb well above it. The pattern completes when price closes back below that original line. A second, steeper line under the bump is optional.

**How far does price fall after a bump and run?**
A common guide is a return to the level where the bump began, roughly where the lead-in ended. Many patterns fall short or go further, so treat this as a guide and not a prediction.

**Is there a bump and run bottom?**
Yes. The mirror version forms after a downtrend: a steady decline, then a sharp, steeper fall, then a break back up through the original trendline. It is less often discussed than the top version.

**Is the bump and run pattern reliable?**
There is no strong academic evidence for it. Its results depend on how strictly the lead-in, bump and break are defined. Test it on your own market and treat it as one signal among several.
