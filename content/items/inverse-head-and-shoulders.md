An inverse head and shoulders is a reversal chart pattern that forms at the end of a downtrend. It has three troughs: a left shoulder, a lower head and a higher right shoulder, joined by a neckline across the rallies between them. A close above the neckline is the usual signal that the downtrend has ended and a rise may follow.

## What is an inverse head and shoulders pattern?

An inverse head and shoulders shows sellers losing control. In a downtrend, price makes a low (the left shoulder), bounces, then falls to a lower low (the head). The next bounce is followed by a decline that stops at about the same level as the first low. That final decline fails to reach the head and turns up from a higher low (the right shoulder). The higher low is the first sign that the run of lower lows is over.

The line joining the two bounce highs is the neckline. While price stays below it, the pattern is only a possibility. A close above it completes the pattern.

The pattern is the upside-down version of the head and shoulders top, described in the early twentieth-century literature and made widely known by Edwards and Magee in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*. No single person is credited with it.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **A prior downtrend.** Without one, there is nothing to reverse.
2. **Left shoulder:** a low followed by a bounce.
3. **Head:** a lower low followed by a bounce to around the same area as the first.
4. **Right shoulder:** a low that is higher than the head and roughly level with the left shoulder.
5. **Neckline:** a line through the two bounce highs. It can slope up, down or be flat.
6. **Break:** a close above the neckline.

**Measured target.** Measure the vertical distance from the bottom of the head to the neckline. Add that distance to the breakout point.

**Worked example.** The head bottoms at 80 and the neckline above it is at 100: a height of 20. If price breaks the neckline at 101, the measured target is 101 + 20 = **121**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. Many patterns fall short, and some go further. The idea is explained more generally on the [Measured Move](/measured-move/) page.

## How to read it

**Neckline break.** A close above the neckline completes the pattern. Many traders wait for a close rather than an intrabar touch to avoid false breaks.

**Retest.** After the break, price often falls back to the neckline, which may now act as support (see [Role Reversal](/role-reversal/)). Some traders enter on that retest rather than on the break itself. Others miss the trade because price never comes back.

**Volume.** Edwards and Magee note that volume matters more on the upside than on a top. A rise in volume as price breaks the neckline is taken as a sign of real buying. A breakout on thin volume is less convincing.

**Neckline slope.** An upward-sloping neckline means buyers are already lifting the bounces, and is often read as the stronger version. A downward-sloping neckline is read as weaker.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks above the neckline and then closes back below the right shoulder, the pattern has failed. Failures can lead to sharp moves down.

## Best market states

**Reversals at the end of a long downtrend (best).** The pattern makes sense only when there is a meaningful decline to reverse. The longer the downtrend, the more weight the pattern carries.

**Strong downtrends (caution).** In a powerful decline, a "right shoulder" can be nothing more than a pause before another leg down.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways price action produces many bumps that can be forced into the shape. Without a prior trend the pattern means little.

**Volatility.** Very volatile markets produce untidy shoulders and frequent false neckline breaks. Wider stops are needed, and the risk per trade grows with them.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear downtrend before the left shoulder |
| Symmetry | Shoulders at roughly similar levels and widths |
| Higher low | Right shoulder clearly above the head |
| Break | Close above the neckline, not just a wick |
| Volume | Rising on the right side and strongest on the break |
| Stop | Below the right shoulder (conservative: below the head) |

## Common mistakes

- Buying before the neckline breaks. Until then it is only a possible pattern.
- Seeing the pattern in sideways markets where there is no downtrend to reverse.
- Ignoring the right shoulder's depth: if it is as low as the head, it is not an inverse head and shoulders.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Having no plan for a failed pattern. A close back below the right shoulder means the idea is wrong.
- Drawing the neckline through wicks and closes inconsistently, so the break point keeps moving.

## FAQ

**Is an inverse head and shoulders bullish or bearish?**
It is bullish. It forms at the end of a downtrend and suggests sellers are running out of steam. It is the mirror image of the head and shoulders top, which is bearish and forms at the end of an uptrend.

**How do you trade an inverse head and shoulders?**
Common approaches are to enter on a close above the neckline, or to wait for a retest of the neckline from above. The stop usually goes below the right shoulder. This is a description of method, not advice.

**Where is the target on an inverse head and shoulders?**
Measure from the bottom of the head up to the neckline, then add that distance to the point where price broke the neckline. Treat the result as a guide only, because many patterns fall short of it.

**Does the neckline have to be flat?**
No. It can slope up or down. A flat neckline is easiest to judge. A sloping one is still valid, but the measured target is usually taken vertically from the head to the neckline at the head's position.

**What makes the pattern fail?**
A close back below the right shoulder after the break means the buyers could not hold the move. Many traders treat that as the signal to exit.
