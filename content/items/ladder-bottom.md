A ladder bottom is a rare five-candle pattern at the end of a decline. Three bearish candles step down, a fourth shows a long upper shadow, and a bullish fifth candle gaps up. It describes selling that has run out of force. It is a bullish warning that needs confirmation.

## What is a ladder bottom?

The name describes the first three candles, which step down like the rungs of a ladder. The pattern looks like [three black crows](/three-black-crows/) at first, then changes character: the fourth candle shows buyers pushing back, and the fifth confirms it.

It needs five candles, with several specific features, so it is rare. Most traders know it as a name in the list of candlestick patterns, not as something they trade often.

## How it is identified

1. **Three consecutive bearish candles**, each closing lower than the last, each opening within the body of the previous one.
2. **A fourth bearish candle** with a noticeable upper shadow. Price rose during the period, but sellers pushed it back and it closed lower.
3. **A fifth bullish candle**, which opens above the body of the fourth candle (a gap up) and closes higher, ideally above the fourth candle's high.

Different books vary on details, such as how long the upper shadow must be. Some treat a close above the fourth candle's high as necessary and others do not.

**Worked example.** Candle 1 opens at 120 and closes at 116. Candle 2 opens at 118, closes at 112. Candle 3 opens at 114, closes at 108. Candle 4 opens at 110, rises to 112, and closes at 107 with an upper shadow. Candle 5 opens at 111 (above the fourth body) and closes at 114. The falling steps, the shadow on the fourth and the gap up fit a ladder bottom.

## How to read it

The first three candles show sustained selling. The fourth is the first hint of change: buyers get price up, even if sellers still win the close. The fifth candle completes the story by gapping up, which shows buyers now in control.

It is a bottoming warning, not a forecast. As with other patterns, the context matters: a decline into support, with a weakening momentum reading, makes the picture more convincing.

## Best market states

**Reversal (best).** After a long decline, particularly into a key level.

**Range (weak).** Five candles in a sideways market are almost always ordinary price movement.

**Trend (weak).** In a strong downtrend, a gap up can be filled quickly and the fall can resume.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear, sustained fall |
| Candles 1 to 3 | Falling closes, each opening inside the last body |
| Candle 4 | Bearish, with a visible upper shadow |
| Candle 5 | Bullish, gapping above the fourth body |
| Location | Near support or a prior low |

## Common mistakes

- Using the name for patterns that are not five candles.
- Ignoring the gap on the fifth candle.
- Treating it as a precise signal when definitions vary.
- Trading it in a sideways market.
- Skipping the need for follow-through after the fifth candle.

## FAQ

**Why is it called a ladder bottom?**
The first three bearish candles step downward like the rungs of a ladder. The "bottom" is the hoped-for end of that run. The name is only a description.

**How is it different from three black crows?**
Three black crows is three falling bearish candles after a rise, a warning of a top. A ladder bottom starts with a similar run of falling candles but comes after a decline, and adds a fourth candle showing buyers and a gap up on the fifth.

**Is there a bearish version?**
Not under this name. Bullish only is the standard description.

**Does it need the gap?**
In the classic description, yes. The gap shows a fast change in control. In 24-hour markets gaps are rare, so many traders use a looser rule.

**Is it reliable?**
It is rare, and its rules vary between sources, so there is little firm evidence on it. Use it only with other evidence and test it on your own data.
