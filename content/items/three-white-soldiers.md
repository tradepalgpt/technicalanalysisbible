Three white soldiers is a pattern of three long rising candles in a row, each opening inside the previous candle's body and closing near its high. It shows steady, determined buying. After a decline or a quiet stretch, it can mark the start of an advance.

## What are three white soldiers?

The name comes from a row of advancing soldiers. "White" refers to the rising candles, which are drawn hollow or white in traditional charts. On modern charts they are often green.

Nison describes the pattern in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*. Each day, buyers push price higher and hold the gains into the close. Three days in a row of that is a sign of persistent demand rather than a one-off bounce.

Unlike most reversal patterns, this one is a sign of strength already under way. By the time it finishes, price has moved a long way up, so the pattern confirms a move rather than predicting one. That makes it a confirmation tool more than an early entry signal.

## How it is identified

Three white soldiers has:

1. **Three consecutive rising candles**, each with a long body.
2. **Each close higher than the one before.**
3. **Each open inside the previous candle's body**, usually in its upper half. This keeps the climb orderly.
4. **Closes near the high of each candle**, so upper wicks are short.

Nison also points out two weaker versions. In an advance block, the three candles show shrinking bodies or growing upper wicks, a sign of fading strength. In stalled soldiers, the third candle is small, a sign that buyers are tiring.

**Worked example.** After a decline:
- Day one opens at 40.0, closes at 42.0 (high 42.2).
- Day two opens at 41.5, closes at 44.0 (high 44.2).
- Day three opens at 43.5, closes at 46.0 (high 46.1).

Each candle has a body of 2.0 to 2.5, opens within the previous body (41.5 sits inside 40.0 to 42.0, and 43.5 sits inside 41.5 to 44.0) and closes near its high. That is three white soldiers.

## How to read it

**Steady demand.** Long bodies and high closes show buyers winning each period, not just once.

**Where it starts.** After a decline or a base, three white soldiers can mark a change in control. It works as confirmation of a move already under way.

**Where it ends.** After a long advance, the same shape may show late buying near a peak. The pattern says what has happened, not what comes next.

**Warning signs.** Shrinking bodies, long upper wicks or a small third candle suggest buyers are fading. Nison's advance block and stalled variants cover these cases.

**Size of the move.** If the three candles have already covered a large distance, a stop placed below the first candle may be far away. Consider how much of the move is already behind you.

## Best market states

**Reversals (good).** At the end of a decline or a base, it can show a convincing change in direction.

**Trends (good).** Early in an advance it can confirm that buyers are in control. Later, it is less useful.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market three rising candles often mark a move to the top of the range and little more.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A decline, or a quiet base |
| Shape | Three long rising bodies, each higher, each opening in the previous body |
| Wicks | Short upper wicks; closes near the highs |
| Position | Not already far extended; check nearby resistance |
| Volume | Steady or rising volume across the three candles adds weight |

## Common mistakes

- Calling any three rising candles soldiers without checking body length and closes.
- Chasing the move after the third candle, when a large part of it is already complete.
- Ignoring nearby resistance that can stop the advance.
- Overlooking shrinking bodies or long upper wicks, which signal fading strength.
- Using it after a long rise, where it can show late buying.
- Treating it as a prediction instead of a confirmation of what has happened.

## FAQ

**Are three white soldiers bullish?**
Yes. Three long rising candles in a row show persistent buying. It is usually read as a bullish sign, especially after a decline. Because the move is already well under way by the third candle, it works more as confirmation than as an early signal.

**Why are they called "white"?**
In traditional Japanese charts, rising candles were drawn white or hollow. Many modern platforms colour them green instead, but the pattern name is unchanged.

**Can the pattern be a warning instead of a buy sign?**
Yes. After a long rise, three strong candles can reflect late buying near a peak. Shrinking bodies or long upper wicks suggest fading strength. Context decides how to read it.

**What is the opposite pattern?**
[Three Black Crows](/three-black-crows/), which is three long falling candles in a row. It shows steady selling, and is read as a bearish sign.

**Do the candles need to be exactly the same size?**
No, but they should all be long and reasonably similar. A clear shrinking in body size or a growing upper wick is a sign of weakening buying, which Nison treats as a weaker variation.
