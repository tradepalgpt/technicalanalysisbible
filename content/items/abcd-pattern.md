The AB=CD pattern is a four-point price pattern made of two equal-length moves, AB and CD, separated by a pullback, BC. The pullback usually retraces about 61.8% or 78.6% of AB. Traders watch point D, where CD matches AB, as a possible turning point to enter or take profit. It is a trader-education pattern with no academic evidence base.

## What is the AB=CD pattern?

The pattern describes a swing that repeats itself. Price makes a move from A to B, pulls back to C, then makes a second move from C to D that is about the same size as the first. Point D is the place where the pattern is "complete" and where traders look for the move to pause or turn.

It is the simplest pattern in the harmonic family and is often described as the building block of more complex ones such as the [Gartley](/gartley-pattern/) and other [harmonic patterns](/harmonic-patterns/). The idea is close to the [measured move](/measured-move/): a second leg often copies the length of the first.

Harmonic and Fibonacci ratio patterns are taught widely among traders, but there is no academic evidence that they predict price. Treat the pattern as a way to organise levels, not as a forecast.

## How it is identified

1. Find a clear first leg, A to B. Use confirmed [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/).
2. Find the pullback, B to C. In the common version it retraces 61.8% of AB. Some traders accept anything from 38.2% to 78.6%.
3. Project the second leg, C to D, so that CD equals AB in length.
4. Optionally check the time: some traders expect CD to take about as many bars as AB.
5. Look for signs of a pause at D, such as a candlestick reversal or a nearby level.

**Bullish version.** A is a high, B a low, C a lower retracement high, D a lower low. Traders look for a bounce at D.

**Bearish version.** The mirror image: A low, B high, C a higher retracement low, D a higher high. Traders look for a fall at D.

**Worked example (bullish).** A is at 100 and B at 80, so AB is 20 points. Price bounces to C. A 61.8% retracement of 20 is about 12.4, so C is near 92.4. Point D is C minus the length of AB: 92.4 - 20 = **72.4**. If price reaches about 72.4 and shows a turn, the pattern is complete.

Some traders also use an "extended" version where CD is 127.2% or 161.8% of AB. This is often used when the pullback BC was shallow. Using the [Fibonacci projection](/fibonacci-projection/) tool makes this quick to draw.

## How to read it

**Entry at D.** The pattern is read as a possible reversal zone. Entering the moment price touches D is risky, because price can run straight through. Many traders wait for a reversal candle or a break of a small trendline first.

**Profit targets.** A common approach is to take profit at 38.2% and then 61.8% of the whole A to D move, measured back from D. These are conventions, not guarantees.

**Invalidation.** If price closes well beyond D, the pattern has failed. The stop is usually just beyond D, which keeps risk small compared with the potential target. That small, clear stop is a main reason traders like the pattern.

**Continuation.** In a strong trend, price may keep going after D and begin a new AB=CD from the next swing. The pattern can repeat.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** The pattern is designed to find the end of a two-legged move. It makes most sense after a clear, tidy swing.

**Trends (good).** Inside a trend, a CD leg that matches AB can mark the end of a pullback or the end of an impulse leg.

**Ranges (weak).** In a range, many swings look similar in size, so you can find an AB=CD almost anywhere. That makes it unreliable as a signal.

**Volatility.** In fast markets the legs are rarely equal. Allow a zone rather than an exact price.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Clean swings | Obvious A, B, C, D points that others would also see |
| Ratios | BC retraces 61.8% to 78.6% of AB, CD close to equal to AB |
| Zone | Treat D as a zone, not one price |
| Confluence | D lines up with a known level or [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) |
| Reaction | A reversal candle or momentum [divergence](/divergence/) at D |
| Stop | Just beyond D |

## Common mistakes

- Drawing the pattern after the fact by choosing swing points that fit.
- Entering at D without any sign of a reaction.
- Demanding an exact price match when the pattern is better treated as a zone.
- Ignoring the bigger trend and trying to fade a strong move.
- Treating the Fibonacci ratios as laws of nature. They are conventions.
- Using it in sideways markets, where equal swings are common and meaningless.

## FAQ

**What does AB=CD mean?**
It means the second price leg, CD, is equal in length to the first leg, AB, with a pullback BC in between. The idea is that a swing tends to repeat its size. It is a rule of thumb used by traders, not a law.

**What Fibonacci ratios does the AB=CD pattern use?**
The classic version has BC retracing 61.8% of AB and CD equal to 100% of AB. Many traders allow BC anywhere from 38.2% to 78.6%, and some use CD extensions of 127.2% or 161.8% of AB.

**Is the AB=CD pattern reliable?**
There is no academic evidence that it predicts price. Results depend heavily on how swings are chosen, which is subjective. Use it to organise levels and manage risk, and test it on your own market before relying on it.

**How is AB=CD different from a measured move?**
They are very similar: both expect a second leg to match the first. The AB=CD pattern adds specific retracement ratios for the pullback and is usually drawn with Fibonacci tools, while a measured move is often a looser, price-only idea.

**Where do you put the stop on an AB=CD trade?**
Most traders put the stop just beyond point D, because a move past D shows the pattern has failed. This keeps the stop close, but it also means the stop can be hit by a brief overshoot.
