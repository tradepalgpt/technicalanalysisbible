Elliott Wave Theory says market prices move in repeating patterns of waves: five waves in the direction of the trend, followed by three corrective waves against it. Traders use it to estimate where a trend might be in its cycle. It relies heavily on judgment, and two analysts can label the same chart differently.

## What is Elliott Wave Theory?

Ralph Nelson Elliott, an American accountant, studied decades of stock market data and concluded that price moves in repeating wave patterns. He set out his ideas in *The Wave Principle* (1938). A. J. Frost and Robert Prechter brought them to a wide audience in *Elliott Wave Principle: Key to Market Behavior* (New Classics Library, 1978).

The basic structure is a cycle of eight waves. Five waves move with the main trend, labelled 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5. Waves 1, 3 and 5 are the pushes ("impulse" waves), and waves 2 and 4 are pullbacks. Then comes a correction of three waves, labelled A, B and C. Each wave is itself made of smaller waves of the same shape, so patterns repeat across scales.

The theory is also its own biggest problem. Wave counts are subjective. A wave that looks like wave 3 to one analyst can be wave C to another, and a count can be revised after the fact. Critics point out that this makes it hard to test. Supporters say the rules reduce the ambiguity. There is no agreed body of evidence that settles the argument, so treat it as a framework for thinking, not a forecasting method.

## How it is identified

**The impulse (five waves).** Wave 1 starts the move. Wave 2 retraces part of it. Wave 3 goes further and is often the strongest. Wave 4 pulls back again. Wave 5 makes the final push.

**The three core rules.** Elliott's guideline set includes three rules that cannot be broken. If one is broken, the count is wrong:

1. **Wave 2 never retraces more than 100 percent of wave 1.** It cannot go beyond the start of wave 1.
2. **Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5.** It may not be the longest, but it cannot be the shortest.
3. **Wave 4 does not overlap the price territory of wave 1.** In an up impulse, wave 4's low stays above wave 1's high. (Diagonal patterns are a recognised exception.)

**The correction (three waves).** A is the first move against the trend, B is a partial bounce, and C is the final move against the trend. Corrections take several forms, such as zigzags, flats and triangles. See [corrective waves](/corrective-waves/).

**Worked example.** Price rises from 100 to 110 (wave 1, 10 points). Wave 2 falls to 104 (a retrace of 6 of 10 points, 60 percent, so rule 1 holds). Wave 3 rises to 130 (26 points, the longest). Wave 4 falls to 120, above 110 (rule 3 holds). Wave 5 rises to 135 (15 points). Wave 3 at 26 is longer than wave 1 at 10 and wave 5 at 15, so rule 2 holds. The count is valid.

**Guidelines (not rules).** Wave 2 often retraces 50 to 61.8 percent of wave 1. Wave 3 is often 1.618 times wave 1. Wave 4 often retraces about 38.2 percent of wave 3. These come from Fibonacci ratios and are tendencies only. See [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) and [Fibonacci extension](/fibonacci-extension/).

## How to read it

**Where are we in the cycle?** The main use is context. Being in wave 3 suggests a strong move, while being in wave 5 suggests the trend may be close to its end.

**Entries.** Common entries are at the end of wave 2 or wave 4 pullbacks, in the direction of the larger trend.

**Targets.** Fibonacci ratios are used for wave targets, such as 1.618 times wave 1 for wave 3.

**Invalidation.** The rules give a clear invalidation level. If price goes beyond the start of wave 1 during wave 2, the count is void. This gives a natural stop.

**Alternate counts.** Experienced users keep an alternative count in mind and switch if price breaks a rule.

## Best market states

**Trends (good).** Wave counts are most visible when a trend has clear pushes and pullbacks.

**Reversals.** The theory claims to mark turns, such as the end of wave 5. It is also where it is most often wrong, since many five-wave moves continue as extended fifth waves.

**Ranges (weak).** In sideways markets, counts are ambiguous and corrections run long. Triangles and flats can be hard to tell apart.

**Volatility.** In fast markets, small waves are lost within large candles and the count becomes guesswork.

**Time frames.** The pattern is said to apply to all time frames, but this too is hard to test.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Rule 1 | Wave 2 does not go beyond the start of wave 1 |
| Rule 2 | Wave 3 is not the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5 |
| Rule 3 | Wave 4 does not overlap wave 1 (except in diagonals) |
| Alternation | Waves 2 and 4 differ in form, for example one sharp and one sideways |
| Fibonacci | Retracements and extensions near common ratios |
| Momentum | Wave 3 often has the strongest momentum; wave 5 may show divergence |

## Common mistakes

- Forcing a count to fit the chart and ignoring a broken rule.
- Having only one count and refusing to change it.
- Treating Fibonacci guidelines as rules.
- Labelling waves in real time with certainty. Counts are often clear only after the fact.
- Using it without a stop or a point where the count is wrong.
- Mixing time frames, so waves from one degree are labelled as another.

## FAQ

**What is Elliott Wave Theory?**
It is a theory that price moves in repeating wave patterns: five waves with the trend, then three against it. Ralph Nelson Elliott introduced it in 1938, and Frost and Prechter popularised it in 1978. It depends a lot on the analyst's judgment.

**What are the three rules of Elliott Wave?**
Wave 2 never retraces more than 100 percent of wave 1. Wave 3 is never the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5. Wave 4 does not overlap the price territory of wave 1, except in diagonal patterns. Break any, and the count is wrong.

**Is Elliott Wave reliable?**
There is no solid, agreed evidence that it forecasts better than chance, and counts are subjective. Some traders find it a useful framework, others reject it. Treat it as context, and use stops that do not rely on the count being right.

**How many waves are in an Elliott Wave cycle?**
Eight in the basic cycle: five impulse waves (1 to 5) in the direction of the trend, then three corrective waves (A, B, C) against it. Each wave is made up of smaller waves of the same type.

**What is the difference between Elliott Wave and Dow Theory?**
Dow Theory deals with trends and confirmation between two averages, and has no wave counts. Elliott Wave gives a detailed wave structure and Fibonacci relationships. Dow Theory is simpler. Elliott Wave tries to say more and is much more subjective.
