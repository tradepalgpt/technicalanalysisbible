An order block is a price zone, usually the last opposite-coloured candle before a strong, structure-breaking move. Traders who use the idea treat that zone as a place where large orders may have been placed, and watch for price to return to it. It is a trader-education concept, not a tested law of markets.

## What is an order block?

The term comes from a style of trading education often called ICT (Inner Circle Trader) or Smart Money Concepts. The idea is that big participants cannot fill a large order at once, so they build positions in a zone. When price leaves that zone with force, the zone is marked. If price later returns, the theory says unfilled orders may react again.

That explanation is a model, not something traders can see on a chart. Nobody outside the market can confirm who placed orders where. A fairer way to describe it is this: an order block marks the last point of balance before a sharp, one-sided move. Price sometimes pauses or turns there on a return. Sometimes it does not.

Order blocks are close to classic supply and demand zones. The main difference is that an order block is usually drawn from a single candle, while a supply or demand zone is often a small base of several candles. The [comparison guide](/compare/order-block-vs-supply-and-demand/) covers the differences.

## How it is identified

A common rule set:

1. **Find a strong move.** Look for a run of large candles in one direction that breaks a recent swing high or low. This is a [break of structure](/break-of-structure/).
2. **Mark the last opposite candle.** For a bullish order block, mark the last down candle before the up move. For a bearish one, the last up candle before the down move.
3. **Draw the zone.** Some traders use the full candle from high to low. Others use only the body, from open to close.
4. **Wait for a return.** Price must come back into the zone. The zone is more useful if it has not already been revisited.

**Worked example.** Price trades sideways near 100. One down candle closes at 100 (high 101, low 99). The next three candles rally hard to 108 and break the previous swing high at 105. The bullish order block is the down candle: 99 to 101 using the full range, or 100 to 101 using the body. If price falls back to 101, a trader using this idea might look for a buy setup with a stop below 99. The distance risked is 2 points (101 minus 99).

## How to read it

**Strength of the move.** A zone that launched a very strong move with large candles is generally taken more seriously than one that led to a slow drift.

**Structure break.** A move that breaks structure suggests the zone sits at a real turning point. A move that stays within a range does not give the same weight.

**First return.** Many traders only trust the first retest. After price has traded through a zone, it is considered used.

**Closing through it.** If price closes beyond the far side of the zone, the order block has failed. At that point it may turn into a [breaker block](/breaker-block/), a related idea where a broken zone is expected to flip sides.

**Higher time frames.** Zones on daily or four-hour charts are treated as more significant than zones on a one-minute chart, simply because more price action built them.

## Best market states

**Reversals (good).** A zone left behind by a sharp turn at a major level, with a break of structure, is the classic setup.

**Trend pullbacks (good).** In an uptrend, a bullish zone below price can give a pullback area to watch. See [pullback trading](/pullback-trading/).

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market almost every candle can be called an order block, so the label stops meaning anything.

**Volatility.** In fast markets zones are wide and price often slices through them. Stops need to allow for that, or the zone is skipped.

**Honest limits.** There is no published, peer-reviewed evidence that order blocks beat simple support and resistance. Treat results claimed online with care, and test the idea on your own data.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | Strong, one-directional, with larger-than-average candles |
| Structure | A swing high or low was broken by the move |
| Fresh zone | Price has not already traded back into it |
| Location | Fits with the trend on a higher time frame |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or a small break of structure on a lower time frame |
| Stop | Beyond the far side of the zone |

## Common mistakes

- Marking every opposite candle as an order block. The zone only matters if a real structure break followed it.
- Treating the zone as a certain turning point. Price often trades straight through.
- Using zones that have already been tested several times.
- Mixing body-only and full-range zones from trade to trade, so results cannot be compared.
- Ignoring the higher time frame trend and trying to buy zones in a strong downtrend.
- Taking the story about "banks" and "smart money" as proven fact. It is an explanation, not evidence.

## FAQ

**What is an order block in trading?**
It is a price zone, usually the last opposite-coloured candle before a strong move that breaks structure. Traders watch for price to return to it and react. The idea comes from ICT and Smart Money Concepts teaching and is not a proven market law.

**How do you find a bullish order block?**
Find an upward move that breaks a recent swing high. Then mark the last down candle before that move began. The zone runs across that candle's range or its body, depending on the rule you choose.

**Are order blocks the same as supply and demand zones?**
They are very similar. Both mark areas where price left sharply. Order blocks are usually a single candle, while supply and demand zones are often a base of several candles. Many traders use the terms loosely.

**Do order blocks work?**
Sometimes price reacts at them, and sometimes it ignores them. No reliable public research shows an edge over plain support and resistance. Test them on your own market and time frame before trusting them.

**Where does the stop go on an order block trade?**
Most traders place it just beyond the far side of the zone, since a close through it means the idea has failed. A wide zone means a wide stop, so position size should be smaller.
