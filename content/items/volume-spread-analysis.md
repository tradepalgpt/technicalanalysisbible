Volume Spread Analysis (VSA) is a method of reading each price bar by comparing its volume, its spread (high minus low) and where it closes. The aim is to judge whether professional buying or selling is behind a move. It builds on the Wyckoff method, and is used mainly to confirm reversals and breakouts.

## What is Volume Spread Analysis?

VSA looks at three things on every bar:

- **Spread:** the distance from the bar's high to its low (its range).
- **Close:** where the bar closes within that range (top, middle or bottom).
- **Volume:** how much traded, compared with recent bars (high, average or low).

The core idea is that volume and price should tell a consistent story. A wide up bar on high volume looks like genuine demand. A wide up bar on very low volume looks suspicious, because few participants were involved. The method reads these agreements and disagreements to guess what larger, better-informed traders ("smart money" in VSA language) are doing.

The approach was developed by Tom Williams, a former trader and later trainer, who built on Richard Wyckoff's early 20th-century work. The method is mostly taught by trading educators rather than tested in academic studies, so claims about its accuracy lack independent evidence. Treat it as a structured way of reading charts, not a proven system.

## How it is identified

There is no formula. Each bar is classed by three inputs, then compared with context. Some commonly taught signals:

| Signal | Typical description | What it is read to suggest |
|---|---|---|
| No demand | Narrow-spread up bar, low volume, in an uptrend | Buyers are absent; weakness may follow |
| No supply | Narrow-spread down bar, low volume, in a downtrend | Sellers are absent; strength may follow |
| Stopping volume | Very high volume, bar closes off the lows after a fall | Large buyers may be absorbing supply |
| Upthrust | Price pushes above recent highs on high volume, then closes near the low | Buying failed; a trap for late buyers |
| Test | Price returns to an earlier low on low volume | Supply seems to have dried up |
| Effort versus result | High volume with little price movement | Opposing forces are balancing; change may come |

**Worked example.** Over the last 20 bars average volume is 10,000. A bar rallies to a new high but its spread is narrow, only 20% of the usual range, and volume is 4,000 (0.4 times average) in an established uptrend. VSA reads this as **no demand**: price crept up without participation. A trader following VSA would treat it as a warning, not a trade on its own, and wait for further bars to confirm weakness.

Because there are many named signals and the definitions vary between authors, results depend on the reader's judgement. Some software tries to automate the labels, but the rules differ from one product to another.

## How to read it

**Effort versus result.** The main rule: volume is the effort, price change is the result. If effort is large and the result small, something is absorbing the move. If the result is large with little effort, the move may be easy but fragile.

**Context first.** The same bar means different things at different places. A high-volume down bar after a long decline and one in the first leg of a new decline are not the same.

**Sequences matter.** Signals are read as groups of bars. A few no-demand bars after a rally, followed by a high-volume bar that fails to rise, tell a stronger story than any one bar.

**Close location.** A heavy bar that closes near its high is read as buying, and near its low as selling, though a wide bar closing mid-range is read as a battle.

**Higher time frame.** Many VSA teachers read the larger trend first, then look for signals in the direction of the bigger picture.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** VSA was built to spot absorption at the end of trends: stopping volume, climaxes, tests and upthrusts. It fits well alongside [Volume Climax](/volume-climax/) and [Wyckoff accumulation](/wyckoff-accumulation/).

**Breakouts (good).** Volume checks help decide whether a breakout bar has real support or is thin and likely to fail.

**Trends (mixed).** Reading continuation signals is possible but less common in teaching.

**Ranges (weak).** In quiet ranges, volume is low on all bars and signals are ambiguous.

**Markets without true volume.** In spot forex, VSA must use tick counts (see [Tick Volume](/tick-volume/)), which weakens the reading.

## Confirmation rules

- Read the trend and key levels first.
- Wait for the signal bar to finish; judge the close, not the bar mid-way.
- Look for a follow-up: the next bars should agree (for example, a low-volume test after a stopping-volume bar).
- Combine with structure: signals at support or resistance carry more weight than in empty space.
- Define each signal numerically for your own use (for example, "high volume = above 1.5 times the 20-bar average") so that your decisions are consistent.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a single bar as a trading signal.
- Ignoring context, so the same bar is read the same way in all situations.
- Using inconsistent definitions of "high" and "low" volume from chart to chart.
- Using VSA on markets with unreliable volume without noting the limits.
- Assuming "smart money" intent can be known from a bar. It can only be inferred, and the inference may be wrong.
- Reading meaning into every bar, which leads to over-analysis.

## FAQ

**What does VSA stand for?**
Volume Spread Analysis. It reads the volume, the spread (range) and the close of each bar together to judge whether larger participants are buying or selling.

**Is VSA the same as Wyckoff?**
No, but VSA grew out of Wyckoff's ideas. Wyckoff describes market phases and the roles of composite operators. VSA focuses on reading individual bars and short sequences using volume and spread.

**Does VSA work without real volume?**
It is less reliable. Spot forex only has tick counts, which approximate activity. VSA signals are better suited to stocks and futures where real volume is reported.

**Is there evidence that VSA works?**
Mostly anecdotal. It is taught by trading educators, and independent academic testing is limited. Judge it by testing your own rules on past data before relying on them.

**What is a no-demand bar?**
A narrow-spread up bar on low volume in an uptrend. VSA reads it as a lack of buying interest, which may come before a fall. It needs confirming by later bars.
