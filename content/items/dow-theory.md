Dow Theory is an early body of trend ideas drawn from Charles Dow's newspaper editorials in the late 1800s and early 1900s. Its core claim is that markets move in trends, that a trend should be confirmed by a second market average, and that a trend lasts until the evidence shows it has ended.

## What is Dow Theory?

Charles Dow, co-founder of Dow Jones and Company and the *Wall Street Journal*, wrote a series of editorials in the late 1800s and early 1900s about how stock prices moved. He did not publish a single "theory". After his death in 1902, his ideas were collected and extended by William Peter Hamilton, who wrote about them in the *Wall Street Journal* and in *The Stock Market Barometer* (1922). Robert Rhea then set them out in order in *The Dow Theory* (1932).

Dow's two averages were the industrials and the rails. Companies that made goods should also be shipping them, so a trend in one should show up in the other. That is the idea behind confirmation.

The theory is the root of much modern technical analysis, including trend lines, support and resistance and the use of higher highs and higher lows. It is also old. The averages, the market and the speed of trading have all changed, and the theory has to be applied with care today.

## How it is identified

The main tenets, as usually listed:

1. **The averages discount everything.** Price reflects all available information.
2. **Markets have three trends.** The primary trend lasts a year or more. Secondary trends, corrections within it, last weeks to a few months. Minor trends last days to weeks.
3. **Primary trends have three phases.** Accumulation, a public participation phase and a distribution phase.
4. **The averages must confirm each other.** A signal in one average counts only if the other agrees.
5. **Volume confirms the trend.** Volume should rise in the direction of the trend.
6. **A trend persists until a clear reversal signal appears.**

**Worked example.** Industrials make a new high at 120, then pull back to 114, then rise to 125. Rails make a high at 60, pull back to 56, then rise to 62. Both averages have made higher highs and higher lows, so both confirm an uptrend. Later, industrials rise to 130 but rails fail to exceed 62 and fall below 56. This is a non-confirmation. Dow followers would treat the uptrend as in doubt until both averages agree again.

The numbers are made up for illustration.

## How to read it

**Higher highs and higher lows.** An uptrend is a sequence of rising peaks and rising troughs. A downtrend is the reverse. This is the root of modern [market structure](/market-structure/).

**Confirmation.** A new high in one average without the other is a warning, not a sell signal. Today, traders apply the idea to related indices or sectors.

**Secondary corrections.** In a bull market, pullbacks often retrace about one third to two thirds of the prior advance. This is a rule of thumb, not a fixed law.

**Lines.** Dow described a "line": a sideways band of two to three weeks or more, in which the averages move within about 5 percent. A break from the line suggests the direction of the next move.

**Reversal.** The trend changes when the pattern of highs and lows breaks, and the other average confirms it.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The theory was built to identify the primary trend and keep you aligned with it.

**Reversals.** It is slow to call turns. A reversal is confirmed only after the sequence of highs and lows is broken, so a good part of the move has already happened.

**Ranges (weak).** Dow Theory gives little guidance in sideways markets, and the "line" concept is only a partial answer.

**Volatility.** Sharp news-driven moves, which are common now, can produce false signals or leave the averages out of step for short periods.

**Modern use.** Most traders now treat it as a framework for thinking about trend, not as a mechanical system. Evidence on how well the original rules perform on modern markets is mixed, and no single result can be quoted here.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Trend sequence | Higher highs and higher lows (or the reverse for a downtrend) |
| Confirmation | A second related index or average agrees |
| Volume | Rises with the trend and dips on pullbacks |
| Secondary moves | Corrections that stay within the broader trend |
| Reversal | A broken sequence of highs and lows, confirmed by the second average |
| Patience | No signal until the trend has clearly ended |

## Common mistakes

- Treating Dow Theory as a precise trading system when it is a framework.
- Expecting early signals. It lags by design.
- Ignoring the confirmation tenet and acting on one average alone.
- Applying rules about the 1930s industrials and rails to markets that have since changed.
- Calling every pullback a reversal.
- Quoting rules of thumb, such as retracement percentages, as if they were exact.

## FAQ

**What is Dow Theory?**
It is a set of ideas about market trends, drawn from Charles Dow's editorials in the late 1800s and formalised by William Hamilton and Robert Rhea. It says markets trend, that trends need confirmation from a second average, and that they continue until a clear reversal.

**Who created Dow Theory?**
Charles Dow laid the groundwork in his *Wall Street Journal* editorials. William Hamilton extended the ideas after Dow died in 1902, and Robert Rhea set them out in *The Dow Theory* in 1932. Dow himself never published it as one theory.

**What are the three trends in Dow Theory?**
The primary trend lasts a year or more. The secondary trend is a correction against it, lasting weeks to a few months. The minor trend lasts days to weeks. Most traders still use the idea of three nested time frames.

**What is confirmation in Dow Theory?**
A trend signal in one average, such as the industrials, should be matched by the other, the transports. If one makes a new high and the other does not, the trend is in doubt. Modern traders apply the idea to related indices.

**Does Dow Theory still work today?**
Its core ideas, such as trends and higher highs and lows, remain in use. As a strict rule set on the original averages, it is slow and less certain. Use it as a framework for reading the trend, and test any rules you build from it.
