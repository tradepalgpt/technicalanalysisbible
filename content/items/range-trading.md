Range trading is a method for markets moving sideways between a floor (support) and a ceiling (resistance). The trader buys near the bottom of the range and sells near the top, with a stop just outside the range. It works while the range holds and loses when price breaks out.

| | |
|---|---|
| Type | Price action method |
| Best market states | Range (sideways) |
| Weak in | Trends and breakouts |
| Best uses | Entry near the edges, exit at the opposite edge |
| Key tools | Support and resistance, oscillators, ADX as a range check |
| Typical stop | Just beyond the range edge |
| Natural opposite | [Breakout trading](/breakout-trading/) |

## What is range trading?

Markets spend a lot of time not trending. Price bounces between a level where buyers keep stepping in and a level where sellers keep stepping in. Range trading takes advantage of that pattern. Each touch of the floor is a chance to buy, and each touch of the ceiling is a chance to sell.

No single person invented it. It follows directly from the idea of [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/), which is described in most technical analysis textbooks.

The risk is built into the method. A range does not last forever. When price finally breaks out, a range trader who keeps buying at "support" takes a loss that can be large compared with the small profits made inside the range. The stop is not optional.

## How it is identified

A range is usually accepted when:

1. **Two or more touches of each edge.** At least two highs near the same level and two lows near the same level.
2. **A flat shape.** The edges are roughly horizontal, not sloping like a [trend channel](/trend-channel/).
3. **A low trend reading.** ADX below about 20 to 25 is a common sign of a market without a strong trend.

**Worked example.** Price has bounced between 100 and 110 four times. A trader buys at 101 with a stop at 98 (3 points of risk) and a target near the top at 109, so the reward is 8 points. The reward to risk ratio is 8 divided by 3, about 2.7 to 1. If price instead closes at 97, the stop is hit, the range is likely broken and the trader steps aside.

Many traders also mark the midpoint (105 here) and avoid entries there, since both edges are far away.

## How to read it

**Edge reactions.** A rejection candle at the edge, such as a [pin bar](/pin-bar/), shows the level is holding.

**Oscillators.** Readings such as RSI below 30 near support, or above 70 near resistance, support the idea that the edge is being defended. See [overbought and oversold](/overbought-oversold/).

**Narrowing or widening.** If the highs fall and the lows rise, the range is compressing, which often ends in a breakout. If the range is widening, the market may be becoming more volatile and less suited to this method.

**Volume.** Falling volume inside a range is normal. A sudden volume rise at an edge deserves attention, since it may be the start of a break.

**Failed edges.** A [false breakout](/false-breakout/) that pushes beyond the edge and then closes back inside is a popular range entry, because it traps breakout traders.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** This is the whole point of the method. Clear horizontal edges and a flat ADX are ideal.

**Trends (weak).** Fading a trend by selling every "resistance" is the main way range traders lose money.

**Breakouts (weak).** The moment price closes decisively outside, the method stops working. The trade should be closed rather than averaged down.

**Volatility.** Very low volatility ranges give small profits that spreads and fees can eat. Very high volatility ranges give wild swings that hit stops from both sides.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Edges | At least two clear touches at each side |
| Trend filter | ADX low or flat; no strong slope in moving averages |
| Reaction | Rejection candle or oscillator turn at the edge |
| Room | Enough distance to the opposite edge for a worthwhile reward |
| Stop | Beyond the edge, so a close outside means the range failed |
| Higher time frame | Not about to hit a major level that could trigger a breakout |

## Common mistakes

- Treating two random turning points as a range and trading before it is proven.
- Trading in the middle of the range, where there is no edge.
- Moving the stop further out when price pushes through the edge.
- Using range rules in a strong trend, buying every dip in a falling market.
- Ignoring the cost of spread and fees on small range moves.
- Not having a plan for the breakout, which will come eventually.

## FAQ

**What is range trading?**
Range trading means buying near support and selling near resistance while a market moves sideways. The stop goes just outside the range. It makes small, repeated gains when the range holds, and takes a loss when price breaks out.

**How do you know a market is ranging?**
Look for at least two touches of both a floor and a ceiling, with flat edges. A low ADX reading supports the view. If price starts closing beyond an edge, the market may have stopped ranging.

**What is the best indicator for range trading?**
There is no single best one. Oscillators such as RSI and the Stochastic are popular because they show overbought and oversold readings at the edges. They work better alongside clear support and resistance than on their own.

**Where should the stop loss go in range trading?**
Usually just beyond the range edge, so a close outside shows the range has failed. Leave a small buffer for wicks and spread. Never move the stop further away to give a losing trade more room.

**What is the difference between range trading and mean reversion?**
Range trading uses fixed edges, support and resistance. [Mean reversion](/mean-reversion/) assumes price returns to an average and does not need a flat range. Range trading can be seen as one version of mean reversion.
