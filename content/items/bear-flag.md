A bear flag is a short continuation pattern in a downtrend. A sharp fall (the flagpole) is followed by a brief, shallow drift upward inside parallel lines (the flag). A close below the bottom of the flag suggests the decline may resume.

## What is a bear flag pattern?

A bear flag is a pause inside a fast fall. Price drops in a steep, near-vertical move: the flagpole. Sellers then take a breather and some shorts cover, so price drifts up or sideways in a narrow, tilted channel: the flag. The channel is bounded by two roughly parallel lines. When sellers return and price closes below the lower line, the pattern is complete.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, describe flags as short formations that appear during rapid moves, with the flag sloping against the prevailing trend. In a downtrend, that means the flag slopes up. Flags form quickly, usually within a few weeks on daily charts, and one that lasts too long loses its meaning.

A bear flag is similar to a [pennant](/pennant/), except that the pennant's lines converge while the flag's stay parallel.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **Flagpole:** a sharp, steep fall, usually on heavy volume.
2. **Flag:** a short consolidation after the pole, drifting slightly up (or sideways), between two roughly parallel lines.
3. **Lower line:** drawn through the flag's lows. The upper line runs through its highs.
4. **Breakdown:** a close below the lower line.

The flag should be short compared with the pole and should retrace only a modest part of it. A large retracement suggests the pole was not a strong move.

**Measured target.** Measure the length of the flagpole, from the start of the sharp fall to its bottom. Subtract that distance from the breakdown point. Flags are often called half-mast patterns because they tend to form around the middle of a fast move, which is where this measuring idea comes from.

**Worked example.** The pole runs from 60 down to 50: a length of 10. The flag drifts up to 53 and then price closes below the lower line at 51. The measured target is 51 - 10 = **41**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Breakdown.** A close below the lower line completes the pattern. Many traders wait for a close rather than a brief wick to reduce false breaks.

**Volume.** The usual picture is heavy volume on the pole and lighter volume through the flag. Light volume during the bounce suggests buyers are not committed.

**Depth of the flag.** A shallow bounce in the lower part of the pole is generally read as stronger than a deep one.

**Retest.** Price sometimes rallies back to the broken lower line, which may now act as resistance. Some traders enter on that retest.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks above the upper line of the flag instead, the continuation idea has failed. Short-lived bounces in a falling market can also be violent, so stops need room.

## Best market states

**Strong, fast downtrends (best).** The pattern needs a sharp pole. It is a pause in momentum, not a top.

**Breakdowns.** The flag line gives a clear trigger level.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market there is no pole, so a drifting channel is just a range.

**Slow trends (weak).** A gentle decline with small bounces does not produce a convincing pole.

**Volatility.** In very volatile markets the flag lines are wide, and stop distances grow with them.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Pole | Sharp, steep fall, usually on strong volume |
| Flag | Short, shallow and slightly upward or sideways |
| Lines | Roughly parallel |
| Volume | Light inside the flag, rising on the breakdown |
| Break | Close below the lower line, not just a wick |
| Stop | Above the flag's high (tighter: above the upper line) |

## Common mistakes

- Calling any small bounce a flag without a clear, sharp pole.
- Letting the flag run too long. A long consolidation is no longer a flag.
- Accepting a bounce that retraces most of the pole, which suggests a reversal.
- Entering inside the flag before the breakdown.
- Treating the pole-length target as certain.
- Ignoring the higher-timeframe trend.

## FAQ

**Is a bear flag bullish or bearish?**
It is bearish. It forms after a sharp fall, and a close below the flag suggests the decline may continue. It is a bias, not a certainty.

**How do you calculate the bear flag target?**
Measure the flagpole, from the start of the sharp fall to its bottom, and subtract that distance from the breakdown point. This is a guide only, and many flags fall short.

**What is the difference between a bear flag and a bull flag?**
They are mirror images. A bull flag follows a sharp rise and tilts downward. A bear flag follows a sharp fall and tilts upward. Each is a pause that may precede a continuation.

**Does a bear flag always slope up?**
Usually it tilts against the trend, so it slopes up, but a flat or very slightly downward flag can also appear. A flag that slopes steeply down is just more of the decline, and is not a pause.

**Where does the stop usually go?**
Many traders place it above the highest point of the flag, where the pause would clearly have failed. A tighter stop just above the upper line risks less but is hit more easily.
