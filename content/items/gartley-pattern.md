The Gartley pattern is a four-leg reversal shape drawn with five points, X, A, B, C and D, where each leg is measured against the last using Fibonacci ratios. The bullish version ends at point D, a pullback of about 78.6% of the first leg. Traders look for a turn there. It is a trader-education concept with no academic evidence base.

## What is the Gartley pattern?

H. M. Gartley described a pattern in his 1935 book *Profits in the Stock Market*. His version was drawn from swings and judged by eye. It is often called the "222" because it appears on page 222 of that book.

Later writers, notably Larry Pesavento and Scott Carney, attached precise Fibonacci ratios to the legs. That modern, ratio-based version is what most platforms draw today, and it is what this page describes.

The idea is simple. After a move from X to A, price pulls back to B, bounces to C, then falls to D, a deeper pullback of the original move. If D lands in the expected zone, some traders treat it as a place where the larger trend may resume. There is no academic evidence that these ratios predict turning points better than chance.

## How it is drawn

A bullish Gartley uses five swing points:

1. **X to A:** an up-leg. This is the reference move.
2. **A to B:** a pullback that retraces about 61.8% of XA.
3. **B to C:** a bounce that retraces 38.2% to 88.6% of AB.
4. **C to D:** a fall that completes the pattern. Point D sits at about 78.6% of XA and must stay above X.
5. **Bearish version:** the same shape flipped upside down.

Authors differ on tolerance. Some accept B anywhere from about 0.55 to 0.68. Some also check that CD is 1.272 to 1.618 times BC. Platforms differ in which ranges they accept.

**Worked example.** X is at 100 and A is at 150, so XA is 50. B retraces 61.8%, giving 150 - 30.9 = 119.1, call it 119. D is at the 78.6% retracement of XA: 150 - 39.3 = 110.7, call it 111. A stop sits just below X, say 99. Entry near 111 risks about 12 points. A first target is often the C level or the A to D retracement area.

## How to read it

**Completion at D.** The pattern is only possible until price reaches D. Treat D as a zone, not an exact tick.

**Reaction.** Traders look for a sign of buying near D, such as a rejection candle, before acting. Entering on the touch alone is a guess.

**Invalidation.** A close below X means the pattern has failed. That is why the stop sits beyond X.

**Targets.** Common targets are the 38.2% and 61.8% retracements of the move from A to D. These are conventions, not tested edges.

**Subjectivity.** Different traders pick different swing points on the same chart. Two people can disagree on whether a Gartley exists. Software that scans for the pattern applies fixed tolerances.

## Best market states

**Reversals after a clear swing (best).** The pattern needs a defined X to A leg. Clean, well-spaced swings make the ratios easier to measure.

**Pullbacks in a trend (moderate).** A bullish Gartley is really a deep pullback inside the larger move, so it can pair with trend-following.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, almost any set of swings can be bent into the pattern.

**Breakouts and high volatility (weak).** Fast moves run through D without pausing.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|

| Order | D stays inside X to A; it does not pass X |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or momentum shift near D |
| Level | D lines up with older support or resistance |
| Stop | Just beyond X |
| Timeframe | Higher timeframes give fewer but cleaner swings |

## Common mistakes

- Forcing the ratios. A "close enough" fit that needs wide tolerances is usually a pattern found after the fact.
- Entering before D is complete, then watching price run through.
- Choosing swing points that make the pattern fit, rather than the obvious ones.
- Ignoring the larger trend. A bullish Gartley against a strong downtrend is a catch of a falling knife.
- Treating it as proven. It has no academic evidence base.
- Using a stop that is too tight to survive normal noise around D.

## FAQ

**Why is it called the Gartley 222?**
The original pattern appears on page 222 of H. M. Gartley's 1935 book *Profits in the Stock Market*. The name stuck, and later authors reused it when they added Fibonacci ratios to the layout.

**What are the Gartley ratios?**
In the modern version, point B retraces about 61.8% of the XA leg and point D completes at about 78.6% of XA. Authors and platforms use slightly different tolerances, so check the settings of the tool you use.

**Is the Gartley pattern reliable?**
There is no academic evidence that it predicts reversals better than chance. Studies of Fibonacci-based tools are limited. Treat it as one way of marking a possible turning area, and judge it with trend, levels and risk control.

**Where does the stop go on a Gartley?**
Most traders place it just beyond point X. A move past X breaks the pattern's rules, so the idea is wrong. The distance from D to X sets the risk for the trade.

**What is the difference between a Gartley and a bat pattern?**
Both are five-point harmonic shapes. The Gartley ends at the 0.786 retracement of XA. The bat has a shallower B and ends at the 0.886 retracement. The ratios are the main difference.
