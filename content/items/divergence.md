Divergence happens when price and a momentum indicator disagree. In bearish divergence, price makes a higher high but the indicator makes a lower high. In bullish divergence, price makes a lower low but the indicator makes a higher low. It suggests the move is losing strength and may turn, but it does not say when.

## What is divergence?

Price and momentum normally move together. When price makes a new high, a momentum oscillator usually makes a new high too. Divergence is the exception: price pushes further, but the oscillator does not follow. The move is being made on weaker and weaker momentum.

There are two common kinds, called "regular" or "classic" divergence:

- **Bearish divergence.** Price makes a higher high. The oscillator makes a lower high. Seen at the end of uptrends.
- **Bullish divergence.** Price makes a lower low. The oscillator makes a higher low. Seen at the end of downtrends.

The opposite pattern, used for trend continuation, is [hidden divergence](/hidden-divergence/).

Nobody is credited with inventing it. The idea of comparing price against an indicator appears in the earliest momentum work, and Wilder described a related RSI idea in 1978 (the failure swing). Divergence is now a standard part of every textbook on oscillators.

## How it is identified

1. **Pick an oscillator.** [RSI](/rsi/) and [MACD](/macd/) are the most common. Use the same one throughout.
2. **Find two matching swing points on price.** Two swing highs for bearish divergence, or two swing lows for bullish.
3. **Look at the oscillator at the same two points.** Compare its peaks or troughs.
4. **Check they disagree.** For bearish: price high 2 above high 1, oscillator high 2 below high 1. For bullish: price low 2 below low 1, oscillator low 2 above low 1.
5. **Draw a line** on price and on the oscillator to make the disagreement clear.

**Worked example (bearish).** Price makes a swing high at 100 with RSI at 74. After a pullback, price makes a new swing high at 104, but RSI peaks at only 66. Price is higher by 4 but RSI is lower by 8, so this is bearish divergence. It says momentum is fading. It does not yet say price will fall.

## How to read it

**It is a warning, not a trigger.** Divergence says a move is tiring. Price can keep rising for many bars after it appears, and can show several divergences in a row before turning.

**Wait for a price signal.** Many traders only act when price confirms: a break of a trendline, a close below the last swing low, or a reversal candle at a known level.

**Strength.** Divergence across a longer time and a bigger gap in the oscillator is often treated as more meaningful. Divergence on a higher time frame (daily or weekly) carries more weight than on a one-minute chart.

**Level matters.** Bearish divergence while the oscillator is in overbought territory, at a known resistance level, is usually considered stronger than divergence in the middle of the scale.

**Failure.** If price simply makes a further new high after the divergence, the signal has failed. Plan for that in advance.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** At the end of a long, stretched move, divergence can show the first crack in momentum.

**Ranges (fair).** Divergence at the edge of a range can help time a fade, but the range itself gives the main signal.

**Strong trends (weak).** In a powerful trend, divergences are common and mostly fail. Price keeps trending while the oscillator resets from its extremes. Trading each one against the trend is a frequent way to lose money.

**Volatility.** Choppy, volatile markets make swing points hard to pick, so divergences become subjective.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Matching swings | Compare swing high to swing high, or swing low to swing low |
| Clear disagreement | Price and oscillator move in opposite directions, not just by a tiny amount |
| Level | At or near a known support or resistance level |
| Price trigger | Break of structure, trendline or reversal candle after the divergence |
| Time frame | Higher time frames are more significant |
| Stop | Beyond the latest extreme (the swing high or low that made the divergence) |

## Common mistakes

- Acting the moment divergence appears, with no price trigger.
- Fighting a strong trend because "there is divergence".
- Connecting the wrong peaks, or picking them in hindsight.
- Using different oscillators until one shows divergence.
- Ignoring the time frame. A divergence on a small chart can be lost on a bigger one.
- Treating divergence as a prediction of how far price will fall or rise.

## Pairs well with

- **[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/)**: divergence at a key level is more meaningful than divergence in open space.

## FAQ

**What is bullish divergence?**
It is when price makes a lower low but the oscillator makes a higher low. It shows selling pressure is weakening and a rise may follow. It is not a buy signal on its own and needs a price trigger.

**What is bearish divergence?**
It is when price makes a higher high but the oscillator makes a lower high. It shows buying pressure is weakening and a fall may follow. Price can still rise for a long time after it appears.

**Which indicator is best for divergence?**
RSI and MACD are the most used, but any momentum oscillator works. Pick one and stick with it. Switching until one agrees with your view is a form of bias.

**How reliable is divergence?**
It is not reliable alone. Many divergences fail, especially in strong trends. It works better as a warning combined with a level and a price trigger. Any hit rate you see quoted depends on the test and the market.

**What is the difference between regular and hidden divergence?**
Regular divergence signals a possible reversal: price makes a new extreme that the oscillator does not confirm. Hidden divergence signals a possible continuation: price makes a shallower pullback while the oscillator makes a deeper one. See [Hidden divergence](/hidden-divergence/).
