Breakout trading means entering when price moves beyond a defined level, such as a range high, a range low or a trendline, on the idea that the move will continue. Many breakouts fail, so the method depends on a firm stop and a clear definition of what counts as a break.

## What is breakout trading?

Price often spends time in a tight area, then leaves it. The idea behind breakout trading is that the longer the build-up, the bigger the move that follows. A breakout trader does not try to guess the direction. They set a level above and below, and act on whichever side breaks.

No single person invented it. Breakout systems go back to the earliest chart trading. One well-known formal version is the Donchian channel breakout, used in classic trend-following systems. Richard Donchian's work is described in Covel's book on trend following. See [Donchian channels](/donchian-channels/).

The weakness is false breakouts. Price pokes through a level, attracts buyers, then falls back. Some experienced traders say most breakouts fail, though there is no single agreed figure, so treat any number you read with care.

## How it is identified

A breakout setup normally has three parts:

1. **A clear level.** A range high or low, a horizontal level that has been tested, or a trendline. The more times it has held, the more it matters.
2. **A build-up.** A period of tight price action, such as a narrow range or an [inside bar](/inside-bar/) sequence, often with falling volatility.
3. **A trigger.** Price moves beyond the level. Rules differ: a close beyond it, a break by a set number of points or a fraction of ATR, or an intrabar touch.

**Worked example.** Price has traded between 100 and 105 for two weeks. A trader sets a buy trigger on a close above 105. Price closes at 106. The stop goes at 103, back inside the range, so risk is 3 points. A simple measured target is the range height (5) added to the breakout level: 105 + 5 = 110. Reward is about 4 points from the entry at 106, so the reward to risk ratio is about 1.3 to 1. If it were lower, many traders would skip the trade. See [measured move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Close or touch.** A close beyond the level filters out some wicks, but gives a later entry. A touch is earlier but more exposed to false breaks.

**Volume.** Higher volume on the break is taken as a sign of real participation. A break on thin volume is treated with more doubt. See [volume](/volume/).

**Retest.** Price often returns to the broken level, which may now act as support. Some traders wait for this, which gives a better entry but misses breakouts that never look back. See [breakout and retest](/breakout-retest/).

**Squeeze.** A very narrow range before the break, as measured by Bollinger Band width, can mark a coiled market. See [Bollinger Squeeze](/bollinger-squeeze/).

**Failure.** A close back inside the old range soon after the break shows the move failed. A [false breakout](/false-breakout/) can itself be traded in the opposite direction.

## Best market states

**Breakouts (best).** After a quiet consolidation near a well-tested level, with the broader trend pointing the same way as the break.

**Ranges (weak).** In a loose, choppy range, breaks of the edge often reverse. Many small losses add up.

**Trends.** A break in the direction of the trend is generally preferred over a break against it.

**Volatility.** Breakouts need volatility to follow through. The method suits markets coming out of low volatility, but wide stops are needed once it starts. Use [ATR](/atr/) to size the stop.

**Timing.** Breakouts at session opens, such as the [opening range breakout](/opening-range-breakout/), are common, but the first minutes are also the noisiest.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Level | Tested at least twice before the break |
| Build-up | Narrowing range or falling volatility |
| Trigger | A fixed rule: close beyond the level, or a buffer such as 0.5 x ATR |
| Volume | Above recent average on the break bar |
| Trend | Break in the direction of the higher time frame trend |
| Stop | Inside the old range, or a multiple of ATR |

## Common mistakes

- Entering on an intrabar wick through a level that then closes back inside.
- Using no buffer and getting stopped by normal noise around the level.
- Chasing a breakout that has already moved far from the level, making the stop too wide.
- Trading breakouts in a market with no prior consolidation.
- Skipping the stop because "it is a breakout, it will run".
- Reading only the results of the breakouts that worked, and forgetting the failures.

## FAQ

**What is breakout trading?**
It is entering a trade when price moves beyond a set level, such as a range high, expecting the move to continue. The trader places a stop back inside the old range, so a failed breakout produces a small, defined loss.

**How do you avoid false breakouts?**
You cannot avoid them completely. Common filters include waiting for a close beyond the level, using a buffer based on ATR, checking for higher volume and waiting for a retest. Each filter costs a later entry or missed trades.

**Is breakout trading profitable?**
It depends on the market, the rules and the costs, so no general answer is possible. Breakout systems often have a low win rate with larger winners, which is hard to stick with. Test your own rules over many trades.

**Should I buy the breakout or wait for the retest?**
Buying the break gets in early but risks false breaks. Waiting for the retest gives a closer stop and better price, but some breakouts never retest and are missed. Neither is better in every case.

**Where do you put the stop on a breakout trade?**
Typically back inside the old range, just beyond the level that was broken, or at a multiple of ATR. If price closes back inside the range, the breakout idea has failed and the trade should end.
