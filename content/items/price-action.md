Price action trading means making decisions from the price bars themselves, such as their size, shape, position and sequence, rather than from indicators calculated from them. Traders read swings, support and resistance and single-bar or multi-bar patterns to find entries, stops and exits. It is a method of reading charts, not a single signal.

## What is price action trading?

Every indicator is built from price: open, high, low, close and sometimes volume. Price action traders skip the middle step and read the bars directly. They ask simple questions. Is price making higher highs or lower lows? Did it stall at an old level? Did a bar close near its high or near its low? Did buyers or sellers win the last push?

There is no single inventor. The roots go back to the chart-reading traditions described by Edwards and Magee in 1948 and by earlier writers on Dow theory. In modern books, Al Brooks (2012) gave the method its most detailed treatment, describing trends, ranges and reversals bar by bar.

Price action is a way of working rather than one strategy. The same chart can be read by a trend follower, a range trader and a breakout trader, each using the bars differently.

## How it is identified

Price action has no formula. In practice, a trader works through a short routine on a clean chart.

1. **Find the market state.** Is price trending, ranging or breaking out? See [Market structure](/market-structure/).
2. **Mark the levels.** Draw [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) from clear [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/).
3. **Wait for a bar pattern at a level.** Examples are a [pin bar](/pin-bar/), an [inside bar](/inside-bar/) or a [pullback](/pullback-trading/) that holds a swing.
4. **Place the stop at the point where the idea is wrong.** This is normally just beyond the swing or the signal bar.
5. **Choose the exit.** The next level, a trailing swing or a fixed reward-to-risk multiple.

**Worked example.** Price rises from 100 to 120, pulls back to 112 and prints a pin bar with a low of 111.5 that closes at 116. A trader who buys above the pin bar's high at 116.5 with a stop below 111 risks 5.5 points. The previous swing high at 120 is 3.5 points away, so the first target gives less than 1 to 1. The trader may pass, or aim further. The routine is about asking those questions before entering.

## How to read it

**Bar size and closes.** Large bars that close near their high show buying pressure. Small overlapping bars show indecision. A long wick shows a move that was rejected.

**Sequence.** A run of higher highs and higher lows is an uptrend. A break of the last swing low is the first sign it may be changing.

**Location.** The same bar means different things in different places. A bullish bar at support is interesting. The same bar in the middle of a range is noise.

**Context.** A pattern with the trend is usually treated as more reliable than one against it. Brooks (2012) puts heavy emphasis on this: most signals fail in the wrong context.

**Time frame.** Read the higher time frame first to set direction, then the lower one for timing.

## Best market states

**Trends (good).** Pullbacks and breaks of structure give clear entries and stop points.

**Ranges (good).** Rejection bars at the edges and failed breakouts are the main signals. Trading the middle is poor.

**Breakouts (good, with care).** Many breakouts fail, so traders look for a strong close and a retest.

**Reversals (mixed).** Reversal signals are common and many fail. They need a clear level and a break of structure to be taken seriously.

**Weak conditions.** Thin markets and news spikes distort bars, so patterns lose meaning.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Direction | The signal agrees with the higher time frame trend, or the trader accepts it is a counter-trend trade |
| Level | The pattern forms at support, resistance or a swing |
| Close | The signal bar closes the way the idea needs it to, not just a wick |
| Follow-through | The next bar breaks the signal bar, or price holds the level |
| Stop | A clear invalidation point exists and the risk fits the plan |
| Count | Not the tenth signal in a choppy area |

## Common mistakes

- Trading every candlestick pattern without checking location. A pattern in the middle of nowhere has little meaning.
- Treating named patterns as magic. A pin bar is just a bar with a long wick.
- Ignoring the higher time frame and trading against a strong trend.
- Marking too many levels, so every bar seems to be at one.
- Moving the stop once the trade goes wrong.
- Believing price action removes subjectivity. Two traders can read the same chart differently, so write the rules down and test them.

## FAQ

**Is price action trading better than using indicators?**
Neither is better in general. Indicators are calculated from price, so they add a view of it rather than new information. Price action removes the lag of smoothing but needs more judgement. Many traders combine a clean chart with one or two indicators for filtering.

**Do I need candlesticks for price action?**
No. Bar charts and even line charts of closes can be read the same way. Candlesticks are popular because the body and wicks are easy to see. What matters is the open, high, low and close of each bar.

**What time frame is best for price action?**
Any can be used, but patterns on higher time frames, such as the daily chart, tend to be less noisy than on one-minute charts. Many traders read the higher frame for direction and a lower one for entries.

**Is price action trading objective?**
Partly. Rules such as "enter above a pin bar that closes in the top third" can be tested. Judging whether a level is meaningful is subjective. Writing clear rules and keeping a record of trades reduces the guesswork.

**Can a beginner learn price action?**
Yes, but it takes screen time. Start with swings, support and resistance and one or two patterns. Review past charts bar by bar before trading, and keep position sizes small while learning.
