A rectangle is a chart pattern in which price moves sideways between two roughly horizontal lines: support below and resistance above. Traders can buy near support and sell near resistance while the range holds, then watch for a close beyond either line as a breakout.

## What is a rectangle pattern?

A rectangle forms when buyers and sellers are evenly matched. Price rises to a resistance level, falls to a support level, and repeats. The highs line up along a flat top and the lows along a flat bottom, so the price action fits in a box.

The pattern is a pause. If it forms inside an up-trend, it is usually treated as a continuation pattern, with the expectation that price eventually breaks upward. If it forms after a long trend, it can mark a top or bottom instead. In practice you cannot know which until the break happens, so most traders wait for it.

The term appears throughout classic chart-pattern literature, including Edwards and Magee's *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends* (first published 1948), and in later references such as Bulkowski's *Encyclopedia of Chart Patterns*. No single person is credited with it.

Rectangles are close relatives of triangles. A triangle has converging lines, while a rectangle has parallel ones. A flat top with rising lows is an [ascending triangle](/ascending-triangle/), and a flat bottom with falling highs is a [descending triangle](/descending-triangle/).

## How it is identified

1. **A resistance line.** At least two highs at about the same price.
2. **A support line.** At least two lows at about the same price.
3. **Alternation.** Price touches each line in turn, so you can count at least four turning points in all.
4. **Enough width and time.** The range should be wide enough to be tradable and last long enough to matter. Several weeks on a daily chart is typical, though no firm minimum exists.
5. **Break.** A close beyond either line ends the pattern.

**Measured target.** Take the height of the rectangle and project it from the breakout point.

**Worked example.** Price bounces between support at 90 and resistance at 100 for two months. The height is 10. Price closes at 101 above resistance.

Target = 100 + 10 = **110**

A conservative stop sits back inside the range, for example at 97, or below the support line at 89 for a wider one. As always, the target is only a guide: some breakouts fall short and some travel much further.

**Trading inside the box.** A trader can also buy near 90 with a stop below 88 and a target near 100, then do the opposite at the top. The risk is that the range breaks while you are still positioned against it.

## How to read it

**Boundaries are zones.** Real price touches rarely hit exactly the same value. Treat each line as a small band, and decide in advance whether a wick or a close counts as a break.

**Volume.** Volume often declines inside the rectangle as interest dries up, and rises on a breakout. A breakout on thin volume is more suspect.

**Direction of the break.** The prior trend gives a hint, but not a rule. Some rectangles break against it.

**Retest.** After a breakout, price often returns to the broken line, which may now act as support (for an upward break). See [Role reversal](/role-reversal/).

**False breaks.** Rectangles produce many false breakouts, where price pokes beyond a line and returns to the box. See [False breakout](/false-breakout/). A close back inside is the warning.

**Longer boxes carry more weight.** A range that has held for many months, and has been tested many times, often leads to a larger move once it breaks, though this is a general tendency, not a rule.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best for trading inside).** The pattern is a range, so range tactics fit: buy near the bottom, sell near the top. See [Range trading](/range-trading/).

**Breakouts (best for the exit from the box).** The move out of a well-formed rectangle is one of the standard breakout setups. See [Breakout trading](/breakout-trading/).

**Trends (weak).** When price is trending strongly there is rarely a clean rectangle. Calling a brief pause a rectangle can mean exiting a trend early.

**Volatility.** As the range tightens, volatility often falls, and a later break often brings it back. A very wide box with large swings is a poorer setup, since the stop must be placed far away.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Touches | At least two on each boundary |
| Parallel lines | Roughly horizontal, within a small tolerance |
| Duration | Long enough to be meaningful on the time frame |
| Break | A close beyond the boundary, not just a wick |
| Volume | Rising on the break |
| Retest | A hold at the broken line improves confidence |
| Stop | Back inside the range, or beyond the opposite side |

## Common mistakes

- Trading the box and ignoring the breakout, so you are positioned against it when it comes.
- Drawing lines that fit too loosely, so any sideways wiggle becomes a rectangle.
- Acting on a wick beyond the line instead of waiting for a close.
- Placing a stop too tight against a boundary that price is likely to test.
- Assuming the break will follow the prior trend.
- Using the measured target as a promise.

## FAQ

**What is a rectangle pattern in trading?**
A rectangle is a sideways price range bounded by flat support and resistance lines. Price bounces between them until it breaks out in one direction. It shows a balance between buyers and sellers and is often seen as a pause.

**Is a rectangle bullish or bearish?**
Neither until it breaks. Inside an up-trend it is often treated as a continuation pattern with an upside bias, but the break can go either way. Wait for a close beyond the lines.

**How do you measure a rectangle target?**
Measure the height from support to resistance and project it from the breakout level in the direction of the break. For example, a 10-point box broken at 100 gives a target of 110. Treat it as a rough guide.

**How long should a rectangle last?**
There is no fixed time. It needs enough touches on each side and enough duration to be meaningful on your time frame. Longer, well-tested ranges are generally treated as more significant.

**What is the difference between a rectangle and a triangle?**
A rectangle has parallel, horizontal boundaries. A triangle has converging lines, so the range narrows. An [ascending triangle](/ascending-triangle/) has a flat top and rising bottom, for example.
