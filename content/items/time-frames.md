A time frame is the length of time each bar or candle on a chart covers, such as 5 minutes, 1 hour or 1 day. It sets how much detail and noise you see. A sensible choice matches the time frame to how long you plan to hold a trade, and checks the next higher frame for context.

## What is a time frame?

Every bar or candle on a time-based chart summarises one period: its open, high, low and close. A 1-hour chart gives one candle per hour. A daily chart gives one candle per day. Moving to a higher time frame joins many smaller periods into one candle and so removes detail.

The same market can look different on each frame. A daily chart might show a clear uptrend while the 5-minute chart shows a messy fall inside it. Neither is wrong. They show different scales of the same price action.

Higher frames have fewer candles and less noise, and each candle represents more trading. Lower frames show more detail but more random movement, and costs such as spreads and commissions take a larger share of each trade.

## How it works

There is no single correct time frame. Most traders work from their **holding period**: how long a trade is meant to last.

| Style | Typical holding period | Frames often used |
|---|---|---|
| Scalping | Seconds to minutes | 1 to 5 minutes |
| Day trading | Minutes to hours, closed the same day | 5 to 60 minutes |
| Swing trading | Days to weeks | 4 hours to daily |
| Position trading | Weeks to months | Daily to monthly |

These are conventions, not rules.

**A three-frame set.** A common practice is to use three frames, each about four to six times the one below it. Alexander Elder's triple screen approach (described in *Trading for a Living*) uses a factor of about five. For example, a swing trader might use weekly (trend), daily (setup) and 4-hour (entry). A day trader might use daily, 1-hour and 15-minute.

**Worked example.** A trader plans to hold for about two days. They pick the 1-hour chart to trade on. They check the 4-hour chart to see the trend, and the daily chart for major support and resistance. If the 1-hour shows a buy setup but the daily chart is at strong resistance, the setup is lower quality.

See [multiple time frame analysis](/multiple-time-frame-analysis/) for the full method.

## How to read it

**Higher frames carry more weight.** A level or trendline on a daily chart usually matters more than one on a 5-minute chart, because more trading went into it.

**Trend on the higher frame.** Many traders look for trades in the direction of the trend on the frame above the one they trade.

**Entry on the lower frame.** A lower frame can help place entries and stops more precisely.

**Consistency.** Indicator settings change meaning with the frame. A 14-period RSI on a 5-minute chart covers 70 minutes, and on a daily chart covers 14 days.

**Session effects.** Intraday frames are shaped by sessions, opening hours and news times. Daily and weekly frames hide these.

## Best market states

**Trends.** Trends are easier to see on higher frames, where noise matters less. A trend on a low frame can be a small pullback in a bigger range.

**Ranges.** A range on one frame can be a trend on a lower one. Check which frame the range is on before using range tactics.

**Volatility.** Volatility readings such as [ATR](/atr/) depend on the frame. A stop sized from a 5-minute ATR is far tighter than one from a daily ATR.

## Settings

| Choice | Guideline |
|---|---|
| Trading frame | Fits your holding period and how often you can watch the chart |
| Higher frame | Four to six times the trading frame |
| Lower frame (optional) | For fine entry timing only |
| Number of frames | Two or three; more usually adds confusion |
| Indicator settings | Keep the same on each frame so signals are comparable |

## Common mistakes

- Hunting through frames until one agrees with a bias.
- Using too many frames and ending up with conflicting signals.
- Trading very short frames without allowing for spreads and commissions.
- Ignoring the higher-frame trend or major levels.
- Changing frame after a loss to find a "better" picture.
- Assuming an indicator setting means the same thing on every frame.

## FAQ

**What is the best time frame for trading?**
There is no best one. It depends on how long you hold trades, how often you can watch the chart and what costs you pay. Match the frame to the holding period, and check the frame above it for context.

**What is the best time frame for beginners?**
Many educators suggest starting on higher frames such as the 4-hour or daily chart. There is less noise, decisions come more slowly, and costs take a smaller share of each trade. This is a common view, not a proven rule.

**How many time frames should I use?**
Two or three is typical: one to read the trend, one to trade on and possibly one for fine entry timing. More than that often leads to conflicting signals and indecision.

**Do indicator settings change with the time frame?**
The settings stay the same but the meaning changes. A 14-period RSI covers 14 candles, so it spans 14 minutes on a 1-minute chart and 14 days on a daily chart. Compare like with like.

**Why do candles on different platforms look different?**
Platforms can use different session times, time zones or data feeds, so a daily or 4-hour candle can open and close at different times. Check the platform's settings if charts do not match.
