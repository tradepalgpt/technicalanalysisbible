A common gap is an empty space on a chart between one bar's range and the next, which appears inside a sideways trading range and has no larger meaning. Unlike a breakaway, runaway or exhaustion gap, it does not mark the start, middle or end of a trend. Traders mostly use it as a filter, to avoid treating a routine gap as a signal.

## What is a common gap?

A gap occurs when a bar opens above the previous bar's high or below its low, leaving an empty space on the chart. See [price gaps](/price-gaps/) for the general idea.

Classic chart texts group gaps into four types. Three of them signal something: a breakaway gap starts a trend, a runaway gap appears in the middle of one, and an exhaustion gap marks its end. The fourth, the common gap, signals nothing. It is also called an area gap or a trading gap because it happens within a trading area, a range where price moves sideways.

Common gaps are most often seen in thinly traded stocks, around dividend dates and in quiet markets, where a small shift in order flow can leave a visible space. They are also common between the close and the next open on stock charts, because trading stops overnight.

The usual view is that common gaps tend to be filled, meaning price returns to cover the gap, fairly quickly. This is a general tendency described in textbooks, not a measured rule. See [gap fill](/gap-fill/).

## How it is identified

A common gap needs these features:

1. **A gap.** The new bar's range does not overlap the previous bar's range.
2. **A sideways setting.** Price has been moving in a range, not in a clear trend.
3. **No breakout.** The gap is not leaving a well-defined support or resistance level.
4. **Ordinary volume.** The gap is not backed by a clear surge.
5. **Quick return.** Price often moves back into the gap within a few bars, although it does not have to.

**Worked example.** A stock has traded between 50 and 55 for several weeks. One day the high is 53. The next day opens at 54 and trades between 54 and 55. The space between 53 and 54 is the gap. It sits inside the 50 to 55 range and happens on average volume. This is a common gap. If price falls back to 53 the next day, the gap is filled.

By contrast, if the same gap had opened above 55, on heavy volume and with price staying above the old range, it would be a breakaway gap.

## How to read it

**It tells you little.** The main reading is that nothing has changed. Price remains in a range, and the gap is not a reason to expect a new trend.

**Fill expectation.** Many traders expect common gaps to close soon. Some trade that idea by fading the gap, taking a position back toward the previous close. This is a trader's habit and is not backed by a reliable statistic, so test any such approach carefully.

**Range edges still matter.** The gap does not change the boundaries of the range. The upper and lower edges of the range are still the key levels. See [range trading](/range-trading/).

**Classification is made after the fact.** A gap that looks common can turn out to be the start of a breakout if price keeps going. Traders often cannot tell a common gap from a breakaway gap at the moment it forms.

**Volume check.** A gap on unusually high volume is more likely to be one of the other types.

## Best market states

**Range (best).** This is where common gaps happen and where the label is useful.

**Trend (weak).** In a trend, a gap is more likely to be a runaway gap, and calling it common can lead you to expect a fill that never comes.

**Breakout (weak).** A gap through a clear level is usually a breakaway gap.

**Reversal.** A gap near the end of a trend may be an exhaustion gap. A gap inside a quiet base is more likely common.

**Volatility.** In volatile markets gaps are frequent, which makes the common type harder to separate from the others.

| Volume | Average or lower than normal, no surge |
| Follow-through | Price does not run away from the gap |
| Fill | Price often returns to the gap, though this is not certain |
| Action | No new trend assumed from the gap alone |

## Common mistakes

- Treating every gap as a signal, when the common type is routine.
- Assuming a gap will always fill. Some do not, and some take a long time.
- Labelling a gap as common when it is actually leaving a range on heavy volume.
- Fading a gap without a stop, assuming it will close.
- Using a gap from an illiquid market, where one trade can create the space.
- Ignoring the range edges and focusing only on the gap.

## FAQ

**What is a common gap?**
It is a gap that forms inside a sideways trading range and does not signal a new trend. It is also called an area gap or a trading gap. It is distinguished from breakaway, runaway and exhaustion gaps, which each carry a meaning about the trend.

**Do common gaps always fill?**
No. Textbooks say they tend to fill quickly, but this is a general tendency, not a guarantee. Some stay open for a long time, and a gap that looks common can turn into the start of a breakout.

**How do you tell a common gap from a breakaway gap?**
A breakaway gap leaves a range or pattern on heavy volume and tends to keep going. A common gap stays inside a range, on ordinary volume. The difference is often clear only after price has moved on.

**Can you trade a common gap?**
Some traders fade it, expecting price to return and fill the gap. Others ignore it. There is no strong evidence for any single approach, so test it on your market and keep a stop.

**Why do common gaps happen?**
They are most common on stock charts between one day's close and the next day's open, and in thin markets, where a small imbalance of orders can leave a space. Dividend dates can also create small gaps.
