A V-reversal is a sharp turn where price falls (or rises) quickly, then reverses just as fast, leaving a V or inverted V on the chart. It has no gradual build-up. Because it gives little warning, it is usually spotted after the turn and used as a confirmation of a change in direction.

## What is a V-reversal?

A V-bottom happens when price drops steeply to a low and then rallies just as steeply, with no base-building between the two. A V-top, or spike top, is the mirror image: a steep rise to a high, followed by a steep fall.

It is the opposite of slow patterns such as the [rounding top](/rounding-top/). In slow patterns, buyers and sellers take time to change control. In a V, the change is abrupt. This often follows news, a panic sell-off, a squeeze or a burst of buying that burns itself out.

Classic chart-pattern books, including Edwards and Magee's, discuss these spike formations and point out how hard they are to trade. The difficulty is that the turn happens fast and price offers little time to prepare. There is also no clear formation until after the fact.

Because of this, a V-reversal is a pattern to recognise rather than anticipate. It is far easier to see on a completed chart than in real time.

## How it is identified

A V-reversal needs:

1. **A steep move into the turn.** A long, near-vertical move over a short period.
2. **A sharp low or high.** The turning bar has a large range, often with a long wick.
3. **A steep move back.** Price retraces most or all of the move quickly.
4. **A retake of the earlier level.** Price returns to roughly where the steep move began.

There is no standard measurement. Some traders require a retake of at least half the prior move, others the full distance. Because there are no standard settings, definitions vary.

**Worked example.** Price falls from 120 to 100 in a few bars, a drop of 20. It then rallies to 118 in a similar time. Price has retraced 18 of the 20 points, so it has retaken 90% of the drop. At 110, it had retaken 50%. A trader using a 50% rule would treat the V as confirmed at 110.

## How to read it

**Confirmation, not prediction.** A V is usually seen after the turn. A sharp fall with no sign of reversal is just a sharp fall.

**Volume.** A surge in volume at the turn, often called a climax, is common (see [volume climax](/volume-climax/)). It is a clue, not a rule.

**Volatility.** The turning bar often has a very wide range, which can be compared with [ATR](/atr/).

**Retake level.** The level where the steep move began acts as a reference. Price holding above it after a V-bottom (or below it after a V-top) supports the reversal.

**Failure.** If price retakes some ground then falls back to the extreme, the V has failed. Such failed turns are common.

## Best market states

**Sharp reversals after panic or euphoric moves (best).** These are the conditions that produce Vs.

**Trends (caution).** A fast pullback in an uptrend can look like a V-top without being a top.

**Ranges (weak).** Spikes within ranges are mostly noise, and the swings return to the middle.

**Volatility.** The pattern lives in high-volatility conditions, so stops need to be wide and sizes small.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Move in | Steep, near-vertical, over a short span |
| Turn | A bar with a wide range, often a long wick |
| Move out | Quick and strong, retaking a large part of the prior move |
| Volume | A surge at or near the turn |
| Hold | Price stays beyond the retake level on a closing basis |
| Stop | Beyond the extreme of the turn |

## Common mistakes

- Trying to buy or sell into the spike on the assumption it will reverse.
- Calling every sharp fall a V-bottom before price has turned.
- Having no rule for how much of the move must be retaken.
- Using tight stops in a market with huge ranges.
- Forgetting that failed Vs, where price returns to the extreme, are common.
- Treating a V as a sign of a new trend. It can be a brief bounce.

## FAQ

**What is a V-reversal?**
It is a sharp turn in price with no base-building. Price moves steeply one way, then reverses just as steeply, leaving a V or inverted V on the chart.

**How is a V-bottom different from a rounding bottom?**
A rounding bottom is a slow, gradual turn over many bars. A V-bottom is abrupt, with a sharp low and a quick rally. They are opposite ways for a market to change direction.

**Can you trade a V-reversal?**
It is hard to trade because the turn happens quickly and there is no pattern to wait for beforehand. Most traders use it as a confirmation after price has already moved back, not as an entry signal.

**How do you confirm a V-reversal?**
Common signs are a surge in volume at the turn and price retaking the level where the steep move began. Rules for how much to retake vary, so each trader sets their own.

**Why do V-reversals fail?**
A sharp bounce can simply be a brief pause before the trend resumes. If price falls back to the extreme, the V has failed. Failed Vs are common, so stops and sizing matter.
