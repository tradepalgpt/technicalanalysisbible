Round numbers are prices that end in zeros, such as 100, 1.2000 or 50,000. Traders tend to place orders, targets and stops at these prices, so markets often pause, reverse or become jumpy near them. They are used as rough support and resistance and as reference points when placing entries and stops.

## What are round numbers?

People like round numbers. When they decide where to buy, sell, take profit or stop out, they often choose a price such as 100 rather than 98.7. As a result, orders can cluster at round prices. The effect is easy to understand and is the basis for treating round numbers as a form of [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).

Some academic work on stock and currency markets has found clustering of orders at round numbers. Evidence that these levels reliably cause reversals is more mixed. This page treats round numbers as a practical reference, not a proven edge.

How "round" a level is matters. A price ending in .00 is usually watched more than .50. In currencies, 1.2000 is more watched than 1.2050. In indices, 5,000 is more watched than 5,100, and so on. The bigger the round number, the more attention it tends to get.

## How it is identified

There is nothing to calculate. Traders mark the levels on the chart with [horizontal lines](/key-levels/).

1. Decide which grain matters for the market. For a stock at 47, whole tens (40, 50) matter. For a currency pair, the "big figures" (1.2000) and half figures (1.2050) matter. For an index around 18,000, hundreds and thousands matter.
2. Draw lines at levels near the current price.
3. Ignore very small multiples that clutter the chart.

**Worked example.** A stock trades at 96. The nearest round level above is 100 and the one below is 90. A trader planning a long might look for a stop just below 90 rather than at 90 exactly, because many other stops will sit at 90. A trader looking to sell might place a limit just below 100, because buy orders can fade at 100.

## How to read it

**Reaction.** Look for stalls, wicks or reversals as price approaches a round number. Rejection wicks at the level are often read as a sign that orders were hit.

**Magnet effect.** In some markets price is drawn to a round number and may trade through it quickly to trigger the orders resting there. After that, price may reverse.

**Stops just beyond.** Because many stops sit at or just past round numbers, price often moves a little beyond a level and then returns. Many traders place stops a few ticks or points beyond the level, not on it.

**Breaks.** A strong close through a round number with volume can signal that the level has given way. Retests of the broken level are common. See [role reversal](/role-reversal/).

**Combination.** A round number that matches a swing high, a pivot level or the previous day's extreme is treated as stronger.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** A range that tops out just under a round number is common.

**Reversals (good).** Reversals often occur near round numbers, but it is hard to separate real effect from the fact that there are so many round numbers.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong trend price passes through round numbers with little pause.

**Volatility.** In fast markets or around news, price can jump through a level with slippage and no reaction.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Scale | The level is round relative to the market (100, 1,000 or 1.2000, not 97.3) |
| Reaction | A wick, stall or reversal candle at the level |
| Overlap | The level matches another level, such as a swing high |
| Volume | An increase in activity near the level |
| Stop | A few ticks beyond the level, not exactly on it |
| Break | A close beyond the level with follow-through |

## Common mistakes

- Treating every round number as a strong level.
- Placing a stop exactly on a round number, where many other stops sit.
- Entering as soon as price reaches the level without waiting for a reaction.
- Ignoring the trend and fading a strong move into a round number.
- Using levels so small they are not "round" for that market.
- Expecting an exact turn rather than a zone of a few ticks.

## FAQ

**What are round numbers in trading?**
They are prices that end in zeros, such as 100, 1.2000 or 50,000. Many traders place orders there, so markets often pause or react near them. They are used as rough support and resistance.

**Why do round numbers act as support and resistance?**
People prefer to choose round prices for entries, targets and stops, so orders can cluster there. This clustering is the usual explanation. Evidence for reliable reversals is mixed, so they should be used with other tools.

**How do I use round numbers for stop placement?**
Because many stops sit on round numbers, price often moves a little past them and returns. Many traders place stops a few ticks beyond the level rather than on it, so a brief sweep does not stop them out.

**Which round numbers matter most?**
The larger the round number, the more attention it tends to draw. In currencies, the big figures like 1.2000 matter more than 1.2050. In indices, round thousands matter more than round hundreds.

**Do round numbers work in every market?**
They can be drawn in any market with a price. Their effect is thought to be strongest where many retail orders cluster, such as currencies, popular stocks and crypto. Test how your market behaves around them.
