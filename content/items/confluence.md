Confluence is when two or more separate technical factors point to the same price area or the same direction. For example, a horizontal support level, a Fibonacci retracement and a rising moving average all sitting at 100. Traders treat the agreement as stronger evidence than any single factor.

## What is confluence?

Every technical tool has flaws. A single moving average, a single level or a single oscillator reading will be wrong often. The idea behind confluence is that when different tools agree, the chance that the area matters goes up. It is the same logic as asking for a second opinion.

The idea is general, and not owned by anyone. It is described in various forms in technical analysis books. Fibonacci confluence, where several retracement or extension levels from different swings cluster together, is a well-known case. See [Fibonacci confluence](/fibonacci-confluence/).

Confluence is not proof. It gives a reason to pay attention, and a reason to take a trade with a tighter stop. Market behaviour at any single level is still uncertain, and there is no agreed number that says how much confluence is enough.

## How it is identified

Look for factors from different families that line up at one price or in one direction.

**Same price.** Levels from support and resistance, Fibonacci retracements, pivot points, previous highs and lows, round numbers, or a moving average all near the same figure.

**Same direction.** Trend, momentum and structure all agree: for example, higher highs and higher lows, price above a rising average, and a bullish momentum reading.

**Different time frames.** A level on the daily chart matching a level on the four-hour chart. See [multiple time frame analysis](/multiple-time-frame-analysis/).

**Worked example.** A trader looks for a long near 100.
- Factor 1: horizontal support, where price turned twice before, at 100.
- Factor 2: the 61.8 percent [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) of the last up-swing is at 100.2.
- Factor 3: a rising 50-period average is at 99.8.
Three factors cluster inside a 0.4-point band. The trader treats 99.8 to 100.2 as one zone, waits for a rejection candle, and places the stop at 99.3, below the cluster. Risk is about 0.5 points. With only one factor present, the trader would pass.

## How to read it

**Independence.** Factors count as separate only if they use different information. A 50-period and a 55-period average are nearly the same thing and count as one.

**Families.** Try to combine a level (support and resistance), a trend or structure factor, and a momentum or volume factor.

**Zones, not lines.** Confluence is rarely exact to the pip. Treat the cluster as a band.

**Reaction still needed.** The cluster says where to look. A reaction by price, such as a rejection candle, says whether to act.

**Dependence on the swing.** Fibonacci levels depend on which swings you pick. If you choose the swings to make the levels fit, the confluence is not real.

## Best market states

**Trends (good).** A pullback to a rising average, a trendline and a Fibonacci level together is a classic setup. See [pullback trading](/pullback-trading/).

**Ranges (good).** Range edges that also match a level on a higher time frame are stronger edges.

**Reversals (good).** At a turning point, a divergence, a major level and a candlestick signal together give a clearer story than any one alone.

**Volatility.** Zones are wider in volatile markets, so the cluster may cover too large a band to give a tight stop.

**Limits.** In a strong move, price passes straight through even a very heavy cluster of levels.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Count | Two to three independent factors, not ten |
| Independence | Factors from different families, not copies of the same data |
| Location | The cluster sits within a narrow band relative to ATR |
| Time frame | At least one factor from a higher time frame |
| Reaction | A price reaction at the zone before entry |
| Stop | Beyond the whole cluster, so a break means all factors failed |

## Common mistakes

- Stacking many tools until something lines up, then calling it confluence. This is cherry-picking.
- Counting related indicators, such as two momentum oscillators, as separate evidence.
- Choosing Fibonacci swings after the fact to match a known level.
- Waiting for perfect confluence and missing every trade.
- Assuming more factors means a higher win rate, with no testing.
- Skipping the stop because "so many things agree".

## FAQ

**What is confluence in trading?**
It is when two or more independent technical factors agree at the same price or in the same direction, such as support, a Fibonacci level and a moving average at one spot. Traders treat that agreement as stronger than a single signal.

**How many confluence factors do I need?**
There is no agreed number. Two or three independent factors are common. Adding more tends to make the setup rare and risks cherry-picking. Test your own rules to see whether extra factors improve results.

**Does confluence improve win rate?**
It may filter out weaker setups, but there is no universal evidence of a specific improvement. Fewer trades is certain. Better results are not. Backtest it on your own market and compare to the single-factor version.

**What counts as an independent factor?**
A factor that uses different information. A support level, a trend reading and a volume reading are different. Two moving averages or two similar oscillators mostly repeat the same price data and should count as one.

**Is confluence the same as Fibonacci confluence?**
No. Fibonacci confluence is one type: several Fibonacci levels from different swings clustering in one area. Confluence in general can combine any tools, such as levels, trends, volume and candles.
