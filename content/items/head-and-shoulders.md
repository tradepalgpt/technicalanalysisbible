A head and shoulders is a reversal chart pattern that forms at the end of an uptrend. It has three peaks: a left shoulder, a higher head and a lower right shoulder, joined at the base by a neckline. A close below the neckline is the usual signal that the uptrend has ended and a fall may follow.

## What is a head and shoulders pattern?

A head and shoulders shows buyers running out of steam. In an uptrend, price makes a high (the left shoulder), pulls back, then rallies to a higher high (the head). The next pullback falls to around the same level as the first. The final rally fails to reach the head and stalls at a lower high (the right shoulder). That lower high is the first sign that the run of higher highs is over.

The line joining the two pullback lows is the neckline. While price stays above it, the pattern is only a possibility. A close below it completes the pattern.

Richard Schabacker described the pattern in the 1930s, and Robert Edwards and John Magee made it famous in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*. It remains one of the best-known chart patterns.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **A prior uptrend.** Without one, there is nothing to reverse.
2. **Left shoulder:** a peak followed by a pullback.
3. **Head:** a higher peak followed by a pullback to around the same area as the first.
4. **Right shoulder:** a lower peak than the head, roughly level with the left shoulder.
5. **Neckline:** a line through the two pullback lows. It can slope up, down or be flat.
6. **Break:** a close below the neckline.

**Measured target.** Measure the vertical distance from the top of the head to the neckline. Subtract that distance from the breakout point.

**Worked example.** The head peaks at 120 and the neckline below it is at 100: a height of 20. If price breaks the neckline at 99, the measured target is 99 - 20 = **79**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. Many patterns fall short, and some go further.

## How to read it

**Neckline break.** A close below the neckline completes the pattern. Many traders wait for a close rather than an intrabar touch to avoid false breaks.

**Retest.** Price often bounces back to the broken neckline, which may now act as resistance (role reversal). Some traders enter on this retest rather than on the break.

**Volume.** In classic examples volume is heaviest on the left shoulder, lighter on the head and lighter still on the right shoulder, then rises on the break. Falling volume on the rallies shows weakening demand.

**Neckline slope.** A downward-sloping neckline shows sellers are already pushing harder and is often considered the stronger version.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks the neckline and then closes back above the right shoulder, the pattern has failed. Failures often lead to sharp moves the other way.

## Best market states

**Reversals at the end of a long uptrend (best).** The pattern makes sense only when there is a meaningful trend to reverse.

**Strong trends (caution).** In a powerful uptrend, a "right shoulder" can simply be a pause before new highs.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways price action produces lots of bumps that can be forced into a head and shoulders shape. Without a prior trend the pattern means little.

**Volatility.** Very volatile markets produce messy shoulders and frequent false neckline breaks. Wider stops are needed.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear uptrend before the left shoulder |
| Symmetry | Shoulders at roughly similar levels and widths |
| Lower high | Right shoulder clearly below the head |
| Break | Close below the neckline, not just a wick |
| Volume | Lighter on the right shoulder, rising on the break |
| Stop | Above the right shoulder (conservative: above the head) |

## Common mistakes

- Selling before the neckline breaks. Until then it is only a possible pattern.
- Seeing the pattern in sideways markets where there is no trend to reverse.
- Ignoring the right shoulder's height: if it is as high as the head, it is not a head and shoulders.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Not having a plan for a failed pattern. A close back above the right shoulder means the idea is wrong.
- Drawing a neckline through wicks and closes inconsistently, so the break point keeps moving.

## FAQ

**Is head and shoulders bullish or bearish?**
The standard head and shoulders top is bearish: it signals the end of an uptrend. Its mirror image, the inverse head and shoulders, forms at the end of a downtrend and is bullish.

**How reliable is the head and shoulders pattern?**
It is one of the most studied chart patterns, but no pattern works every time. Reliability depends on a clear prior trend, a confirmed neckline break and sensible risk control. Many patterns fail or fall short of the measured target.

**Where do you put the stop loss on a head and shoulders?**
Most traders place it above the right shoulder, the point where the pattern would be proven wrong. A more conservative stop goes above the head, which is safer but makes the risk larger.

**How do you calculate the head and shoulders target?**
Measure from the top of the head down to the neckline, then subtract that distance from the point where price broke the neckline. Treat the result as a guide, not a guarantee.

**What is the difference between a head and shoulders and a double top?**
A double top has two peaks at about the same height. A head and shoulders has three peaks, with the middle one highest. Both are reversal patterns confirmed by a break of the support between the peaks.
