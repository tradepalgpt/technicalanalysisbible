A homing pigeon is a two-candle pattern in a downtrend. A long bearish candle is followed by a smaller bearish candle whose body sits inside the first body. It is like a bullish harami, but both candles are the same colour. It shows selling losing force, and it needs confirmation.

## What is a homing pigeon?

The name comes from the image of a pigeon returning home. Price falls hard on the first day, then on the second day it falls again but only a little, and stays inside the first day's body, as if it has nowhere to go.

It is a relative of the [bullish harami](/bullish-harami/). In that pattern the second candle is bullish. In the homing pigeon the second candle is also bearish, so the sellers are still technically in control, but much less so.

## How it is identified

1. **A downtrend** is in place.
2. **A long bearish candle** with a large body.
3. **A smaller bearish candle** whose open and close both sit inside the body of the first candle. Its open is below the first open and its close is above the first close.

Some sources also require the second candle's shadows to stay within the first candle's range. Rules vary, so check how your platform defines it.

**Worked example.** Candle 1 opens at 120 and closes at 110. Candle 2 opens at 117 and closes at 113, both inside 110 to 120. Both candles are bearish, but the second is much smaller. Its low of 112.5 sits above the low of the first candle at 109.5. This is a homing pigeon.

## How to read it

The second candle has lower prices than the previous day's, if you look at it alone, but it fails to push past the first candle's body. Sellers could not extend the move, which suggests their push is running out.

It is a weak signal. The second candle is still bearish, so the trend has technically continued. Traders usually wait for a bullish candle to follow, closing above the second candle's high, before they treat it as a possible bottom.

## Best market states

**Reversal (best).** After a long decline, particularly near support, it can mark the end of selling pressure.

**Range (weak).** Small candles within larger ones are common and carry little meaning.

**Trend (weak).** A smaller red candle is often just a pause before the next leg down.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear, extended fall |
| First candle | A long bearish body |
| Second candle | Bearish, with a body inside the first |
| Next candle | A bullish candle closing above the second candle's high |
| Location | Near support or a prior low |

## Common mistakes

- Treating it as strong as a bullish engulfing candle. It is far milder.
- Confusing it with a bullish harami, whose second candle is bullish.
- Using it in a sideways market.
- Buying on the second candle without any confirmation.
- Expecting a bearish mirror under this name. There is no standard bearish "homing pigeon".

## FAQ

**How is a homing pigeon different from a bullish harami?**
Both have a small second candle inside a long first candle. In a bullish harami the second candle is bullish. In a homing pigeon it is bearish, so the pattern is more tentative.

**Is it a bullish pattern?**
It is classed as a bullish reversal warning, but only after a decline, and only if the next candle confirms. By itself it shows the selling is slowing.

**Is there a bearish version?**
Not under this name. The nearest mirror idea is a bearish harami, but its second candle has the opposite colour, and no standard pattern name covers two bullish candles in that form.

**Why would a second red candle suggest a bottom?**
Because it makes less progress than the first. Fewer sellers are willing to push price lower, and the market starts to balance.

**How reliable is it?**
It is a rarely discussed, mild pattern. No source gives a dependable edge, so test it on your market before relying on it.
