Intermarket analysis studies how the major markets (stocks, bonds, commodities and currencies) influence each other, and uses the trend in one to judge another. A trader might look at bond yields, the dollar and commodity prices before deciding whether a stock market trend has support. It works as a background filter, not as an entry signal.

## What is intermarket analysis?

No market moves alone. Interest rates affect bonds and stocks. The dollar affects commodity prices priced in dollars. Commodity prices feed into inflation, which feeds back into interest rates. Intermarket analysis tracks these links.

A commonly described set of relationships, which Murphy discussed in his writing, runs like this:

- Bond prices and the dollar have often moved in the same direction.
- The dollar and commodity prices have often moved in opposite directions.
- Commodity prices and bond prices have often moved in opposite directions, because commodity-led inflation tends to push yields up and bond prices down.
- Bond prices have often led stocks, so a trend change in bonds can come before one in stocks.

These are tendencies, not laws. They hold in some periods and weaken or reverse in others. Relationships can also shift from one decade to the next.

The method is a branch of technical analysis, because it uses charts of each market. It is closely related to fundamental ideas about inflation, growth and interest rates.

## Formula

There is no formula. A typical process is:

1. Pick the markets that matter for your trade: for example a stock index, a long-dated bond or yield, a dollar index and a commodity index.
2. Chart each on the same time frame.
3. Mark the trend in each with trendlines or a moving average.
4. Compare the trends with the relationships you expect.
5. Note where the markets agree and where they disagree.

**Worked example.** Suppose a stock index is in an uptrend. Using a simple rule, you mark an uptrend as "price above its 50-day moving average" and a downtrend as "below".

- Stock index above its 50-day average: uptrend
- Bond price above its 50-day average: uptrend
- Dollar index below its 50-day average: downtrend

Two of the three support the stock trend and one is neutral. If the bond market turns down while stocks keep rising, that is a disagreement worth noting.

You can measure how steady a link is with the [correlation coefficient](/correlation-coefficient/).

## How to read it

**Agreement.** When the related markets are moving as expected, the trend in the market you trade has more support.

**Disagreement.** If a market that has often led stocks, such as bonds, turns against the stock trend, it can be an early warning. It is a prompt for caution, not a trigger.

**Regime change.** If a link you rely on stops working, for example stocks and bonds moving together instead of apart, it is a sign that the market's drivers have changed. Update your assumptions.

**Sector rotation.** Intermarket thinking is also used to link the economic cycle to the sectors that tend to lead in each phase.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** Clear trends in several markets make the relationships easier to see.

**Reversals (useful).** Disagreement between markets can mark the early stage of a turn, though many disagreements fade without one.

**Ranges (weak).** In sideways markets the links are noisy and give little guidance.

**Volatility.** In stress periods, many markets move together and normal links can break down.

## Settings

There are no standard settings. The main choices are:

| Choice | Options | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Markets compared | Stocks, bonds or yields, a dollar index, commodities | More markets give a fuller picture but more conflicting signals |
| Trend method | Moving average, trendline, or relative strength | A simple, consistent rule is easier to apply |
| Time frame | Daily, weekly | Weekly charts show the broader link and are less noisy |

Pick one method and keep it fixed. Changing the rule to fit past views makes the analysis meaningless.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the usual relationships as fixed laws.
- Using intermarket links as an entry signal instead of as background.
- Mixing time frames, such as comparing a daily stock chart with a monthly bond chart.
- Ignoring that bond yields and bond prices move in opposite directions, which flips the signal.
- Collecting many markets until something always agrees with the view you already hold.
- Not checking whether the link has held recently.

## FAQ

**What is intermarket analysis?**
It is the study of how the main asset classes (stocks, bonds, commodities and currencies) affect each other. Traders compare trends across them to judge whether a move in one market has support from the rest.

**Who developed intermarket analysis?**
John Murphy popularised the modern approach in his early 1990s book on the subject, and expanded it in 2004. The idea that markets influence each other is older and also appears in economic and fundamental analysis.

**Do intermarket relationships always hold?**
No. They are tendencies that have held in some periods and failed in others. For example, stocks and bonds sometimes move together instead of in opposite directions. Check recent behaviour before relying on a link.

**Is intermarket analysis a trading system?**
Not on its own. It gives background on the wider setting. Most traders use it to decide whether to favour long or short ideas, then use price patterns or indicators for entries and exits.

**Which markets should I compare?**
A common starting set is a stock index, bonds or yields, a dollar index and a commodity index. Add markets that relate to what you trade, such as oil for energy stocks.
