A rising wedge is a chart pattern where price makes higher highs and higher lows, but the two trendlines slope upward and squeeze toward each other. It shows rallies losing strength. It usually ends with a break below the lower line, so it is most often read as bearish, whether it forms after a rise or inside a downtrend.

## What is a rising wedge?

A rising wedge is built from two rising lines that converge. The lower line joins the higher lows. The upper line joins the higher highs. Because the lower line rises faster than the upper one, the range between them narrows over time.

The meaning is in the squeeze. Each new high gains less ground than the one before. Buyers are still in charge, but with less force. When the lower line finally breaks, the narrowing ends.

The pattern can appear in two places. After a long rise, it can mark a top (a reversal). Inside a downtrend, it can be a weak bounce that fails (a continuation of the fall). Either way, the break is usually downward.

No one person is credited with the pattern. It appears in the classic chart-pattern texts and in Thomas Bulkowski's pattern studies.

## How it is drawn

1. **Find two swing highs and two swing lows** that each step higher.
2. **Draw the upper line** through the highs and the **lower line** through the lows.
3. **Check the lines converge.** Both slope up, and the lower line is steeper.
4. **Look for at least five touches** in total (for example three on one line and two on the other), though some traders accept fewer.
5. **Wait for the break:** a close below the lower line.

**Measured target.** A common rule is that price falls back to where the wedge began, the low at its start.

**Worked example.** The wedge starts at a low of 80. It rises to a peak of 100 before the lower line breaks at 97. The measured target is the start of the wedge at 80, a fall of 17 from the break. A stop above the last swing high at 100 puts the risk at 3. The reward is 17 and the risk is 3, so the target sits at about 5.7R, where 1R is the initial risk including spread. In practice, price often falls short of the full target, so many traders plan partial exits.

## How to read it

**The break.** A close below the lower line completes the pattern. A touch or a wick through it is less reliable.

**Retest.** Price often rallies back to the broken line, which can now act as resistance (see [Role reversal](/role-reversal/)). Some traders enter on this retest rather than on the break.

**Volume.** In classic descriptions, volume fades as the wedge narrows and picks up on the break. Rising volume inside the wedge is a warning that buyers may still be strong.

**Divergence.** A momentum indicator that makes lower highs while price makes higher highs adds weight. See [Divergence](/divergence/).

**Failure.** If price breaks upward through the upper line instead, the bearish idea is wrong. A rising wedge that breaks up is a known outcome and should be planned for.

## Best market states

**Reversals after an advance (best).** The pattern is most meaningful at the end of a clear rise, where weakening momentum matters.

**Downtrends (good).** A rising wedge as a bounce inside a falling market often resolves in the direction of the larger trend.

**Strong uptrends (caution).** In a powerful advance, a rising wedge can resolve upward. The larger trend can overpower the pattern.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways action produces many converging lines that mean little.

**Volatility.** Choppy markets produce loose lines and frequent false breaks, so the lines need more tolerance.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Direction | Both lines slope up, the lower one more steeply |
| Touches | At least two on each line |
| Narrowing | Each swing gains less than the one before |
| Break | Close below the lower line, not just a wick |
| Volume | Fading inside the wedge, rising on the break |
| Stop | Above the last swing high (conservative: above the wedge peak) |

## Common mistakes

- Selling inside the wedge before the lower line breaks.
- Drawing lines that do not converge, or forcing a wedge onto a plain uptrend.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Ignoring the larger trend. In a strong advance, an upward break is common.
- Moving the trendlines after the fact so the break appears cleaner.
- Using a stop so tight that normal noise at the top of the wedge hits it.

## FAQ

**Is a rising wedge bullish or bearish?**
It is usually read as bearish, even though price is rising inside it. The narrowing shows each rally gaining less. A break below the lower line is the normal signal. An upward break does happen, so wait for the close.

**How do you trade a rising wedge?**
Many traders wait for a close below the lower line, then enter on the break or on a retest, with a stop above the last swing high. The decision rules should be set before the trade, and each setup should be tested on past data.

**What is the target for a rising wedge?**
A common guide is a return to where the wedge started. Measure from the break down to that low. It is a guide, not a promise, and many patterns fall short, so partial exits are common.

**How is a rising wedge different from an ascending triangle?**
An ascending triangle has a flat top and a rising bottom, and usually breaks up. A rising wedge has both lines sloping up and usually breaks down. See [Ascending triangle](/ascending-triangle/).

**Can a rising wedge break upward?**
Yes. In a strong uptrend, price can push through the upper line. That is why a stop above the wedge, and a plan for the failure, matter.
