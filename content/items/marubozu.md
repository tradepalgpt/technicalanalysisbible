A marubozu is a candlestick with a long body and no wicks, or only very short ones. A bullish marubozu opens at the low and closes at the high; a bearish one does the reverse. It shows one side was in control for the whole period.

## What is a marubozu?

Every candlestick has a body and, usually, wicks (shadows) that show how far price went beyond the open and close. A marubozu has none, or almost none. In Japanese the word is often translated as "bald" or "shaven".

A bullish marubozu opens at its low and closes at its high. Buyers were in control from the first trade to the last, and sellers never managed to push price below the open or pull it off the high. A bearish marubozu is the mirror image, with sellers in control throughout.

That makes it a picture of conviction, the opposite of a [doji](/doji/), which shows indecision. It is widely read as a sign of strength in the direction of the candle.

## How it is identified

A marubozu has:

1. **A long body**, clearly larger than recent candles.
2. **No wicks.** In real markets, a perfect candle is rare, so most traders allow tiny wicks, often under 5% of the candle's range.

Variants are described in Nison's book:

- **Closing marubozu:** no wick at the close end only (a small wick at the open end is allowed).
- **Opening marubozu:** no wick at the open end only.

A bullish candle with no lower wick is also called a [belt hold](/belt-hold/) when it appears after a decline.

**Worked example.** A candle opens at 200.00, trades up to 210.00 and closes at 210.00, with a low of 200.00. Range = 10. Both wicks are 0, so it is a bullish marubozu. If the low had been 199.70, the wick is 0.3, or 3% of the range, which many traders would still accept.

## How to read it

**Within a trend.** A marubozu in the direction of the trend shows the trend is healthy. Strings of them, such as [three white soldiers](/three-white-soldiers/) or [three black crows](/three-black-crows/), can show strong momentum.

**On a breakout.** A marubozu that closes beyond support or resistance shows decisive follow-through, which is more convincing than a candle with a large wick back inside the level.

**After a long move.** A very large marubozu at the end of an extended move can sometimes mark exhaustion, where the last buyers or sellers pile in. This is a minority view and needs other evidence.

**Mid-body support.** Some traders treat the middle of a marubozu body as a support or resistance level for later pullbacks. This is a common convention, not a proven rule.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** It shows continued control by one side.

**Breakouts (good).** A full-bodied close through a level is stronger than a candle that finishes with a long wick.

**Ranges and reversals (weak).** Inside a range, a single strong candle often fades. At reversals, a marubozu is not a reversal pattern by itself.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Size | A body clearly larger than recent candles, for example against an average true range |
| Wicks | None or tiny, using the same tolerance each time |
| Context | In trend direction or through a key level |
| Volume | Higher volume adds weight (where volume is reliable) |
| Follow-through | The next candle does not give back the whole body |

## Common mistakes

- Treating every large candle as a marubozu when it has meaningful wicks.
- Chasing a marubozu after a huge move, when the entry is far from any sensible stop.
- Assuming a marubozu predicts a reversal. It mainly shows strength.
- Ignoring news. Single large candles are often driven by data releases and can reverse quickly.
- Using different wick tolerances on different charts.

## FAQ

**What does a marubozu mean?**
It means one side controlled the whole period. A bullish marubozu has buyers in charge from open to close; a bearish one has sellers in charge. It is read as a sign of strength in that direction.

**Is a marubozu a reversal or continuation signal?**
Mainly a sign of strength, so it is more often used for trend continuation and breakouts. Some traders see an oversized marubozu after a long move as exhaustion, but that needs other evidence.

**Does a marubozu have to have no wicks at all?**
Strictly yes, but real candles rarely do. Most traders allow tiny wicks, for example under about 5% of the range. Pick a tolerance and use it consistently.

**What is the difference between a marubozu and a belt hold?**
A belt hold is a long candle opening at an extreme, with no wick on the open side, and it appears after a trend as a possible reversal. A marubozu has no wicks on either side. See [Belt hold](/belt-hold/).

**How often does a marubozu appear?**
Perfect ones are rare, especially on liquid markets. On lower time frames it is more common to see near-marubozu candles with tiny wicks. Daily and higher charts give clearer signals.
