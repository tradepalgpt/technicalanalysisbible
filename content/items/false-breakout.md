A false breakout is when price moves beyond a support or resistance level and then returns back inside the old range, instead of continuing. The traders who bought the break are caught on the wrong side. Others trade the failure itself, entering against the break with a stop just beyond the extreme.

## What is a false breakout?

Markets often stop at obvious levels such as a range high. Sometimes price pushes through, triggers breakout buyers and stop orders, then runs out of buying and drops back inside. The breakout was "false" because it did not lead to a lasting move.

Edwards and Magee (1948) and Murphy (1999) both warn that breakouts can fail, and suggest waiting for a close beyond the level, a minimum percentage move or a volume increase to filter weak ones. Al Brooks (2012) argues that most breakouts of trading ranges fail and reverse, and that traders should expect failure as often as success. No published figure applies to every market, and none is quoted here.

There is no single originator of the idea. "Fakeout", "bull trap" and "bear trap" are everyday trader terms for the same behaviour.

## How it is identified

1. **Mark the level.** A clear range top or bottom, or a prior swing. See [Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).
2. **Wait for a break.** Price trades or closes beyond the level.
3. **Look for the return.** Within a few bars (1 to 3 is typical), price closes back inside the range.
4. **Mark the extreme.** The high (or low) of the failed move becomes the stop reference.

**Worked example.** A range has resistance at 100 and support at 90. Price spikes to 102, then a bar closes at 98. This is a close back inside the range after a break. A trader selling at 97.5 with a stop at 102.5 risks 5 points. The first target is the middle of the range at 95, which is 2.5 points away, and the other side of the range at 90.5 is 7 points away.

**Variants.** A wick-only break that closes inside on the same bar is the quickest form; see [Pin bar](/pin-bar/). A break of an inside bar's range that fails is the [fakey pattern](/fakey-pattern/). The same behaviour at swing highs and lows is often called a [liquidity sweep](/liquidity-sweep/).

## How to read it

**Close back inside.** The key event. A bar closing back inside the range turns a possible breakout into a possible failure.

**Speed.** Fast returns, within one to three bars, are usually treated as stronger failures than slow ones.

**Volume.** A break on low volume that reverses is often read as weak. A break on high volume that reverses can be a climax. Neither is a rule. See [Volume](/volume/).

**Target.** The common targets are the mid-point of the range and the opposite side. The far side is a larger reward but takes longer.

**Failed failure.** If price pushes back through the extreme after the short entry, the failure signal itself has failed. The stop exists for this case.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** Price rotates between known edges, so a failed break gives a defined target and stop.

**Reversals at major levels (good).** A failed break of a long-standing high with a sharp rejection can mark a top.

**Strong trends (weak).** In a strong trend, breaks tend to continue. Fading them is a counter-trend trade with less chance.

**Volatile markets.** News can create sharp breaks and reversals on both sides, making the signal harder to read.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Level | A clear and tested range edge or swing |
| Break | Price trades beyond the level |
| Return | A bar closes back inside, within a few bars |
| Strength | The return bar is strong, with a close well inside |
| Trigger | A break of the return bar's low (for a short) |
| Stop | Beyond the false-break extreme, with room for spread |

## Common mistakes

- Fading every break. In a trend, most breaks work and fading them loses money.
- Selling the first touch above resistance before any return. Wait for the close back inside.
- Using a very tight stop inside the spike. The retest of the extreme often shakes such stops out.
- Marking a level that was never a real level.
- Taking the full range as a target and ignoring the mid-point.
- Not accepting that a break can turn out to be real. The stop is part of the trade.

## FAQ

**What is a false breakout?**
It is a move beyond a support or resistance level that does not hold. Price returns inside the old range, often within a few bars. Breakout traders are caught on the wrong side, and others trade the reversal.

**How do you avoid false breakouts?**
You cannot avoid them completely. Common filters are waiting for a candle close beyond the level, requiring a retest that holds, checking volume, and trading in the direction of the higher time frame trend. Each filter costs some good trades too.

**How do you trade a false breakout?**
Wait for price to close back inside the range, then enter against the failed break with a stop beyond its extreme. Targets are usually the range middle and the other side. Make sure the reward is larger than the risk.

**Is a false breakout the same as a bull trap?**
A bull trap is a false breakout above resistance that traps buyers. A bear trap is a false breakout below support that traps sellers. "False breakout" and "fakeout" cover both.

**How is a false breakout different from a liquidity sweep?**
They describe nearly the same price behaviour. "Liquidity sweep" comes from smart money language and stresses stop orders resting beyond swings. "False breakout" is the older term and focuses on the range level that failed.
