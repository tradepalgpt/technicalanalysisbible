A Fibonacci projection, also called an expansion, uses three points on a chart to estimate where a move might end. It measures the first swing, then copies fractions of that size (61.8%, 100%, 161.8%) from the end of the pullback. Traders use the projected prices mainly as profit targets in trends. It has no academic evidence base.

## What is a Fibonacci projection?

A [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/) looks back: it measures how much of a move a pullback has given back. A projection looks forward: it takes the size of the first swing and adds it onto the end of the pullback to estimate how far the next swing could go.

It needs three points. Point 1 is the start of the move, point 2 is the end of the first swing, and point 3 is the end of the pullback. The distance from 1 to 2 is the base. Levels are then measured from point 3.

The 100% level means the second swing equals the first. This is the same idea as the [measured move](/measured-move/) and the [AB=CD pattern](/abcd-pattern/). The 161.8% level, linked to the [golden ratio](/golden-ratio/), is often used as a more ambitious target.

Do not confuse it with the [Fibonacci extension](/fibonacci-extension/). Many platforms use the two names loosely. Strictly, an extension uses two points and extends beyond the end of a swing, while a projection uses three and measures from the pullback. Always check which tool your platform draws.

## How it is drawn

1. Pick a clear swing in the trend from point 1 to point 2.
2. Find the pullback and mark its end as point 3.
3. Use the platform's three-point tool (often called "trend-based Fibonacci extension"). Click points 1, 2 and 3 in order.
4. The tool draws horizontal levels at set ratios of the 1 to 2 distance, starting from point 3.

**Worked example (uptrend).** Point 1 is at 100 and point 2 at 150, so the first swing is 50 points. The pullback ends at point 3 = 130.
- 61.8% level: 130 + 0.618 x 50 = **160.9**
- 100% level: 130 + 50 = **180**
- 161.8% level: 130 + 1.618 x 50 = **210.9**

In a downtrend the levels are measured downwards from point 3.

## How to read it

**Targets in layers.** Many traders pick a first target at 61.8% or 100%, then trail a stop for the rest. The 100% level is the most commonly watched.

**Zones.** Price rarely stops at an exact number. Treat each level as a zone and watch for a reaction such as a reversal candle.

**Overlap with other levels.** A projection level that matches an old swing high or a round number is often treated as a stronger target. See [Fibonacci confluence](/fibonacci-confluence/).

**Failure.** If price blows through 161.8% easily, the trend is strong and the tool has little more to say. If price turns well short of 61.8%, the move may be weakening.

**Shape of the pullback.** A shallow pullback (point 3 close to point 2) puts the projected levels closer to the current price, so targets may be hit sooner.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The tool assumes a second leg of similar size to the first, which suits a trend that moves in steps.

**Ranges (weak).** In a range price does not extend; it returns. Projected targets are rarely reached.

**Reversals (not suited).** The tool is built for continuation. For turning points other tools such as retracements or [chart patterns](/head-and-shoulders/) are more common.

**Volatility.** In very volatile markets swing sizes change quickly, so the projected levels are less stable.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Clean swings | Points 1, 2 and 3 are obvious on the chart |
| Pullback depth | Pullback is a normal one, not a full reversal of the first swing |
| Level overlap | Projected level matches another level such as resistance |
| Trend intact | Price is still making higher highs (or lower lows) |
| Reaction | A stall or reversal signal near the projected level |
| Plan | Decide the target and stop before entering |

## Common mistakes

- Mixing up projection and extension and measuring from the wrong point.
- Choosing swing points after the fact so the target looks neat.
- Treating a projected level as a prediction rather than an area to watch.
- Using it in a sideways market with no trend to project.
- Taking all profit at one level without a plan for a stronger move.
- Forgetting that the target does not change the risk: a stop is still needed.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between Fibonacci projection and extension?**
Strictly, an extension uses two points and extends past the end of a swing, while a projection uses three points and measures from the end of the pullback. Many platforms and writers use the terms interchangeably, so check how your tool is drawn.

**Which Fibonacci projection levels are used?**
The common levels are 61.8%, 100%, 127.2% and 161.8% of the first swing. Some traders add 200% and 261.8%. There is no proof any one level is better than another.

**How do I use a Fibonacci projection for profit targets?**
Draw it across the first swing and pullback, then pick one or two projected levels as targets. Many traders take part profit at the first level and trail a stop on the remainder. Decide the plan before entering.

**Is Fibonacci projection accurate?**
There is no academic evidence it predicts price. Levels can act as areas where traders pay attention, and some moves do stall there, but many do not. Treat it as a planning aid, not a forecast.

**Can I use it in a downtrend?**
Yes. Draw it the same way from the first down swing and the pullback high. The levels are then measured downwards from point 3.
