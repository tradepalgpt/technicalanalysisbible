A tweezer top is a two-candle pattern at the end of an uptrend. Both candles reach the same high, and the second candle turns down. The matching highs show that price was rejected twice at the same level. It is a warning of a possible turn lower, and it works best when it forms at resistance.

## What are tweezer tops?

The name comes from the shape, like the two prongs of a pair of tweezers pointing up. Price rises to a high on the first candle. On the second candle it tries again, reaches the same high, and fails. The second failure suggests that sellers are defending the level.

Tweezer patterns have no strict official definition. Traders disagree about whether the two candles must have particular colours or sizes, or whether the highs need to match exactly. Because of this, results from different studies and books are hard to compare.

## How it is identified

A tweezer top has:

1. **A prior uptrend.** Without a rise there is nothing for the pattern to reverse.
2. **A first candle that continues the trend.** It is usually bullish, often with a solid body.
3. **A second candle with the same high.** The highs are equal or nearly equal. How close is a personal rule, for example within a small fraction of the average candle range.
4. **A second candle that turns down.** It is usually bearish. A bearish second candle that closes near or below the first candle's open makes a stronger signal.

**Worked example.** After a rise, candle one opens at 50, reaches a high of 55 and closes at 54. Candle two opens at 54, reaches 55 again and closes at 51. Both highs are 55, and the second candle closed well below its open. This is a tweezer top with resistance at 55.

The two candles do not need to be neighbours in a strict sense, but most traders count only candles that sit directly next to each other.

## How to read it

**Rejection of a level.** Two attempts failing at the same price shows that sellers are active there. The high becomes a reference point: the pattern is wrong if price later closes above it.

**Location matters.** A tweezer top at a known [resistance](/support-and-resistance/) level, a prior high or a round number is more meaningful than one in open space.

**The second candle.** A long bearish second candle, or one that closes back inside the first candle's body, shows stronger selling than a small one.

**Next candle.** Many traders wait for a third candle that closes lower before acting. This also gives a clear stop level at the shared high.

**Similar patterns.** A tweezer top can overlap with a [bearish engulfing](/bearish-engulfing/), a [dark cloud cover](/dark-cloud-cover/) or a [shooting star](/shooting-star/). Where it overlaps, the stronger pattern is usually named instead.

## Best market states

**End of an uptrend (best).** A clear run-up followed by a rejection gives the pattern a story.

**At resistance (good).** Matching highs at an existing ceiling carry more weight than isolated ones.

**Strong trends (caution).** A pause at a high can be followed by a new high. One pair of matching highs is not enough on its own.

**Ranges (weak).** In sideways markets matching highs are common and mean little unless they sit at the top of the range.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear rise into the pattern |
| Matching highs | Highs equal or within a tight, consistent tolerance |
| Second candle | Bearish, ideally closing below the first candle's midpoint |
| Level | A known resistance, prior high or round number |
| Follow-through | A third candle closing below the pattern's low |
| Volume | Rising volume on the turn adds weight where volume is reliable |
| Stop | Above the shared high |

## Common mistakes

- Seeing a tweezer top in a sideways market where there is no trend to reverse.
- Insisting on exactly equal highs, or accepting highs so far apart the pattern means nothing.
- Acting on the pattern alone, without a level or a confirming candle.
- Ignoring the second candle: a small or bullish second candle is a weak signal.
- Using it on very short time frames where matching highs appear constantly.
- Confusing it with a [double top](/double-top/), which forms over a longer time with a trough between two peaks.

## Pairs well with

- **[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/)**: the pattern is far more useful at a level that has held before.
- **[RSI](/rsi/)**: an overbought reading or bearish divergence at the second high strengthens the warning.
- **[Volume](/volume/)**: heavy volume on the rejection suggests real selling.
- **[Divergence](/divergence/)**: weaker momentum at the second high supports the reversal idea.

## FAQ

**What is a tweezer top?**
It is a two-candle pattern where both candles share the same high after an uptrend, and the second turns down. The matching highs show a price level that buyers failed to break twice, which can warn of a reversal.

**Is a tweezer top bullish or bearish?**
It is a bearish reversal pattern. It forms at the end of an uptrend and suggests that sellers are taking control. Its mirror image, the tweezer bottom, is the bullish version at the end of a downtrend.

**Do the two candles have to be different colours?**
Not by strict rule, but the usual textbook form has a bullish first candle and a bearish second one. A bearish second candle shows that the rejection was decisive, so it is the more convincing version.

**How exact do the highs need to match?**
There is no standard. Many traders allow a small tolerance, such as a fraction of the average candle range. Pick a rule and keep to it, so that results can be compared fairly.

**How reliable is a tweezer top?**
There is no solid evidence base that makes it work on its own. It is a simple warning about price rejection, and it is best used with a level, a trend and a confirming candle.
