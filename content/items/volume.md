Volume is the number of shares, contracts or lots traded in a period. A volume bar under each price bar shows how much trading took place. Traders use it to judge whether a move has broad participation: a breakout or trend on heavy volume is usually seen as more convincing than one on thin volume.

## What is volume?

Volume counts how much of a market changed hands during each bar. On a stock or futures chart, it is the number of shares or contracts traded. A bar with twice the usual volume means twice as much activity.

Volume does not say who was buying and who was selling. Every trade has both a buyer and a seller, so heavy volume on a rising bar does not prove buyers were "stronger". What it does show is how many people took part, which helps judge how much weight to give a price move. This is the basis of Charles Dow's old principle that volume should confirm the trend.

Other indicators are built from volume, such as [On-Balance Volume](/on-balance-volume/), [VWAP](/vwap/) and the [volume profile](/volume-profile/). This page covers reading raw volume bars.

**Forex note.** Spot forex has no central exchange, so there is no single true volume figure. Forex charts show tick volume: the number of price changes in a bar. See [tick volume](/tick-volume/). Tick volume tracks real volume reasonably in many conditions, but it is not the same thing. Futures volume (for example currency futures) is real exchange volume.

## Formula

Volume is not a formula, but a few related measures are common.

1. **Raw volume**: the total traded in the bar.
2. **Volume average**: a simple average of the last 20 bars of volume, drawn over the bars as a line. Bars above it are "above average".
3. **Relative volume**: today's volume divided by the average. See [relative volume](/relative-volume/).

**Worked example.** The 20-bar average volume is 50,000 shares. Today's bar shows 120,000 shares.
Relative volume = 120,000 / 50,000 = **2.4**, so today is 2.4 times the normal activity.

## How to read it

**Breakouts.** A close beyond support or resistance on high volume is read as stronger. A breakout on weak volume is often called suspect, because few traders took part.

**Trends.** In a healthy uptrend volume tends to rise on advances and fall on pullbacks. If rallies keep making new highs on shrinking volume, the move may be tiring.

**Climaxes.** A very large volume spike after a long move can mark exhaustion, when the last buyers or sellers pile in. See [volume climax](/volume-climax/). It is a warning, not a certain turn.

**Reversal patterns.** Patterns such as [head and shoulders](/head-and-shoulders/) are usually described with falling volume across the formation and a rise on the break.

**Low-volume periods.** Quiet holiday sessions or the middle of the day often give thin trading, where price moves are easier to push and less reliable.

**Context matters.** Volume has daily patterns: most markets are busiest near the open and close. Compare a bar with the same time of day, not with the average of the whole day.

## Best market states

**Breakouts (best).** Volume is most often used to judge if a break from a range or pattern is real.

**Trends (good).** Rising volume with the trend and falling volume on pullbacks supports it.

**Reversals (good).** Heavy volume at a turning point, or divergence between price and volume, can warn that a move is ending.

**Ranges (weak).** Within a sideways range, volume is noisy and says little about direction.

**Volatility.** News spikes bring large volume for a short time. A single huge bar can distort the average for weeks.

## Settings

| Setting | Values | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Volume average | 20 bars (common) | Shows what "normal" volume is. Shorter is more reactive; 50 gives a slower baseline |
| Colouring | Up bar / down bar | Colours the volume by the direction of the price bar |
| Volume type | Real or tick | Real volume on stocks and futures; tick volume on spot forex and some CFDs |

There is no standard length for the average. Any number is a convention that you should test on your own market.

## Common mistakes

- Believing high volume on a down bar proves "sellers won". Both sides trade every share.
- Comparing volume across different markets or sessions without adjusting.
- Using tick volume from forex as if it were exchange volume.
- Treating one big bar as a signal without checking the context.
- Expecting volume to predict direction by itself. It shows participation, not intent.
- Ignoring that daily volume figures on some data feeds are revised later.

## FAQ

**What does high volume mean?**
It means many shares or contracts traded in the bar, so more people took part. It adds weight to a move such as a breakout. It does not say whether buyers or sellers were in control, because every trade has both.

**Does volume matter in forex?**
Spot forex has no central volume figure. Charts show tick volume, which counts price changes. It is a decent stand-in for activity but is not true volume. Currency futures have real exchange volume.

**Is low volume bullish or bearish?**
Neither. Low volume shows low participation. A rise on low volume is often treated with suspicion because few traders took part, but it can still continue. Judge it against price structure.

**How do you confirm a breakout with volume?**
Compare the breakout bar's volume with the recent average, such as the last 20 bars. A reading well above it is taken as stronger confirmation. It is a guide, as many breakouts on heavy volume still fail.

**What is the difference between volume and open interest?**
Volume is how many contracts traded in a period. Open interest is how many futures or options contracts remain open at a point in time. Volume resets each period; open interest carries forward.
