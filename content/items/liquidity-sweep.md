A liquidity sweep is a brief move beyond an obvious swing high or low, where many stop orders are thought to rest, followed by a quick return. Traders who use the term read the move as price taking those orders and then reversing. It is a smart money concepts (SMC) and ICT idea, not from academic research.

## What is a liquidity sweep?

"Liquidity" here means resting orders. Traders who go long often place their stops just below a recent swing low. Traders who short often put stops above a swing high. Breakout traders place buy orders above resistance. These clusters mean there are many orders at obvious prices.

Smart money concept teachers say large players need that liquidity to fill big orders, so price is drawn to those areas. A sweep is the move that reaches them: price pokes beyond the swing, triggers the orders, then reverses. The move below a low is a "sell-side" sweep and above a high is a "buy-side" sweep.

This is trader language, and the claim about who is doing the sweeping is a belief, not something chart data can prove. What can be observed is simple: price often spikes past obvious levels and then returns. Older books describe the same shape under other names. Edwards and Magee (1948) and Murphy (1999) describe false breakouts, and Al Brooks (2012) discusses failed breakouts at swing points. The term "liquidity sweep" itself has no academic source.

## How it is identified

1. **Mark an obvious level.** A clear swing high or low, or equal highs or lows. See [Swing highs and swing lows](/swing-highs-lows/) and [Equal highs and equal lows](/equal-highs-lows/).
2. **Wait for price to trade beyond it.** Usually a wick or a short spike.
3. **Look for the return.** Price closes back inside the old range, usually on the same bar or within a few bars.
4. **Look for a structure break.** Many traders also want a break of the last swing in the opposite direction before entering.

**Worked example.** Two swing lows sit at 100 and 100.2. Price drops to 99.2, then rallies and closes the bar at 101. The low at 99.2 went beyond both lows and closed back above them. This is a sell-side sweep. A trader may buy at 101.5 with a stop at 98.9, below the sweep low, risking 2.6 points. If the last lower high was at 104, that target gives about 1 to 1; the trader may aim for the next level beyond.

**Compared with a false breakout.** The shapes are nearly the same. "Liquidity sweep" is used when the move is short and sharp at a prior swing. "False breakout" is the older term for a range level that fails.

## How to read it

**The level matters.** The more obvious the swing and the more times it was touched, the more orders are assumed to sit beyond it. Equal highs and lows are the most-cited examples.

**Speed of the return.** A fast return, within a bar or two, is read as a cleaner sweep. A slow grind back is less clear.

**Structure break.** After a sell-side sweep, a break above the last lower high, called a market structure shift or change of character by some teachers, is the usual confirmation. See [Break of structure](/break-of-structure/).

**Time frame.** A sweep on the daily chart carries more weight than one on a one-minute chart, which is full of small sweeps.

**Failure.** If price closes beyond the swing and stays out, the move was a breakout, not a sweep.

## Best market states

**Reversals at obvious levels (best).** The idea is aimed at turning points after a run into a prior high or low.

**Ranges (good).** The range edges are the obvious levels, and sweeps of them are common.

**Strong trends (weak).** In trends, breaks of swings continue. Calling each one a sweep and trading against it loses money.

**News events.** Spikes beyond levels at news times can look like sweeps but are volatility, not a setup.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Level | An obvious, tested swing or equal highs or lows |
| Move beyond | A wick or spike through the level |
| Close | A close back inside the old range |
| Structure | A break of the last opposing swing after the sweep |
| Context | Agrees with the higher time frame, or the trade is counter-trend by choice |
| Stop | Beyond the sweep extreme, with room for spread |

## Common mistakes

- Labelling every wick as a sweep. Without an obvious level, it is just a wick.
- Entering at the first touch beyond the level, before any return.
- Treating the theory about who is hunting stops as fact. Trade what the chart does.
- Ignoring the trend. In a strong trend, the sweep is usually followed by continuation.
- Placing the stop inside the spike.
- Using a one-minute sweep to take a trade against a daily trend without good reason.

## FAQ

**What is a liquidity sweep?**
It is a short move past an obvious swing high or low, where stop orders are thought to rest, followed by a return back inside. Traders who use the term read it as a possible reversal setup.

**Is a liquidity sweep the same as a stop hunt?**
Traders use the terms for the same price behaviour. "Stop hunt" suggests someone deliberately targets stops. "Liquidity sweep" is the more neutral phrase. Whether anyone is hunting is a belief, not something a chart can show.

**Is liquidity sweep an official technical analysis term?**
No. It comes from smart money concepts and ICT-style trader education. The behaviour it describes, failed breakouts at obvious levels, is covered in older books under other names.

**How do you tell a sweep from a real breakout?**
Closes tell you. A sweep closes back inside the old range, often quickly. A real breakout closes beyond the level and holds there. Even so, you only know afterwards, which is why a stop is needed.

**Do liquidity sweeps work?**
Sometimes. Many failed breakouts at obvious levels do reverse, but many do not, and no reliable win rate is published. Test the setup on your own market and time frame, and use a stop beyond the sweep.
