An opening range breakout (ORB) is a day-trading method that marks the high and low of the first minutes of a session, then trades a break of either edge. Buyers enter on a move above the high, and sellers on a move below the low. The stop usually sits at the other side of the range.

## What is an opening range breakout?

The first minutes of a session are often busy. Orders built up overnight meet the market, and news is absorbed. The high and low of that early window define the opening range. The idea is that when price leaves this range, it shows a direction that the market may follow for the rest of the day.

The method is most often linked to Toby Crabel, whose 1990 book *Day Trading with Short Term Price Patterns and Opening Range Breakout* covers opening range ideas and related short-term patterns. Many versions exist and the length of the opening range is a choice, not a standard.

Crabel's work is about the idea of an opening range as a reference for the day. Other traders have since made many variations, so the exact rules in use online may differ from the original. The method has no guarantee, and it is known to give many false breaks.

## How it is drawn

1. **Choose the window.** Common choices are the first 5, 15 or 30 minutes after the market opens.
2. **Mark the high and the low** of that window once it ends.
3. **Set the trigger.** A break above the high is a long signal; a break below the low is a short signal. Many traders require a close beyond the level rather than a touch.
4. **Set the stop.** The opposite side of the range, or the middle of the range for a tighter stop.
5. **Set the target.** Common choices are a multiple of the range width, a prior high or low, or the end of the session.

**Worked example.** A share trades between 49.80 and 50.40 in its first 30 minutes, a range of 0.60. A close above 50.40 triggers a long entry. The stop goes at 49.80, a risk of 0.60. A target of one times the range would be 51.00, and two times would be 51.60.

## How to read it

**Break with volume.** A move beyond the range on strong volume suggests real participation. A break on thin volume is more likely to fail.

**Narrow versus wide.** A narrow opening range, compared with recent days, often leads to larger moves after the break. A very wide range leaves little room to the target, since the stop is far away.

**Direction filter.** Many traders only take breaks in the direction of the larger trend or the daily bias, for example only longs above the previous day's close.

**Failed breaks.** If price breaks one side and quickly falls back into the range, it may head for the other side. See [false breakout](/false-breakout/).

**Time limit.** Breaks later in the day often have less follow-through. Some traders skip the signal after a set time, such as midday.

## Best market states

**Breakouts and volatile days (best).** News days, earnings days and days with a gap and heavy volume give the method the most room.

**Ranges (weak).** On quiet days, price chops back and forth across the edges and the stop is hit on both sides.

**Trends (mixed).** Breaks in the direction of a strong higher time frame trend tend to be favoured.

**Volatility.** The method needs enough movement to pay for the range and any costs. In very low volatility, costs and spreads can consume the result.

| Context | Agrees with the daily trend or the [previous day's high or low](/previous-day-high-low/) |
| Stop | Opposite side of the range, or a fixed share of it |
| Cut-off | No new entries late in the session |

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the window length after looking at which one "worked" on past data.
- Trading both directions without limit, taking a loss on each side.
- Ignoring costs and spreads, which matter much more for short-term methods.
- Entering on a wick through the range edge instead of waiting for a close.
- Using the same rules in a market with different opening hours or thin early trading.
- Taking breaks against a strong trend with no extra evidence.

## FAQ

**What is the opening range?**
It is the high and low of the first part of the trading session, commonly the first 5, 15 or 30 minutes. Traders mark those two prices and watch for a break of either one.

**How long should the opening range be?**
There is no standard length. Five, fifteen and thirty minutes are all used. Shorter windows give earlier signals but more false breaks, and longer windows give fewer but later signals. Test your choice before using it with real money.

**Where do you put the stop on an opening range breakout?**
Most traders place it at the opposite side of the range, since a return through the range shows the break has failed. A tighter stop at the middle of the range lowers the risk but is hit more often.

**Does the opening range breakout work?**
It can, but results depend on the market, the window and costs, and many breaks fail. Published claims of high success rates should be treated with care. Testing on your own market with realistic costs is the only fair check.

**Can I use it outside stock markets?**
Yes, on any market with a clear session open, such as index futures. In forex, which trades around the clock, traders often mark the opening range of a major session such as London or New York.
