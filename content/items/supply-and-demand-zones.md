Supply and demand zones are price areas, drawn as boxes rather than lines, where a strong move began. A demand zone sits where buyers overwhelmed sellers and price rose sharply. A supply zone sits where sellers overwhelmed buyers and price fell. Traders watch for price to return to the zone and react again.

## What are supply and demand zones?

The idea is that a sharp move leaves behind unfilled orders. If price shot upward from a tight pause, large buyers probably did not finish buying, and they may buy again if price returns to that area. The same logic applies in reverse for a sharp fall.

A zone differs from a classic support or resistance line in two ways. It is an area with a top and a bottom, not a thin line. It is also drawn from the origin of a strong move, not from repeated touches. A zone can be valid after only one visit, and it is often considered weaker the more times price has returned to it, because each visit uses up some of the waiting orders.

The method is often credited to traders who taught it in courses and online, with Sam Seiden a frequently named figure. There is no single original source, and the explanation about unfilled institutional orders is a theory, not something a price chart can prove. Treat it as a way to organise the chart, not as proof of what big players are doing.

## How it is drawn

1. **Find a strong move.** Look for a sharp, impulsive rise or fall, with large candles and little overlap.
2. **Mark the base.** This is the small pause or cluster of candles just before the move started.
3. **Draw the box.** The top and bottom of the base set the zone. Some traders use the candle bodies for one edge and the wicks for the other.
4. **Extend it to the right.** Wait for price to come back.
5. **Retire it** once price closes clearly through it.

**Worked example.** Price sits between 100.0 and 100.6 for several bars, then rises quickly to 104.0. The base is 100.0 to 100.6, and that is the demand zone. If price falls back to 100.4 a week later and buyers step in, the zone has held. A close at 99.5 would show it has failed.

## How to read it

**Fresh zones.** A zone price has not returned to is called fresh. Many traders prefer fresh zones, since no orders have been used.

**Strength of the departure.** The sharper and faster the move away, the more significant the zone is generally seen to be.

**First return.** The first retest is the one most traders watch. Later returns tend to be treated with less trust.

**Zone failure.** A close beyond the far edge means the zone no longer works. A failed demand zone can turn into supply, much like [role reversal](/role-reversal/) for lines.

**Higher time frame context.** A demand zone that sits inside a larger uptrend or at a major higher time frame level carries more weight than a lone zone in the middle of a range.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** Zones at the top and bottom of a range show clear places to look for reactions, with a stop placed just outside.

**Reversals (good).** A zone at the end of a long move, with a sign of exhaustion, is a common reversal setup.

**Trends (weak to mixed).** In a strong trend, zones against the trend are often run through. Zones that form within a trend and point with it, such as a demand zone in an uptrend, are used for pullback entries.

**Volatility.** Zones are wider in volatile markets. Using [ATR](/atr/) to judge whether a zone is too large for a sensible stop can help.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Departure | Strong, fast move away from the base |
| Freshness | Price has not yet returned to the zone |
| Context | Zone sits with the higher time frame trend or at a major level |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or quick turn when price enters the zone |
| Stop | Just beyond the far edge of the zone |
| Failure | A close through the zone ends the idea |

## Common mistakes

- Drawing zones on every small move, so the chart fills with boxes.
- Making zones so wide that the stop distance ruins the trade's risk.
- Treating a zone as certain to hold. Many zones fail.
- Ignoring the trend: buying a demand zone in a strong downtrend often fails.
- Using different drawing rules for each zone, so the method cannot be tested.
- Believing the institutional-order story as fact. It is an explanation, not evidence.

## FAQ

**What is a supply and demand zone?**
It is a price area, drawn as a box, where price left sharply. A demand zone marks where price rose away and a supply zone marks where it fell away. Traders watch for a return to the zone as a possible place for another reaction.

**How is it different from support and resistance?**
Support and resistance are usually thin lines drawn through repeated turning points. A zone is a wider area drawn around the base of a sharp move and can matter after a single visit. See [support and resistance vs supply and demand](/compare/support-and-resistance-vs-supply-and-demand/).

**How do I draw a zone?**
Find a sharp move, mark the small pause just before it, and draw a box around that pause. Extend the box to the right. Pick one rule for the edges, such as bodies and wicks, and use it every time.

**Do zones weaken after being touched?**
Many traders think so, because each visit may use up orders waiting there. Others find zones work on later visits too. Test it on your own market rather than assuming. Fresh zones are simply easier to define and judge.

**Is a supply and demand zone the same as an order block?**
They are related but not the same. An order block is a narrower idea from smart money concepts, usually a single candle before a move. See [order block vs supply and demand](/compare/order-block-vs-supply-and-demand/).
