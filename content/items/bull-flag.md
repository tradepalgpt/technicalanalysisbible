A bull flag is a short continuation pattern in an uptrend. A sharp rise (the flagpole) is followed by a brief, shallow drift downward inside parallel lines (the flag). A close above the top of the flag suggests the advance may resume.

## What is a bull flag pattern?

A bull flag is a pause inside a fast rise. Price jumps up in a steep, near-vertical move: the flagpole. Buyers then take a breather and some holders take profit, so price drifts down or sideways in a narrow, tilted channel: the flag. The channel is bounded by two roughly parallel lines. When buyers return and price closes above the upper line, the pattern is complete.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, describe flags as short-lived formations that appear during rapid moves, with the flag typically sloping against the prevailing trend. They also note that flags tend to form quickly, usually within a few weeks on daily charts. A flag that drags on for a long time is losing its meaning.

A bull flag is similar to a [pennant](/pennant/), except that the pennant's lines converge while the flag's stay parallel.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **Flagpole:** a sharp, steep rise on strong volume.
2. **Flag:** a short consolidation after the pole, drifting slightly down (or sideways), between two roughly parallel lines.
3. **Upper line:** drawn through the flag's highs. The lower line runs through its lows.
4. **Breakout:** a close above the upper line.

The flag should be short compared with the pole and should retrace only a modest part of it. A deep retracement suggests the pole was not a strong move.

**Measured target.** Measure the length of the flagpole, from the start of the sharp rise to its top. Add that distance to the breakout point. Edwards and Magee describe flags as forming around the half-way point of a move, which is why they are called half-mast patterns. This is where the measuring idea comes from.

**Worked example.** The pole runs from 40 to 50: a length of 10. The flag drifts down to 47 and then price closes above the upper line at 49. The measured target is 49 + 10 = **59**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Breakout.** A close above the upper line completes the pattern. Many traders wait for a close rather than a brief wick, to reduce false breaks.

**Volume.** The usual picture is heavy volume on the pole, falling volume through the flag and a rise on the breakout. Falling volume in the flag suggests the pullback is a pause, not a reversal.

**Depth of the flag.** A shallow pullback in the upper part of the pole is generally read as stronger than a deep one.

**Retest.** Price sometimes returns to the broken upper line, which may now act as support. Some traders enter on that retest.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks below the lower line of the flag instead, the continuation idea has failed. Because the pattern is short, the stop is usually close, which keeps the risk per trade small relative to the target but makes the stop easy to hit in noisy markets.

## Best market states

**Strong, fast uptrends (best).** The pattern needs a sharp pole. It is a pause in momentum, not a bottom.

**Breakouts.** The flag line gives a clear trigger level.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market there is no pole, so a drifting channel is just a range.

**Slow trends (weak).** A gentle uptrend with small pullbacks does not produce a convincing pole, so many "flags" in such markets are guesses.

**Volatility.** In very volatile markets the flag lines are wide and the stop distances grow.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Pole | Sharp, steep rise on strong volume |
| Flag | Short, shallow and slightly downward or sideways |
| Lines | Roughly parallel |
| Volume | Fading inside the flag, rising on the break |
| Break | Close above the upper line, not just a wick |
| Stop | Below the flag's low (tighter: below the lower line) |

## Common mistakes

- Calling any small pullback a flag without a clear, sharp pole.
- Letting the flag run too long. A long consolidation is no longer a flag.
- Drawing lines through a flag that is too deep, which suggests a reversal rather than a pause.
- Entering inside the flag before the breakout.
- Treating the pole-length target as certain.
- Ignoring the higher-timeframe trend.

## FAQ

**Is a bull flag bullish or bearish?**
It is bullish. It forms after a sharp rise, and a close above the flag suggests the advance may continue. It does not mean the rise must resume, so treat it as a bias.

**How do you calculate the bull flag target?**
Measure the flagpole, from the start of the sharp rise to its top, and add that distance to the breakout point. This is a guide only, and many flags fall short.

**What is the difference between a flag and a pennant?**
A flag has two parallel lines and tilts slightly against the trend. A pennant has converging lines, like a small triangle. Both follow a sharp move and both are short-lived. See the comparison page.

**How long should a bull flag last?**
Edwards and Magee describe flags as forming quickly, usually within a few weeks on daily charts. On other timeframes, the flag should be short compared with the pole.

**Where does the stop usually go?**
Many traders place it below the lowest point of the flag, where the pause would clearly have failed. A tighter stop just below the lower line risks less but is hit more easily.
