Trend following is a method of trading in the direction of an established trend, and staying in it until it ends. The follower does not try to predict tops or bottoms. Losses are small and frequent in choppy markets, and the gains come from a few long moves.

## What is trend following?

A trend is a series of higher highs and higher lows (uptrend) or lower highs and lower lows (downtrend). Trend following accepts the trend as given and tries to join it. The idea is simple: what has been going up tends to keep going up for a while, until it does not.

No one person owns the approach. Richard Donchian is often named as an early figure, with his channel breakout rules from the mid-20th century. Michael Covel's book *Trend Following* gathers accounts of trend-following traders and funds. Treat the book as a description of the approach, not as proof it will work for you.

The cost of trend following is the many small losses taken when a trend does not appear, and the give-back at the end of each move. Winning trades are often a minority. The approach asks for patience and a stop that is never moved the wrong way.

## How it is identified

Trend followers need rules to say whether a trend exists. Common ones:

1. **Price versus a moving average.** Price above a long moving average, such as the 200-period, counts as an uptrend. See [exponential moving average](/exponential-moving-average/).
2. **Moving average crossover.** A fast average crossing above a slow one. See [moving average crossover](/moving-average-crossover/).
3. **Channel breakout.** Price makes a new 20-day or 55-day high, as in [Donchian channels](/donchian-channels/).
4. **Market structure.** A series of higher highs and higher lows. See [market structure](/market-structure/).
5. **Trend strength.** ADX above a threshold, often 25, to show the trend is strong enough.

**Worked example.** A trader buys when price closes above its 20-day high, and uses a trailing stop three times the ATR below the highest close. Price breaks out at 100. ATR is 2, so the first stop is at 100 - 6 = 94. Price climbs to 130. ATR is still 2, so the stop is at 130 - 6 = 124. Price then falls and hits 124. The trade makes 24 points on a risk of 6, a ratio of 4 to 1. If price had reversed at once, the loss would have been 6 points.

## How to read it

**Direction only.** The method answers "which side?", not "when is the top?". Do not use overbought readings as a reason to exit a strong trend.

**Entries.** Some followers enter on the breakout. Others wait for a [pullback](/pullback-trading/) in the trend for a better price and a closer stop.

**Exits.** A [trailing stop](/trailing-stop/) lets the trend run and exits when it fails. Fixed profit targets cap the winners, which hurts this style the most.

**Position size.** Risk a small, fixed part of the account per trade so a long losing streak is survivable.

**Whipsaws.** Rapid turns in a choppy market produce stop-outs. A trend filter such as ADX reduces them but does not remove them.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** Sustained directional moves, especially across many days or weeks.

**Ranges (weak).** Every signal is a false one. Losses mount until a real trend begins.

**Breakouts.** The start of a trend often looks like a breakout, so the two overlap. See [breakout trading](/breakout-trading/).

**Volatility.** Trends often come with rising volatility, so stops based on ATR are wider. Position size must be cut to match.

**Markets.** The approach has been applied across stocks, futures, forex and crypto. Results vary a great deal by market and period, and no general return figure can be quoted.

## Settings

| Style | Example rules | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Fast | 20-period breakout, 2 x ATR trailing stop | More trades, more whipsaws, shorter holds |
| Medium | 50-period average, 3 x ATR trailing stop | Balanced |
| Slow | 200-period average or 100-day breakout | Fewer trades, long holds, deep give-backs |

These are common examples, not rules. Test any set on the market and time frame you trade, on data you did not use to choose it.

## Common mistakes

- Taking profit early at a fixed target. This removes the rare big winners the method depends on.
- Trading it in a range and expecting the same results as in a trend.
- Using no stop, or moving the stop away when price turns.
- Changing the rules after a run of losses.
- Over-sizing. A long losing streak is normal and must be survivable.
- Quitting during a quiet period, then missing the next big trend.

## FAQ

**What is trend following?**
It is trading in the direction of an established trend and staying until the trend ends, usually with a trailing stop. The trader does not predict turns. Small losses are common in sideways markets, and the profit comes from a few long moves.

**Does trend following work?**
It has worked for some traders and funds in some periods, and failed in others. It tends to do well in long, strong trends and poorly in choppy markets. Test any rule set yourself, with realistic costs, before relying on it.

**What is the best indicator for trend following?**
There is no single best. Moving averages, Donchian channel breakouts and ADX are common. The choice matters less than having clear entry, exit and position size rules and sticking to them through losing streaks.

**Why is the win rate low in trend following?**
Because many signals come in markets that do not go on to trend, and the exits give back some profit. Followers accept many small losses in return for a few large gains. A low win rate with large winners can still be sound.

**What is the difference between trend following and mean reversion?**
Trend following expects moves to continue, so it buys strength. [Mean reversion](/mean-reversion/) expects moves to reverse toward an average, so it buys weakness. They suit opposite market states.
