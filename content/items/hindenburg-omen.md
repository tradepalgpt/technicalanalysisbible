The Hindenburg Omen is a market-breadth warning that combines several conditions: an uptrending index, a high number of both new 52-week highs and new 52-week lows, and weak breadth momentum. Its supporters read it as a sign of internal stress under a rising market. It is a rare, loosely defined signal, and versions differ.

## What is the Hindenburg Omen?

In a healthy rising market, most stocks make new highs and few make new lows. The omen looks for the opposite mix: an index still near its highs while a large number of stocks are making new 52-week highs and a large number are making new 52-week lows at the same time. That split suggests the market is divided, with some stocks surging and others collapsing.

The signal is widely credited to Jim Miekka and became well known in the late 2000s and early 2010s, when it was reported in the financial press. The name refers to the airship that burned in 1937, to suggest a hidden flaw under a market that looks sound.

There is no official definition. Published versions differ in thresholds, in the exchange used and in how long a signal stays active. Studies and commentary on it are mixed, and the signal has fired many times without a major fall following. Treat it as one cautionary input among many.

## How it is identified

One commonly described version asks for all of these on the same day, using NYSE data:

1. The index is in an uptrend, for example above its level 50 weeks earlier.
2. The number of new 52-week highs and the number of new 52-week lows are each above a set share of issues traded. A threshold of 2.2% is often quoted.
3. The McClellan Oscillator is negative.
4. New highs are not more than about twice the number of new lows.

**Worked example.** Suppose 3,000 NYSE issues trade. At 2.2%, the threshold is 3,000 x 0.022 = **66** issues. If 80 stocks make new highs and 75 make new lows, both clear the threshold. If the index is in an uptrend and the McClellan Oscillator reads -15, the conditions are met and the omen is said to be triggered.

Other sources use different thresholds, different markets or require a confirmation signal on later days. When you see a claim about the omen, check which version it used.

## How to read it

**A triggered omen.** It says breadth is split and rising momentum is fading. It is a prompt to check risk, not a statement that a fall is coming.

**Confirmation.** Some versions require a second trigger within a set time window. This reduces the number of signals.

**Context.** The signal means more after a long advance and less in the first leg of a rise.

**Absence of signal.** No omen tells you nothing. It rarely fires, and most market falls happen without one.

## Best market states

**Reversals (the intended use).** The omen is a caution flag after an extended advance.

**Strong trends (frequent false alarms).** In a strong uptrend, the conditions can appear and the advance can continue.

**Ranges and breakouts (weak).** The trend condition is often not met, or the signal appears while price is simply moving sideways.

**Volatility.** Market stress can raise both new highs and new lows, which can trigger the signal without a clear pattern.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Trend | Index in a clear uptrend |
| New highs and lows | Both above the threshold of the version you follow |
| Breadth momentum | McClellan Oscillator below zero |
| Version | Use one stated version and do not change it afterwards |
| Price | Use price signals such as a break of support for any action |

## Common mistakes

- Treating the omen as a precise sell signal. It is a loosely defined warning.
- Using different thresholds each time so that the signal always "worked".
- Ignoring that it can trigger and be followed by further rises.
- Relying on claims about its past success rate. Published figures differ, and they depend on the rules used.
- Acting on the omen without checking price, trend and risk limits.

## FAQ

**What is the Hindenburg Omen?**
It is a breadth-based warning signal. It triggers when an index is rising, a large number of stocks are making both new 52-week highs and new 52-week lows, and breadth momentum is negative. It suggests stress under the surface but is not a forecast.

**Does the Hindenburg Omen predict crashes?**
Not reliably. It has been linked with some market falls, but it has also appeared many times with no major decline after it. Because its rules vary by source, claims about its record are hard to compare.

**Who created the Hindenburg Omen?**
It is widely credited to Jim Miekka. There is no single official definition, and several versions with different thresholds are in circulation.

**What is the 2.2% threshold?**
In one common version, both the number of new highs and the number of new lows must each be above 2.2% of the issues traded on the NYSE. Other sources use different figures, so check the version before comparing signals.

**Can I use it to trade?**
Most traders use it as a warning that prompts a risk review rather than as an entry or exit trigger. Because it fires rarely and with mixed results, it works best next to price-based signals.
