An evening star is a three-candle pattern at the end of a rise. A long rising candle is followed by a small candle that shows hesitation, then a long falling candle that closes well into the first candle's body. It suggests buying has run out and sellers are taking over.

## What is an evening star?

The evening star is the mirror of the [Morning Star](/morning-star/). The name refers to the planet Venus appearing at dusk, just before the dark. In the same way, the pattern is read as a sign that a bright phase may be ending.

Nison describes it in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*. It tells a three-act story:

1. **Buyers dominate.** A long rising candle continues the uptrend.
2. **Hesitation.** The next candle has a small body, showing neither side is winning. It is often a spinning top or a [Doji](/doji/).
3. **Sellers take over.** A long falling candle closes well down into the first candle's body, undoing much of the earlier rise.

It needs three candles, so it is slower than a single-candle pattern, but it often carries more weight.

## How it is identified

An evening star has:

1. **A prior advance.**
2. **Candle one:** a long rising body, in line with the trend.
3. **Candle two:** a small body (either colour) that sits above candle one's body. Nison says the star's body should gap above the first body. In markets that rarely gap, many traders accept a small body that stays high.
4. **Candle three:** a long falling body that closes beyond the midpoint of candle one's body.

**Worked example.** After a rise:
- Candle one opens at 50.0 and closes at 56.0 (rising, body of 6.0).
- Candle two opens at 56.3 and closes at 56.1 (tiny body, high of 57.0).
- Candle three opens at 55.9 and closes at 51.5 (falling, body of 4.4).

The midpoint of candle one's body is 53.0. Candle three closes at 51.5, below it, so this is an evening star.

## How to read it

**Candle two shows the pause.** The small body means the advance has stalled. A long upper wick on the star adds to the message, since buyers pushed up and were rejected. If the star is a doji, the indecision is stronger. That version is often treated as a separate pattern, the evening doji star.

**Candle three shows the shift.** A long falling body that undoes much of the first candle's rise shows sellers have taken control. The deeper it closes into candle one, the stronger the reading.

**Location.** At a known resistance level, after a clear rise, the pattern means more. Three candles that happen to fit the shape midway through a rally are weaker.

**Stop reference.** Some traders place a protective stop just above the star's high, since a move over it means the turn failed. That is a common method, not advice.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** After a sustained advance, it is a well-known sign that buyers are tiring.

**Ranges (weak).** At the top of a range it can help. Elsewhere in a range, the shape appears by chance and is mostly noise.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong uptrend, an evening star can be a brief pause before the rise continues.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A clear advance into the pattern |
| Candle one | Long rising body |
| Candle two | Small body, ideally above the first body |
| Candle three | Long falling body closing beyond the midpoint of candle one |
| Level | Resistance, a prior high or a trendline nearby |
| Volume | Higher volume on candle three adds weight |

## Common mistakes

- Accepting a first or third candle that is not clearly long.
- Calling it an evening star in a sideways market with no advance to reverse.
- Insisting on a gap in markets that do not gap, and so missing real examples.
- Acting on the first two candles in anticipation. The pattern is not complete until candle three closes.
- Ignoring support and resistance. An evening star just above strong support may go nowhere.
- Confusing it with a bearish engulfing pattern, which uses only two candles.

## Pairs well with

- **[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/)**: resistance above the star gives the turn a reason to hold.
- **[RSI](/rsi/)**: an overbought reading or bearish divergence adds weight.
- **[Volume](/volume/)**: heavy volume on the third candle shows broad participation in the move down.
- **[Trendline](/trendline/)**: a break of a rising trendline alongside the pattern strengthens the case.

## FAQ

**Is an evening star bearish?**
Yes. It is a bearish reversal pattern that forms after a rise. It shows a long buying candle, a pause and then a strong selling candle. It is a clue, not a guarantee, so most traders look for resistance or other evidence too.

**Does the middle candle need to gap?**
Nison describes the star's body as gapping above the first body. In markets that trade nearly continuously, such as forex, gaps are rare, so many traders accept a small body that stays high without a true gap.

**Does the colour of the star matter?**
Not much. Nison notes that a falling star is slightly more bearish than a rising one, but the small body and the strong third candle matter far more.

**What is the difference between an evening star and a bearish engulfing pattern?**
A bearish engulfing uses two candles, with the second body covering the first. An evening star uses three, with a pause followed by a strong fall. See [Bearish Engulfing](/bearish-engulfing/).

**What if candle three does not reach the midpoint of candle one?**
Then the pattern is weaker, and many traders would not count it. A third candle that closes only a little way into the first body shows sellers have not clearly taken over.
