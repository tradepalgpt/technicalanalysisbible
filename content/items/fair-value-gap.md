A fair value gap (FVG), also called an imbalance, is a three-candle pattern where a strong middle candle leaves a gap between the first candle's wick and the third candle's wick. Traders who use the idea expect price to return and partly fill that gap. It is a trader-education concept, not a proven law.

## What is a fair value gap?

When price moves very fast, it can pass through a price range without much trading there. On a candlestick chart this shows as a stretch of price covered by only one candle's body. The idea is that this range was traded in one direction only, so the market is "imbalanced". Some traders expect price to come back to trade it more evenly.

The term comes from ICT-style teaching. "Fair value" here does not mean an economic valuation. It is just a label for the gap. There is no published research showing that these gaps fill more often than chance, so treat the concept as a way to organise a chart, not a forecast.

An FVG is different from a regular [price gap](/price-gaps/), which is a visible space between one candle's close and the next candle's open, usually between sessions. An FVG is found inside a continuous market, including 24-hour markets such as forex and crypto. See the [comparison](/compare/fair-value-gap-vs-price-gap/).

## How it is identified

Take three consecutive candles.

**Bullish FVG.** The high of candle 1 is lower than the low of candle 3. The gap is the space between those two prices. Candle 2 is usually a large up candle.

**Bearish FVG.** The low of candle 1 is higher than the high of candle 3. The gap is the space between them. Candle 2 is usually a large down candle.

**Worked example (bullish).** Candle 1 has a high of 102. Candle 2 rallies from 102 to 108. Candle 3 has a low of 105. The wicks do not overlap: candle 1 tops out at 102, and candle 3 starts at 105. The gap runs from 102 to 105, three points wide.

A trader using the idea might watch for price to pull back into the 102 to 105 area. Some look for a reaction at the midpoint (103.5), and treat a close below 102 as proof the idea has failed.

## How to read it

**Size of the gap.** Larger gaps relative to recent candle sizes are taken more seriously. Tiny gaps are common and often ignored.

**Fill level.** Traders differ on what counts as filled. Some say any touch, some say the midpoint, some say a full close through the gap. Pick one rule and keep to it.

**Direction of the trend.** A bullish gap in an uptrend is treated as a possible support area. A bullish gap in a downtrend is usually seen as weaker.

**Inversion.** If price closes fully through a gap, some traders flip it: a broken bullish gap becomes a possible resistance zone. This is a further layer of the same story and is even less tested.

**As a target.** Because the theory says price is drawn to gaps, some traders use an unfilled gap above or below as a profit target.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** After a strong move that breaks structure, pullbacks into the gap give a defined area to watch. See [break of structure](/break-of-structure/).

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways markets print small gaps all the time, and most mean nothing.

**Reversals.** A gap left by a sharp turn can mark the start of the new move, but many traders prefer to wait for confirmation first.

**Volatility.** News spikes create very large gaps that price may never revisit. Gaps made by thin liquidity are less reliable than ones inside normal trading.

**Honest limits.** Many gaps fill, but price also leaves many unfilled for a long time. Counting only the ones that filled gives a false picture.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Three-candle shape | Wicks of candle 1 and 3 clearly do not overlap |
| Candle size | The middle candle is large compared with recent candles |
| Structure | The move broke a swing high or low |
| Trend | The gap is in the direction of the higher time frame trend |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or small structure shift when price re-enters the gap |
| Failure | A full close through the far edge of the gap |

## Common mistakes

- Marking every tiny gap. Filter by size or the chart becomes unreadable.
- Assuming every gap must fill. Many do not, and the ones that fill are the ones remembered.
- Mixing up FVGs with session [price gaps](/price-gaps/).
- Trading a gap against the higher time frame trend.
- Entering at the first touch with no confirmation and no stop beyond the gap.
- Describing "fair value" as if it were a real measured value. It is only a label.

## FAQ

**What is a fair value gap?**
It is a three-candle pattern in which the wicks of the first and third candles do not overlap, leaving a gap across the middle candle. Traders expect price may return to fill it. The term comes from ICT teaching and is not proven research.

**How do you find a fair value gap?**
Look at three candles in a row. If the first candle's high is below the third candle's low, there is a bullish FVG. If the first candle's low is above the third candle's high, it is bearish. The gap is the space between those prices.

**Is a fair value gap the same as a regular price gap?**
No. A regular gap is a visible space between sessions, often caused by news overnight. An FVG is found within continuous trading and is made from the wicks of three candles. The two ideas look alike but are defined differently.

**Do fair value gaps always get filled?**
No. Many are filled, many are not, and some take a long time. There is no reliable published evidence that they fill more often than chance. Use them as a reference area, with a stop in case they fail.

**What time frame is best for fair value gaps?**
Gaps on higher time frames such as four-hour or daily are generally treated as more significant. Lower time frames give more gaps but also more noise. Choose one based on how long you plan to hold, and test it.
