Speed resistance lines are two sloping lines drawn from the start of a major move through the one-third and two-thirds points of that move. They give possible support in an uptrend (or resistance in a downtrend) when price pulls back. The tool is credited to the analyst Edson Gould and is a traditional charting convention rather than a tested method.

## What are speed resistance lines?

Speed resistance lines (SRL) divide a price move into thirds and project two trendlines from the start of that move. The idea is that the angle, or "speed", of a pullback shows whether the trend is holding. A pullback that stops at the upper line is shallow and fast. One that falls to the lower line is deeper and slower.

The tool is credited to Edson Gould, a market analyst. The decade is given as a range here because the exact year is not firmly established in the sources this guide relies on. The method is a charting tradition and does not have an academic evidence base. It is a near relative of [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/), but it works with sloping lines rather than horizontal levels, and it uses thirds instead of Fibonacci ratios.

## How it is drawn

1. Find a major swing low and the swing high that follows it (for an uptrend). For a downtrend, use a high and the low that follows.
2. Measure the vertical distance between the two extremes.
3. At the bar of the second extreme, mark the points one-third and two-thirds of the way up from the start (for an uptrend).
4. Draw a line from the start extreme through the one-third point, and another from the start extreme through the two-thirds point. Extend both forward.

**Worked example.** An uptrend starts at a low of 100 and peaks at 130. The move is 30 points.

- One-third of the move: 100 + 10 = 110.
- Two-thirds of the move: 100 + 20 = 120.
- Line A runs from 100 (the low) through 120 at the bar of the high. This is the upper, steeper line.
- Line B runs from 100 through 110 at the bar of the high. This is the lower, flatter line.

As time passes, both lines rise. If price pulls back, the first support to watch is line A, then line B.

## How to read it

The traditional reading for an uptrend is as follows.

- **Pullback holds at the upper (2/3) line.** The trend is strong and the pullback was shallow.
- **Price breaks the upper line.** Expect a test of the lower (1/3) line.
- **Price breaks the lower line.** Many traders read this as a sign that the whole move may be retraced, with the start of the move as the next area of interest.
- **A broken line often becomes resistance.** Once price falls through a line and rallies back, that line can act as resistance, the same idea as role reversal.

In a downtrend the lines work the same way upside down, with resistance in place of support.

These readings come from teaching tradition. No one should expect each line to hold, and this guide does not give figures for how often it does.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The tool needs a clear, large move to measure. It then gives a map for pullbacks.

**Ranges (weak).** Without a clear move, the start and end points are arbitrary and the lines have no meaning.

**Reversals.** A decisive break through both lines is a warning sign, but price action should confirm.

**Volatility.** The lines are drawn from two extreme points, so a single spike can change them completely.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Move | A clear, major swing, not a minor wiggle |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or a hold at a line |
| Close | A close through a line, not just a wick, before calling it broken |
| Other tools | The line agrees with a horizontal level or a retracement area |
| Stop | Beyond the line, or beyond the swing that invalidates the idea |

## Common mistakes

- Picking the start and end points after the fact so the lines fit.
- Using a small swing. The lines only mean something on a large, obvious move.
- Treating the lines as certain support.
- Ignoring that lines slope, so their price changes each bar, and a level read off an old chart is wrong.
- Forgetting that a new extreme means the lines must be redrawn.

## FAQ

**What are speed resistance lines?**
They are two trendlines drawn from the start of a major move through its one-third and two-thirds points. In an uptrend they mark possible support levels during a pullback. In a downtrend they mark possible resistance.

**Who created speed resistance lines?**
The tool is credited to the analyst Edson Gould. The decade is given as a range, 1950s to 1960s, because the exact year is not firmly documented here.

**How are speed resistance lines different from Fibonacci retracements?**
Fibonacci retracements use horizontal levels at set ratios such as 38.2 or 61.8 percent. Speed resistance lines slope with time and use thirds. Both are charting conventions without an academic evidence base, and some traders show both on one chart.

**Which line is the most important?**
The traditional teaching treats the two-thirds line as the first one to watch in a strong trend. If it fails, the one-third line is next. This is a convention and not a measured rule.

**Do speed resistance lines work in a downtrend?**
Yes, the construction is mirrored. Measure from the high to the following low, and the lines act as possible resistance during a rally. The same limits apply, including that a clear major move is needed.
