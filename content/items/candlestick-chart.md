A candlestick chart draws one candle for each time period, showing the open, high, low and close in a single shape. The thick body spans the open to the close, and thin lines called wicks reach the high and low. The colour tells you at a glance whether price closed up or down for the period.

## What are candlestick charts?

A candlestick chart is a way of drawing price. Each candle covers one period, such as a day or an hour, and holds four prices: the **open** (first trade), the **high**, the **low** and the **close** (last trade).

The same data can be drawn as an OHLC bar chart. Candles make the relationship between the open and the close much easier to see, because that gap becomes a filled or hollow box.

The method is usually traced to Japanese rice trading in earlier centuries. Western traders mostly learned it from Steve Nison's *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*, published in 1991. Since then it has become the default chart style on most platforms.

A candle is not a signal by itself. It is a compact record of what happened in one period. Candlestick patterns, such as the [Doji](/doji/) or the [Bullish Engulfing](/bullish-engulfing/), are named shapes made from one or more candles.

## How it is identified

Every candle has the same parts:

1. **The body.** The box between the open and the close.
2. **The upper wick** (also called a shadow or tail). A thin line from the top of the body to the period's high.
3. **The lower wick.** A thin line from the bottom of the body to the period's low.
4. **The colour.** Most charts show a rising candle (close above open) as green or hollow, and a falling candle (close below open) as red or filled. Colours are a setting, so check your own platform.

**Worked example.** A day opens at 100, rises to 106, falls to 98 and closes at 104.
- Close is above open, so it is a rising candle.
- Body runs from 100 to 104 (size 4).
- Upper wick runs from 104 to 106 (size 2).
- Lower wick runs from 100 to 98 (size 2).
- Full range, high to low, is 8.

The next day opens at 104 and closes at 101 with a high of 105 and a low of 100. That is a falling candle with a body of 3.

## How to read it

**Body size.** A long body means one side dominated the period. A short body means the open and close were close together, so neither side won clearly.

**Wick length.** A long wick shows price travelled far and was pushed back. A long upper wick means buyers pushed up and were rejected. A long lower wick means sellers pushed down and were rejected.

**Closing position.** A close near the high shows buyers finished in control. A close near the low shows sellers did.

**Sequence.** One candle says little. Reading several together shows momentum building or fading. A run of long rising candles shows steady buying; a run of shrinking bodies suggests the move is slowing.

**Location.** The same candle means different things at different places. A long lower wick at a known support level is more interesting than one in the middle of nowhere.

## Best market states

**Trends.** Candles show whether pullbacks are shallow or deep, and whether each push is getting stronger or weaker.

**Ranges.** Wicks that poke above or below a range and close back inside show the edges holding.

**Time frames.** Candles work on any time frame. Higher time frames (daily, weekly) carry more weight than very short ones, where small candles appear constantly and mean less.

| Time frame | Prefer candles from higher time frames for important decisions |

## Common mistakes

- Judging a candle before its period has closed. A candle that looks like a hammer at mid-session can end as something different.
- Treating a named pattern as a prediction instead of a clue.
- Ignoring context, such as the trend or nearby support and resistance.
- Memorising dozens of pattern names instead of understanding body, wick and close.
- Assuming the open is the same everywhere. In 24-hour markets, the "open" depends on the chart's session cut-off.
- Mixing up colour conventions between platforms, especially for hollow candles.

## FAQ

**What does a green or red candle mean?**
Usually green (or hollow) means the close was above the open, and red (or filled) means the close was below the open. This is only a default. Platforms let you change the colours, so check your own settings before reading a chart.

**What is the difference between a candlestick chart and a bar chart?**
Both show open, high, low and close. A bar chart uses a vertical line with small ticks for the open and close. A candlestick chart draws the open-to-close range as a box, which many people find quicker to read.

**What are wicks on a candle?**
Wicks, also called shadows or tails, are the thin lines above and below the body. They reach the period's highest and lowest prices. Long wicks show price travelled a long way and was pushed back.

**Do candlesticks predict the market?**
No. A candle records what happened in a period. Patterns made from candles can hint at shifts in buying and selling pressure, but they work best as clues alongside trend, levels and other evidence, not as forecasts.

**What time frame should I use for candles?**
Any time frame works, since the shapes mean the same thing. Candles on higher time frames such as daily or weekly usually carry more weight because each one summarises more trading. Very short time frames produce many small, noisy candles.
