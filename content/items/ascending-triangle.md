An ascending triangle is a chart pattern with a flat upper line and a rising lower line. Price keeps hitting the same resistance while making higher lows. It is usually read as a bullish continuation pattern, and a close above the flat line is the usual signal.

## What is an ascending triangle pattern?

An ascending triangle shows buyers getting more aggressive while sellers hold one price. Price rises to a resistance level and is turned back. Buyers then step in at a higher low than before. Price rises to the same resistance again and falls back to another, higher low. The lows climb while the highs stay level, so the range narrows into a triangle with a flat top and a rising base.

The reading is that sellers are defending a fixed price, but buyers are willing to pay more each time. Eventually the sellers at that level are used up, or the buyers push through.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, describe the ascending triangle as a bullish formation, with the flat top acting as a line of supply that is eventually absorbed. It most often forms as a pause inside an uptrend, though it can appear at the end of a decline.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts:

1. **A flat upper line** touching at least two highs at about the same price.
2. **A rising lower line** touching at least two higher lows.
3. **Converging lines.** The range narrows as the lines approach each other. Edwards and Magee describe a minimum of four reversal points, two on each line.
4. **A breakout:** a close above the flat upper line.

Volume tends to fall as the triangle forms, because the market is waiting for a decision.

**Measured target.** Measure the height of the triangle at its widest point, which is at the start, between the first high and the first low. Add that height to the breakout level.

**Worked example.** The flat top is at 50 and the first low is at 40: a height of 10. If price closes above 50 at 51, the measured target is 51 + 10 = **61**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Breakout.** A close above the flat line completes the pattern. Because the upper line is horizontal, it is a clear and easy level to watch. Many traders wait for a close rather than a brief wick above it.

**Volume.** Volume usually dries up inside the triangle and should rise on a genuine breakout. A break on weak volume deserves caution.

**Timing.** Murphy notes that breakouts from triangles usually occur before price runs all the way to the apex, where the lines meet. A breakout very close to the apex tends to carry less force.

**Retest.** Price sometimes falls back to the broken line, which may now act as support (see [Breakout Retest](/breakout-retest/)). Some traders enter on that retest.

**Failed breakout and downside breaks.** The pattern is only a bias. Price can break down through the rising line instead, and that is a signal in its own right. A breakout that falls back inside the triangle is a failed breakout.

## Best market states

**Uptrends (best).** The pattern is most often treated as a pause in an existing advance, with the prior trend resuming on the break.

**Breakouts from consolidation.** The flat top gives a clear trigger level, which suits breakout traders.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, a flat top with slightly higher lows is easy to see by accident. Without a clear prior trend and some narrowing, it is weak evidence.

**After long advances.** If price has already run a long way, a triangle near the top can turn out to be a distribution zone. Context matters.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Touches | At least two on the flat top and two on the rising line |
| Flat top | Highs at about the same price |
| Higher lows | Each low clearly above the one before |
| Volume | Fading inside the triangle, rising on the breakout |
| Break | Close above the flat line, not just a wick |
| Stop | Below the last higher low (conservative: below the triangle's base) |

## Common mistakes

- Buying inside the triangle on the assumption that it must break up. It can break either way.
- Drawing a "flat" top through highs that are in fact spread over a wide price range.
- Trading a breakout that happens very near the apex, where momentum is often weaker.
- Ignoring volume.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Having no exit plan if price falls back inside the triangle.

## FAQ

**Is an ascending triangle bullish or bearish?**
It is usually read as bullish, because buyers push the lows higher while sellers hold a flat ceiling. But it is not certain. Price can break down through the rising line, so wait for the actual break.

**How do you calculate the ascending triangle target?**
Measure the triangle's height at its widest point, then add that to the breakout price. Treat the result as a guide only. Many patterns fall short of the target.

**Where does the stop go on an ascending triangle?**
Many traders place it below the most recent higher low. A more conservative stop goes below the base of the triangle. The tighter the stop, the more likely it is hit by noise.

**How many touches are needed?**
Two on each line is the minimum, giving four reversal points. More touches make the lines more reliable, but a very long triangle may lose its meaning, as the pattern can fade away.

**What is the difference between an ascending triangle and a rising wedge?**
An ascending triangle has a flat top and a rising base. A rising wedge slopes upward on both lines, which narrows the range. The wedge is generally read as bearish, so the two are not interchangeable.
