A symmetrical triangle is a chart pattern in which price swings get smaller, with lower highs and higher lows squeezing into a point. It shows a pause and a build-up of pressure. It has no built-in direction. The break of either line is the signal, and the move often follows the trend that came before.

## What is a symmetrical triangle pattern?

A symmetrical triangle shows a market in balance. Each rally peaks lower than the last, and each dip bottoms higher than the last. Buyers and sellers are both holding back, and the range keeps narrowing. Two trendlines, one falling through the highs and one rising through the lows, meet at a point called the apex.

Unlike the ascending and descending triangles, neither side has a flat line, so there is no built-in lean. Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, treat it as a pattern of uncertainty that most often resolves in the direction of the trend before it, though not always. Volatility falls during the squeeze, and usually rises again when price breaks out.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts:

1. **A prior move.** The triangle is usually a pause after a trend, which gives the squeeze a direction to resolve towards.
2. **A falling upper line** through at least two lower highs.
3. **A rising lower line** through at least two higher lows.
4. **Converging lines.** Edwards and Magee describe a minimum of four reversal points, two on each line.
5. **A breakout:** a close beyond either line.

Volume tends to shrink as the triangle forms.

**Measured target.** Measure the height of the triangle at its widest point, at the start, between the first high and first low. Add it to the breakout point if price breaks up, or subtract it if price breaks down.

**Worked example.** The first high is at 60 and the first low is at 50: a height of 10. If price closes above the upper line at 55, the measured target is 55 + 10 = **65**. If it had closed below the lower line at 52, the target would be 52 - 10 = **42**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Breakout direction.** Price can break either way. Many traders favour a break in the direction of the earlier trend, and are more cautious about a break against it.

**Timing.** Edwards and Magee and Murphy both note that breakouts typically occur well before price reaches the apex, often most of the way along the triangle. A break very close to the apex tends to lack force, and price may simply drift out of the triangle.

**Volume.** Volume usually dries up inside the triangle. A rise on the breakout is taken as confirmation. A break on weak volume is more likely to fail.

**Retest.** Price sometimes returns to the broken trendline (see [Breakout Retest](/breakout-retest/)). Some traders enter on that retest.

**False breaks.** Because the lines are close together near the apex, small moves can cross them. A close, rather than a wick, helps filter out some of the noise.

## Best market states

**After a squeeze in volatility (best).** The triangle is a picture of falling volatility. When it ends, a larger move often follows, which is why the pattern appears in breakout and volatility work.

**Trends.** Triangles inside trends are usually read as continuation patterns.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, price can form a triangle with no prior move. Then there is no trend to resume, and the pattern is little more than a quiet stretch.

**Near the apex.** The closer price gets to the apex without breaking, the weaker the signal.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Touches | At least two on each line |
| Convergence | Lower highs and higher lows, narrowing steadily |
| Prior move | A clear trend before the pattern |
| Volume | Fading inside the triangle, rising on the break |
| Break | Close beyond a line, not just a wick |
| Stop | Back inside the triangle (conservative: beyond the opposite line) |

## Common mistakes

- Guessing the direction in advance. The pattern has no fixed bias.
- Counting a triangle with only three touches or with lines that do not converge.
- Trading a break that occurs almost at the apex.
- Ignoring volume.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Using a stop that sits inside the noise at the narrow end of the triangle.

## FAQ

**Is a symmetrical triangle bullish or bearish?**
Neither by itself. It is a pause. Many traders expect a break in the direction of the earlier trend, but price can go either way. The signal comes only when price closes beyond one of the lines.

**How do you calculate the symmetrical triangle target?**
Measure the height at the widest part, then add it to the breakout price for an upward break or subtract it for a downward one. It is a guide only, and many patterns fall short.

**What is the difference between a symmetrical and an ascending triangle?**
An ascending triangle has a flat top and rising lows, which leans bullish. A symmetrical triangle has both lines sloping towards each other, so it has no built-in lean.

**How many touches does a symmetrical triangle need?**
Two on each line, giving four reversal points, is the usual minimum. More touches make the lines more reliable, though very long triangles may lose their meaning.

**Where does the stop go?**
Many traders put it just inside the triangle on the other side of the broken line. A more conservative stop goes beyond the opposite line, which costs more per trade but gives price more room.
