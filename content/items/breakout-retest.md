A breakout and retest is a two-step setup. Price first closes beyond a support or resistance level, then comes back to test that level from the other side. If the old level now holds as the opposite (old resistance becomes support), traders enter with a stop just beyond it. It trades a slower entry for a clearer stop.

## What is a breakout and retest?

A breakout on its own is hard to enter well. If you buy as price breaks, the stop is far away, and many breaks fail. A retest offers a second chance. After the break, price often pulls back to the level it just crossed. If buyers defend that level, the break is more likely to be real.

Edwards and Magee (1948) describe this behaviour as a "throwback" after an upward breakout and a "pullback" after a downward one. Murphy (1999) covers the same idea in his discussion of the principle that broken resistance becomes support. See [Role reversal](/role-reversal/).

"Break and retest" as a named setup is common trader language. It has no single originator, and it does not guarantee a retest will occur or hold.

## How it is identified

1. **Mark the level.** A clear and tested support, resistance or pattern edge.
2. **Wait for a close beyond it.** A wick alone is not enough for most traders.
3. **Wait for price to return.** It comes back and touches or crosses the level, often within a few bars.
4. **Look for a hold.** A rejection bar, such as a [pin bar](/pin-bar/), or a close back on the breakout side.
5. **Enter** on the hold or on a break of the rejection bar, with the stop beyond the level.

**Worked example.** Resistance sits at 100. Price closes at 103. It pulls back to 100.4 and prints a bar with a low of 100.1 that closes at 101.5. A trader buys at 101.6 with a stop at 99.5 (beyond the level and a little for noise). Risk is 2.1 points. The measured move from the range, say 8 points high, gives a target near 108, so the reward is about 6.4 points for 2.1 of risk. These numbers are only for illustration.

The retest can also be shallow and stop short of the level, or deep and cross it before holding. Many traders treat a zone around the level, not an exact line.

## How to read it

**Role reversal.** The broken level now has the opposite role. Resistance becomes support. See [Role reversal](/role-reversal/).

**Depth.** A retest that holds near the level is stronger than one that closes back deep inside the old range.

**Volume.** Rising volume on the breakout and lighter volume on the retest are often read as healthy. This is a guide, not a rule. See [Volume](/volume/).

**Failure.** A close back inside the old range through the level means the break failed. This is a [false breakout](/false-breakout/) and the stop is hit.

**No retest.** Strong breakouts often run without returning. A trader who waits for the retest misses these. That is the price of the cleaner entry.

## Best market states

**Breakouts from ranges and patterns (best).** The level is clear, so the retest has a defined meaning.

**Trends (good).** After a break of structure, price often retests the broken swing. See [Break of structure](/break-of-structure/).

**Ranges (weak).** Without a clear level the setup does not apply, and breaks that fail are common.

**Fast markets.** News-driven moves often leave no chance to retest, or whip through the level in both directions.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Level | Tested at least twice before the break |
| Break | A close beyond the level, with a strong bar |
| Retest | Price returns to the level within a reasonable number of bars |
| Hold | A rejection bar or a close back on the breakout side |
| Stop | Beyond the level, with room for noise and spread |
| Target | The next level or a measured move, with reward larger than risk |

## Common mistakes

- Entering at the first touch with no sign it holds. Wait for a bar that shows the level working.
- Placing the stop exactly on the level where many other stops sit.
- Waiting forever. If there is no retest within a few bars, the setup has changed.
- Confusing a deep pullback into the range with a retest.
- Taking retests of levels that were never meaningful.
- Ignoring the trend. A retest against the higher time frame trend is a lower-odds trade.

## FAQ

**What is a breakout and retest?**
It is a setup where price breaks a key level and then returns to test it from the other side. If the level holds as new support or resistance, traders enter with a stop just beyond it.

**Does price always retest after a breakout?**
No. Strong breakouts can run without looking back. Studies of chart patterns such as those in Edwards and Magee describe throwbacks as common but not constant. Traders who need a retest will miss the fastest moves.

**Where do you put the stop on a retest trade?**
Usually just beyond the broken level, on the far side from the breakout. Allow a margin for noise and spread, since many stops cluster at obvious levels. A close back through the level means the idea has failed.

**How long can a retest take?**
It varies. Some happen within a few bars and others take many. A common rule is to drop the setup if no retest occurs within a set number of bars, chosen and tested in advance.

**Is a retest better than entering on the breakout?**
It trades certainty for entries. A retest gives a tighter stop and a check that the level holds, but you miss trades that never retest. A breakout entry catches more moves but has a wider stop and more failures.
