Murrey Math Lines are a set of horizontal levels that split a price range into eight equal steps, labelled 0/8 to 8/8, with extra lines above and below. Traders treat the lines as possible support and resistance, with the 0/8, 4/8 and 8/8 lines given the most weight. The system is a trader-education method with no academic evidence base.

## What are Murrey Math Lines?

Murrey Math Lines divide a price frame into eighths. The frame is chosen from the recent high and low, and the lines are then drawn at each eighth. Because the lines are placed by a formula rather than by touches on the chart, they appear before price reaches them.

The method is credited to T. H. Murrey and is usually described as being built on the ideas of W. D. Gann, especially his octaves and the [Gann Square of Nine](/gann-square-of-nine/). Gann's own work is hard to read and open to interpretation, so the link is a matter of teaching tradition rather than something that can be tested step by step. Like the rest of [Gann theory](/gann-theory/), the method has no academic evidence base. Treat it as a way of putting grid-like levels on a chart, not a proven forecaster.

## How it is drawn

Most charting packages offer Murrey Math as a ready-made indicator. The exact steps differ between packages, but the idea is the same:

1. Take the highest high and the lowest low over a lookback period (64 bars is a common default).
2. Choose a frame size, often a round number scale, that contains that range.
3. Divide the frame by eight. Each step is one eighth.
4. Draw lines at 0/8, 1/8, 2/8 and so on up to 8/8. Many versions add lines at +1/8, +2/8, -1/8 and -2/8 beyond the frame.

**Worked example.** Suppose the frame runs from 0 to 80. One eighth is 80 / 8 = 10. The lines fall at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80. The 4/8 line is at 40, the middle of the frame.

Because each platform picks the frame in its own way, two indicators with the same name can draw lines at slightly different prices. Check the frame logic of the tool you use.

## How to read it

Murrey's usual descriptions give each line a role. Taught labels vary a little, but the common version is:

- **0/8 and 8/8:** the bottom and top of the frame. Often called the hardest support and resistance, where price is expected to struggle to continue.
- **4/8:** the middle line, treated as the strongest level inside the frame.
- **3/8 and 5/8:** the lower and upper edges of the central trading range. A market stuck between them is seen as balanced.
- **2/8 and 6/8:** pivot lines where price is often said to reverse sharply.
- **1/8 and 7/8:** weak lines that price tends to pass through, or stall near without a clear reversal.
- **Extension lines (+1/8, +2/8, -1/8, -2/8):** mark possible overshoots beyond the frame. A move beyond +2/8 or -2/8 is treated as a stretched market.

Traders look for price to react at these lines, for example a rejection candle at 2/8 in a range. No line is a certain turning point, and the importance given to each is a convention rather than a measured result.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** In a sideways market, price spends time between fixed levels, so a grid of horizontal lines can line up with the edges and middle of the range.

**Strong trends (weak).** Price can run through several lines with only a pause at each. A trend also changes the frame, so lines move when the lookback high or low changes.

**Breakouts.** A close beyond 8/8 or the +1/8 line may show that the range is ending, but the method does not give a clear rule for it.

**Volatility.** In fast markets price can skip between lines without a useful reaction. Lines drawn from a very wide frame are far apart.

## Settings

| Setting | Typical value | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Lookback period | 64 bars is a common default | Longer lookbacks give a stable frame and fewer changes; shorter ones redraw the grid more often |
| Step back or shift | Platform specific | Some versions use the frame from earlier bars to keep lines from moving |
| Extension lines | Off or on | Adds the +1/8, +2/8, -1/8 and -2/8 lines |

There is no setting with proven value. Whatever you pick, test it on the market and time frame you trade, and note that the lines shift whenever the lookback high or low changes.

## Common mistakes

- Trading every touch of a line. Not every line has a reaction, and the importance of each line is a convention.
- Forgetting that the grid can move when a new high or low enters the lookback.
- Mixing up indicators from different platforms, which can place the lines at different prices.
- Using the lines alone without a price signal such as a rejection candle.
- Applying them in a strong trend and fading each line.

## FAQ

**What are Murrey Math Lines?**
They are horizontal lines that split a recent price range into eight equal parts, with extra lines beyond. Traders use them as possible support and resistance. The 0/8, 4/8 and 8/8 lines are usually given the most weight.

**Who created Murrey Math Lines?**
The method is credited to T. H. Murrey and is usually said to build on W. D. Gann's ideas. The exact year is not firmly documented, and the 1990s is an approximate decade.

**What is the 4/8 line?**
It is the middle line of the frame and is treated as the strongest level inside it. A market trading between the 3/8 and 5/8 lines is seen as balanced. The idea is a teaching convention, not a tested result.

**Do Murrey Math Lines repaint?**
The lines can move when the lookback window picks up a new high or low, because the frame changes. Some versions shift or step back to reduce this. Check how your platform handles it before relying on the lines.

**Are Murrey Math Lines reliable?**
There is no academic evidence base for them. They give you a grid to plan against, and any reaction at a line should be confirmed by price action. Test any setting on your own market before using it with real money.
