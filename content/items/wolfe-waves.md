## What are Wolfe Waves?

A Wolfe Wave is a five-wave structure that fits inside two converging trendlines, like a wedge. The idea is that the market overshoots its upper or lower line on the fifth wave. That overshoot is where the pattern expects a turn. The pattern is traditionally credited to Bill Wolfe, but there is no standard textbook definition. Different sources set different rules, so descriptions of the pattern vary.

There are two forms. A bullish Wolfe Wave points to a rise and a bearish Wolfe Wave points to a fall. The shape is related to the [rising wedge](/rising-wedge/) and [falling wedge](/falling-wedge/), but it adds numbered points and a target line.

The pattern has no academic evidence base. Because the five points can be picked after the event, it looks neat on past charts.

## How it is drawn

A bullish Wolfe Wave uses five points:

1. **Point 1:** a swing low.
2. **Point 2:** a swing high after point 1.
3. **Point 3:** a swing low that is lower than point 1.
4. **Point 4:** a lower high than point 2, but above point 3.
5. **Point 5:** a new low, below the line joining points 1 and 3, which overshoots the lower trendline.

Lines used:

- **1-3 line:** joins points 1 and 3, and is extended past point 5.
- **2-4 line:** joins points 2 and 4. The two lines converge, forming the wedge.
- **1-4 line:** the target line, drawn from point 1 through point 4 and projected forward.

A bearish version is the mirror image.

**Worked example (bullish).** Point 1 is at 100, point 2 at 110, point 3 at 98, point 4 at 106 and point 5 at 94. Counting each point as one step, the 1-3 line falls 2 points over two steps, so it sits at 96 by point 5. Point 5 at 94 is below it, which is the overshoot. The 1-4 line rises from 100 to 106 over three steps, 2 points per step. Extended two steps past point 4 it reaches 110, and extended three steps it reaches 112. That is the target area. The numbers only show the geometry.

## How to read it

**Entry.** Traders enter after point 5, either as price turns or when it closes back above the 1-3 line. Some enter at the point 5 turn, others wait for a break.

**Target.** The 1-4 line is the usual target. The expected arrival time is often estimated by how far the line is projected.

**Stop.** A stop is placed just beyond point 5, as a move past it breaks the shape.

**Overshoot.** Point 5 breaking the 1-3 line is a feature, not a flaw.

**Variation.** Sources differ on how symmetrical the shape must be and how the target is timed.

## Best market states

**Reversal after an extended move (best).** The pattern is a late-stage structure.

**Trends (caution).** In a strong trend, price can keep going through point 5.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways action produces many five-swing sequences that can be forced to fit.

**Breakouts (weak).** A genuine breakout looks like a point 5 overshoot but does not reverse.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Points | Five clear swings, in order |

| Overshoot | Point 5 beyond the 1-3 line |
| Turn | Price reversing from point 5, ideally on a close back inside |
| Momentum | [Divergence](/divergence/) at point 5 adds weight |
| Stop | Just beyond point 5 |

## Common mistakes

- Picking five swings to fit after the move has happened.
- Entering at point 5 before there is any sign of a turn.
- Treating the 1-4 target line as a prediction instead of a rough guide.
- Using one source's rules and assuming they are universal.
- Taking a real breakout for a point 5 overshoot.
- Ignoring that the stop beyond point 5 must be sized for.

## FAQ

**What is a Wolfe Wave?**
It is a five-point reversal pattern inside a converging wedge. A line from point 1 through point 4 is projected forward as a target for the move that follows point 5.

**Who invented Wolfe Waves?**
They are traditionally credited to Bill Wolfe. There is no standard textbook definition, so descriptions vary between sources.

**What is point 5 supposed to do?**
Point 5 overshoots the line joining points 1 and 3. That overshoot is where the pattern expects the market to turn. Price that keeps running beyond it means the pattern has failed.

**Do Wolfe Waves work?**
They have no academic evidence base. Five points can be chosen after the fact, so past charts look tidy. Real-time results depend on confirmation and risk control.

**How is it different from a wedge?**
A wedge is just two converging lines. A Wolfe Wave adds numbered points, an overshoot at point 5 and a target line from point 1 through point 4.
