Market cycles are the repeating swings of rising and falling prices that traders try to measure. The idea is that markets move through phases, from lows to highs and back, at fairly regular spacing. Traders use cycles to judge which phase a market is in and when the next turn might come.

## What are market cycles?

A cycle is a pattern of rise and fall that repeats. In finance the word covers several different things:

**Economic cycles.** Expansion, peak, contraction and trough in the economy. Economists have proposed named cycles of different lengths, for example the Kitchin (about 3 to 4 years, tied to inventories), the Juglar (about 7 to 11 years, tied to investment) and the Kondratiev (about 50 years). Their exact lengths are debated.

**Market phase models.** The Wyckoff [method](/wyckoff-method/) describes four phases: accumulation, markup, distribution and markdown. Price moves through them in order, in theory.

**Trading cycles.** Short rhythms in price, found by looking at spacing between lows. J. M. Hurst's work on cycle analysis is a well-known example, using nested cycles and moving-average envelopes.

**Seasonal cycles.** Repeating calendar effects. See [seasonality](/seasonality/).

Be careful with the word "cycle". Real markets do not repeat on a fixed clock. Cycle lengths vary and cycles can disappear. Cycle analysis is best seen as a way to describe phases, not a way to forecast exact turning dates.

## How it is identified

A simple, practical approach to a trading cycle:

1. Mark the major swing lows on the chart.
2. Measure the number of bars between each pair.
3. Look for an average spacing.
4. Project the next expected low from the last one, with a window rather than a single bar.
5. Check whether the actual low arrived inside the window.

**Worked example.** A market makes lows on bars 10, 30, 52 and 71. The gaps are 20, 22 and 19 bars, averaging about 20. The last low was on bar 71, so the next is expected around bar 91, with a window of roughly bars 87 to 95. If price makes a low in that window and a reversal signal appears, the cycle idea has some support. If it does not, the cycle has failed to repeat.

## How to read it

**Phase.** Is price near a cycle low, in the middle of a rising phase or near a cycle high? A phase judgement helps decide whether to look for buys or sells.

**Longer cycles dominate.** Short cycles tend to be tied to a longer cycle. Cycle lows inside a rising longer cycle are usually pullback buying spots; highs inside a falling longer cycle are selling spots.

**Translation.** Some cycle writers note that in a rising market the peak tends to come after the middle of the cycle ("right translated"), and in a falling market before it. This is an observation, not a rule.

**Failure.** A low that does not appear in the window, or a cycle that breaks down, is a warning that the structure has changed.

## Best market states

**Reversals (good).** Cycle lows and highs are used to look for turning points, with a reversal signal as the trigger.

**Trends (good).** Longer cycles frame the trend.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways markets often show no clear rhythm, and cycles found there are usually noise.

**Volatility.** Changes in volatility stretch and squeeze cycle length, which makes timing less exact.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Evidence | At least three similar-length swings before trusting a cycle |
| Window | Use a time window, not a single bar |
| Price signal | A reversal or break at the expected time |
| Longer cycle | Direction of the next larger cycle |
| Failure | Plan for a cycle that does not repeat |
| Stop | Beyond the swing extreme the cycle idea relies on |

## Common mistakes

- Treating cycle lengths as fixed. They drift.
- Finding a cycle by fitting it to past data and then trusting it forwards.
- Using one bar as the turning date rather than a window.
- Mixing different kinds of cycle (economic, seasonal, trading) as if they were the same thing.
- Ignoring the larger trend.
- Keeping a cycle count that has clearly broken down.

## FAQ

**What are the four phases of a market cycle?**
The Wyckoff model names accumulation, markup, distribution and markdown. Another common split is expansion, peak, contraction and trough. Real markets do not move through them neatly, and phases vary in length.

**How long is a market cycle?**
There is no fixed length. Economic cycles are often described as lasting several years, and trading cycles can be days or weeks. Lengths vary from one cycle to the next, so use them as rough guides.

**Can you predict turning points with cycles?**
Not reliably. Cycles can suggest a time window when a turn is more likely, but they often fail to repeat. Use a price signal to confirm any turn, and treat the cycle as context.

**Is cycle analysis proven?**
Economic cycles are an area of academic study, though their regularity is debated. Short trading cycles are a trader-education concept without solid academic support. Test any cycle rule on past data before using it.

**How is a market cycle different from seasonality?**
Seasonality is tied to the calendar, such as months or quarters. A market cycle is measured from the price series itself and does not need to match the calendar. The two can overlap, but they are different ideas.
