The Quasimodo pattern, also called over-and-under, is a reversal shape often described as a head and shoulders with a twist. In an uptrend price makes a higher high, falls below the last higher low, then returns to the left shoulder area. Traders watch that area for a short entry. It is a trader-education concept, not a tested rule.

## What is the Quasimodo pattern?

The shape looks like a head and shoulders, but it trades differently. A classic head and shoulders waits for a break of the neckline, after the right shoulder forms. The Quasimodo enters earlier, on a return to the left shoulder, after structure has already changed.

In a bearish version, an uptrend makes a higher low, then a higher high (the head). Price then falls below the last higher low. That break is the first sign that structure has shifted. Price then rises back toward the old left shoulder, and traders look for sellers there.

The name comes from the hunchback in Victor Hugo's novel, because the lopsided shape resembles a hunch. No single inventor is known. It spread through online price-action courses, and no peer-reviewed study supports it.

## How it is drawn

A bearish Quasimodo has four reference points:

1. **Left shoulder:** a swing high in an uptrend, followed by a higher low.
2. **Head:** a higher high than the left shoulder.
3. **Break:** price falls below the higher low that came before the head. This is the break of structure.
4. **Entry zone:** the left shoulder level, usually drawn as a band from the shoulder's high to its body or wick.

The bullish version is the mirror image.

**Worked example.** Price rises to a left shoulder at 100, pulls back to a low at 94, then makes a head at 106. It then falls through 94 and closes at 92. The entry zone is around 100. A short is considered on a return to 100, with a stop above the head at 107. The risk is about 7 points. A first target might be the next swing low below 92.

## How to read it

**The break comes first.** Without a clear close below the last higher low, there is no change of structure and no pattern.

**The return may not happen.** Price may fall away without ever revisiting the left shoulder. A trader who waits will miss the move.

**The reaction matters.** At the zone, traders look for rejection, such as a bearish candle or a failed push, rather than selling blindly into the level.

**Liquidity idea.** Some traders say the head takes the stops above older highs before price reverses. That is a plausible story, not a proven mechanism.

**Failure.** A close above the head cancels the pattern.

## Best market states

**Reversals after a clear trend (best).** The pattern needs a trend to reverse and distinct swings.

**Early trend changes (moderate).** Traders use it to join a change of character before the neckline of a head and shoulders breaks.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways markets are full of small highs and lows that can look like the pattern.

**Strong trends (weak).** A deep pullback in a powerful trend can mimic it and then resume.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear uptrend (or downtrend for the bullish form) |
| Head | A new extreme beyond the left shoulder |
| Break | Close beyond the last higher low (or lower high) |
| Zone | Left shoulder area marked before price returns |
| Reaction | Rejection candle or failed push at the zone |
| Stop | Beyond the head |

## Common mistakes

- Entering before structure has broken, so the "pattern" is only a guess.
- Drawing the zone after the move, picking whichever shoulder fits.
- Assuming price must return. Many moves never retest.
- Using it in ranges, where the shape is easy to find and meaningless.
- Placing the stop inside the head, so a normal retest hits it.
- Treating it as proven. It has no academic evidence base.

## FAQ

**What is a Quasimodo pattern?**
It is a reversal setup that looks like a head and shoulders. Price makes a new extreme, breaks the last swing in the other direction, then returns to the left shoulder area, where traders look for an entry against the old trend.

**What is the difference between a Quasimodo and a head and shoulders?**
A head and shoulders enters on a break of the neckline, after the right shoulder. A Quasimodo enters on a return to the left shoulder, after structure has already broken. It is an earlier and more aggressive entry.

**Where does the stop go on a Quasimodo?**
Most traders place it just beyond the head, because a new extreme there cancels the pattern. That gives a clear invalidation point but can make the stop wide.

**Why is it called Quasimodo?**
The name comes from the hunchback in Victor Hugo's novel. The lopsided shape of the swings looks like a hunch. It is also called over-and-under, after the higher high followed by a lower low.

**Does the Quasimodo pattern work?**
No academic study supports it. It is a trader-education concept, and results depend on how swings are picked and on risk control. Many returns to the zone fail, and many moves never return.
