A three line strike is a four-candle pattern. Three candles move in the same direction, then a fourth long candle goes the other way, opening beyond the third close and closing beyond the first open. It looks like a reversal, but the way it is read differs between sources, so confirmation matters.

## What is a three line strike?

The first three candles form a steady run, like [three white soldiers](/three-white-soldiers/) or [three black crows](/three-black-crows/). Each closes beyond the one before. The fourth candle then reverses the whole run in a single period.

Nison describes the bullish version, with three black candles followed by a white candle that engulfs them, as a signal that can end a decline. The twist is that a trend with three steady candles is a strong trend, so a single large candle against it is a surprising event.

Because of that, interpretations differ. Some sources read it as a reversal. Others read it as a continuation pattern, as in Nison's original description. Treat both readings as possible and test it on your own market.

## How it is identified

For the **bullish** three line strike (after a decline):

1. **Candles 1 to 3:** three bearish candles, each closing lower than the one before.
2. **Candle 4:** a bullish candle that opens below candle 3's close and closes above candle 1's open.

The **bearish** version is the mirror image: three bullish candles, then a bearish candle that opens above candle 3's close and closes below candle 1's open.

**Worked example.** Candle 1 opens at 100 and closes at 97. Candle 2 opens at 97 and closes at 94. Candle 3 opens at 94 and closes at 91. Candle 4 opens at 90.5 and closes at 101, above 100. This is a bullish three line strike.

Software varies on whether the first three candles must open inside the prior body, so check your platform's rule.

## How to read it

The three run candles show a strong trend. The fourth candle shows a sharp and sudden change. It can mean the move is exhausted, or it can be a one-period shake-out before the trend resumes.

The next candle gives a clue. Follow-through in the direction of candle 4 supports a reversal. A return back in the direction of the old trend supports the continuation reading.

## Best market states

**Turning points at known levels (best).** A level that already matters helps judge which reading fits.

**Trends (use care).** The pattern can mark either the end or a pause.

**Ranges (weak).** The run of three candles is less meaningful in a sideways market.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Run | Three clean candles in one direction |
| Candle 4 | Closes beyond candle 1's open |
| Next candle | Follow-through in candle 4's direction |
| Level | Support or resistance nearby |
| Volume | Heavier volume on candle 4, where available |

## Common mistakes

- Assuming it must be a reversal, or that it must be a continuation.
- Entering on candle 4 without waiting for the next candle.
- Ignoring the size of candle 4 compared with the run.
- Using it in ranges.
- Skipping a test on your own market.

## FAQ

**Is a three line strike a reversal or a continuation pattern?**
Sources disagree. The traditional reading is a reversal. Some testing suggests the earlier trend often resumes. Treat the candle as a signal that needs a confirming candle and a level.

**What is the bullish version?**
Three bearish candles in a decline, followed by a long bullish candle that opens lower and closes above the first candle's open. The bearish version is the mirror image.

**How does it differ from an engulfing pattern?**
An engulfing candle covers one candle. The strike candle covers three. That makes it larger and rarer.

**Can I use it as an entry?**
Some traders do, after confirmation. Because the reading is disputed, many use it only as supporting evidence.

**How common is it?**
It is uncommon. Strict versions appear rarely, which makes them hard to test.
