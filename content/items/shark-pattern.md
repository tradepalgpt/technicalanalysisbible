The Shark pattern is a harmonic reversal shape drawn with five points, O, X, A, B and C. Unlike the classic harmonic patterns, it starts with an extension rather than a retracement, and traders look for a turn at point C. It is a trader-education concept with no academic evidence base.

## What is the Shark pattern?

The Shark is one of the newer members of the [harmonic patterns](/harmonic-patterns/) family. Harmonic patterns claim that price swings relate to each other by Fibonacci ratios. Most older patterns, such as the [Gartley](/gartley-pattern/), [Bat](/bat-pattern/), [Butterfly](/butterfly-pattern/) and [Crab](/crab-pattern/), end with a pullback or an extension of the first leg. The Shark differs because its middle legs are extensions, and it ends near a retracement of the early swing.

Descriptions of the Shark vary between sources, and charting platforms do not all use the same ratio ranges. Because of that variation and the lack of independent testing, treat the ratios as areas of interest, not as laws.

Like all harmonic patterns, it has no academic evidence base. Patterns drawn on past charts tend to look neat because the swing points can be picked after the fact.

## How it is drawn

A bullish Shark uses five points in order, labelled O, X, A, B and C in one common scheme:

1. **O to X:** the first leg, a down move. It sets the scale.
2. **X to A:** a move up.
3. **A to B:** a move down that pushes beyond X, so the pattern starts with an extension rather than a retracement.
4. **B to C:** a move up that extends past A.
5. **Completion area:** the point where traders look for a turn, commonly quoted as a retracement of about 0.886 to 1.13 of the first leg.

Sources and platforms disagree on the exact ranges for the middle legs, so this guide does not give fixed bounds for them. Results from one platform are not directly comparable with another.

**Worked example (arithmetic only).** Take a first leg O to X from 100 to 80: 20 points. Suppose the completion area is quoted at 0.886 of that leg, measured from X back toward O. That is 80 + 0.886 x 20 = 80 + 17.72 = **97.72**. At 1.13 of the leg it would be 80 + 22.6 = 102.6. Which swing is measured, and from which point, differs between sources, so this shows only how the ratio arithmetic works.

**Stops.** The stop is usually placed just beyond the completion area, because a move past it breaks the shape.

## How to read it

**Reversal zone.** The completion area is a possible turning point, not a signal. Most traders want evidence of a turn, such as a reversal candle or [divergence](/divergence/), before acting.

**Invalid shapes.** If the legs do not fit the quoted ranges, or a leg moves past the completion area before it forms, the shape does not count.

**Targets.** Typical targets are Fibonacci retracements of the final leg. These are conventions, not tested rules.

**Role of the first leg.** The first leg sets the scale for everything else. A tiny first leg gives tiny ratios, which can fit almost any later swing.

## Best market states

**Reversal after a stretched move (best).** The pattern assumes the final leg is overextended.

**Trends (caution).** In a strong trend, the completion area often fails to hold.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways markets offer many small swings that can be forced to fit.

**Breakouts (weak).** A breakout can go straight through the completion area.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Ratios | Legs fall inside the ranges quoted by your source |
| Extension | The B leg clearly extends past the start of the shape |
| Price action | A reversal candle or turn at the completion area |
| Momentum | [Divergence](/divergence/) adds weight |
| Level | Completion area lines up with [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) |
| Stop | Just beyond the completion point |

## Common mistakes

- Picking swing points to fit the ratios after the fact.
- Using ratio ranges from one source and assuming everyone uses them.
- Entering at the completion area with no sign of a turn.
- Forgetting that the stop is usually wide.
- Mixing it up with the older harmonic patterns, which have different structures.
- Taking neat results on past charts as proof the pattern works.

## FAQ

**What is the Shark pattern?**
It is a harmonic reversal pattern built from five swing points and Fibonacci ratios. It starts with an extension rather than a retracement, and traders watch the final point as a possible turning area.

**How is it different from the Gartley or Bat?**
Those older patterns use retracements of the first leg and end at a fixed ratio of it. The Shark uses extensions in its middle legs, so its shape and ratio ranges are different.

**Who created the Shark pattern?**
It is not attributed to one person here. It is a more recent addition to the harmonic family, and accounts of its origin differ.

**Are the ratios fixed?**
No. Sources and platforms define the ranges differently. That is why the figures in any one description should be treated as typical rather than exact.

**Does the Shark pattern work?**
It has no academic evidence base. Results on past charts are easy to flatter, since swing points can be picked afterwards. Real-time results depend on confirmation and risk control.
