Fibonacci arcs are curved lines drawn around the end of a price move, at 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% of the distance between a swing low and a swing high. Traders watch them as curved support or resistance that mixes price and time. They are a niche tool, and their shape depends heavily on the chart scale.

## What are Fibonacci arcs?

Fibonacci retracement uses flat lines. Fibonacci arcs use circles. After a trend move, an arc is drawn centred on the end of that move, with a radius equal to a Fibonacci ratio of the distance between the two swing points.

As time passes, the arc curves. Its price level changes bar by bar, so a trader reading the chart sees a line that moves with time as well as price. The idea is that pullbacks may find support or resistance on these curves, which respond to how long the move has taken.

There is no single inventor, and no academic evidence that price respects arcs. Because the tool uses both axes of the chart, the shape on screen changes if you zoom or change the scale, which is its main weakness.

## How it is drawn

1. Pick the two ends of a move: the swing low and swing high in an uptrend.
2. Measure the distance between them on the chart. The tool does this on the chart's own pixel scale, which is why zooming changes the arcs.
3. Draw a circle centred on the end of the move (the swing high in an uptrend), with a radius of 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% of that distance.
4. Keep only the half of each circle that lies back towards the start of the move. Those curves are the arcs.

**Worked example.** A move runs from a low at (bar 0, price 100) to a high at (bar 20, price 140). Suppose the chart's scale makes the straight-line distance between the points 100 units on screen. The arcs have radii of 38.2, 50 and 61.8 units, centred on the high at bar 20. At bar 20 the 50% arc is 50 units below the high on the screen's scale, and it curves back upwards on either side. The exact price it meets depends on how many screen units a dollar represents. Change the scale and the arc meets a different price.

That worked example shows the problem: the numbers only mean something for one particular chart.

## How to read it

**Support in an uptrend.** Price falls back to an arc and stalls. The 38.2% arc is the shallowest, which suggests a strong trend.

**Resistance in a downtrend.** Draw from a high to a low; rallies may stall at the arcs.

**Arc breaks.** A close through the 61.8% arc suggests the pullback is deeper than normal and the trend is under pressure.

**Clusters.** An arc crossing a flat Fibonacci level or an old support level gives a point where several ideas agree.

**Time element.** Because arcs curve, a touch early in the pullback happens at a different price from a touch later. The tool therefore says "support is here at this time", not just "support is here".

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** Arcs need a clear swing and a pullback that keeps pace with the trend.

**Ranges (weak).** There is no trend swing to measure.

**Breakouts and volatility.** Fast moves overshoot any curve. Gaps in particular make the arcs less useful.

**Reversals.** A pullback that slices through all three arcs may be an early sign that a reversal is happening, but it should be confirmed with other tools.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Anchor points | Clear, widely seen swings |
| Scale | Same chart scale every time you draw (linear or log) |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or stall at the arc |
| Confluence | An arc near a flat Fibonacci level or horizontal support |
| Break | A close beyond the arc, not just a wick |
| Stop | Beyond the next arc or beyond the pullback swing |

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting that the arcs change shape when the chart is zoomed or its scale is changed.
- Comparing arcs drawn on different monitors, platforms or chart widths.
- Moving the anchor points until the arcs seem to fit past price.
- Expecting each touch to produce a bounce.
- Using arcs in sideways markets.
- Relying on arcs alone instead of looking for confluence.

## FAQ

**What are Fibonacci arcs used for?**
They mark curved support or resistance around the end of a move, at 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% of the distance between the two swing points. Traders watch for a pullback to stall at an arc.

**Why do Fibonacci arcs change when I zoom the chart?**
The radius is measured on the screen scale, using both price and time. Changing the scale changes how many screen units a price move represents, so the arcs land at different prices. This makes them less repeatable than flat levels.

**What is the difference between Fibonacci arcs and fans?**
Both use the same ratios. Arcs are curves around the end of the move, while fans are straight lines sloping out from the start. Both change price with time.

**Do Fibonacci arcs work?**
There is no academic evidence that price respects them more often than chance. Some traders use them as one clue among several. Test the tool on the market and time frame you trade.

**Which platforms offer Fibonacci arcs?**
Many charting packages include them in the Fibonacci toolset. Default levels and options differ, so check the settings before comparing charts.
