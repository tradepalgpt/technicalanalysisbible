A pennant is a short continuation pattern. A sharp move (the flagpole) is followed by a small, tight consolidation in which the highs and lows converge like a tiny triangle. A break in the direction of the pole suggests the earlier move may resume.

## What is a pennant pattern?

A pennant is a brief pause after a fast move. Price surges up (or crashes down) in a steep run, the flagpole. It then stalls and trades in a narrowing range, with lower highs and higher lows squeezing together. The shape looks like a small, symmetrical triangle sitting on the pole, like a flag on a flagpole. When price breaks out of the squeeze in the direction of the pole, the pattern is complete.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, describe pennants as close relatives of flags. Both form during rapid moves and last only a short time. The difference is in the shape: a flag's lines run parallel, while a pennant's lines converge. Murphy treats the two together as the best-known short continuation patterns.

A pennant is also close to a [symmetrical triangle](/symmetrical-triangle/), but smaller, shorter and attached to a sharp pole.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **Flagpole:** a sharp, steep move, usually on strong volume.
2. **Pennant:** a short, tight consolidation with two converging lines, one falling through the highs and one rising through the lows.
3. **Breakout:** a close beyond either line, expected in the direction of the pole.

The pennant should be short compared with the pole. Edwards and Magee describe these patterns as forming quickly, normally within a few weeks on daily charts. A long, drawn-out squeeze is a triangle rather than a pennant.

**Measured target.** Measure the length of the flagpole, from the start of the sharp move to its end. Add it to the breakout point for an upward move or subtract it for a downward one. The idea comes from the half-mast reading, where the pause forms around the middle of a fast move.

**Worked example.** The pole runs from 40 to 50: a length of 10. The pennant squeezes between 47 and 50, and price closes above the upper line at 49. The measured target is 49 + 10 = **59**. After a fall from 60 to 50 and a break below the lower line at 51, the target would be 51 - 10 = **41**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Direction.** The expectation is a break in the direction of the pole. A break against the pole is a warning that the pause was in fact a turning point.

**Volume.** The usual picture is heavy volume on the pole, falling volume through the pennant and a rise on the breakout. Quiet trade in the pennant suggests a pause, not a change of heart.

**Timing.** The break normally arrives before the lines meet. A pennant that drifts right to its tip without a break loses force.

**Retest.** Price sometimes returns to the broken line before continuing. Some traders enter on that retest.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks out in the expected direction and then falls back inside, or breaks the opposite line, the continuation idea has failed.

## Best market states

**Strong, fast trends (best).** The pattern needs a steep pole. It is a pause in momentum, not a reversal.

**Breakouts.** The converging lines give clear trigger levels, though they are tight.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market there is no pole, so a small squeeze is just noise.

**Slow trends (weak).** Without a sharp move before it, a pennant has nothing to continue.

**Volatility.** The squeeze reflects a fall in volatility. When the break comes, volatility usually rises again, so stops sized to the pennant can be hit by normal noise.

| Pennant | Short, with two converging lines |
| Touches | At least two on each line |
| Volume | Fading inside the pennant, rising on the break |
| Break | Close beyond a line in the direction of the pole |
| Stop | Beyond the opposite end of the pennant |

## Common mistakes

- Seeing a pennant without a real pole.
- Letting the pattern run so long that it becomes a triangle.
- Assuming the break must go with the pole. It can fail.
- Entering inside the pennant before the break.
- Treating the pole-length target as certain.
- Confusing a pennant with a flag. Parallel lines make a flag, converging lines make a pennant.

## FAQ

**Is a pennant bullish or bearish?**
It depends on the pole. A pennant after a sharp rise is bullish, and one after a sharp fall is bearish. In both cases the expectation is that the earlier move resumes.

**How do you calculate the pennant target?**
Measure the flagpole, then add it to the breakout point for an upward move or subtract it for a downward one. This is a guide only, and many pennants fall short.

**What is the difference between a pennant and a flag?**
A flag has two parallel lines and tilts against the trend. A pennant has two converging lines, like a small triangle. Both follow a sharp move and both are short-lived.

**What is the difference between a pennant and a symmetrical triangle?**
A pennant is small and short and follows a sharp pole. A symmetrical triangle is usually larger and longer, and does not need a pole. The shapes are similar, so the context decides.

**How long should a pennant last?**
Edwards and Magee describe such patterns as forming quickly, normally within a few weeks on daily charts. The pennant should be short compared with the pole. If it keeps going, it is no longer behaving like one.
