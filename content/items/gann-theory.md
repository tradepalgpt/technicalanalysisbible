Gann theory is a collection of market methods attributed to the American trader William Delbert Gann. It combines price and time, using angles, a grid of numbers, retracement levels and cycles. It is a trader-education tradition, not an academic one, and there is no peer-reviewed evidence that it works. This page explains the ideas neutrally.

## What is Gann theory?

William Delbert Gann (1878 to 1955) was a trader who wrote books and courses on markets from the 1920s onwards. His central claim was that markets move in geometric and mathematical patterns, and that time matters as much as price. He gave his tools names and rules, and later writers added their own interpretations.

Gann's own writing is often vague, and many of the rules in circulation come from later authors. For that reason, two people who say they use Gann methods may be using quite different things.

Important caution: Gann is a trader-education concept. Academic studies have not established that Gann tools predict prices. Where they seem to work, the explanation may be that they draw many lines, so something is always near price. Treat them as a way to frame a chart, and test any rule before relying on it.

## How it is identified

The main ideas, described in broad terms:

**Gann angles.** Lines drawn from a swing high or low at fixed slopes of price per unit of time. The 1x1 angle moves one price unit per bar, a 45-degree line on a chart scaled to match. See [Gann fan](/gann-fan/).

**Square of nine.** A spiral of numbers used to find possible support and resistance levels from square roots. See [Gann square of nine](/gann-square-of-nine/).

**Percentage retracements.** Gann commonly used 50% and 12.5% steps (eighths) of a range as levels, along with 33% and 67%.

**Time cycles.** Gann looked for repeating time spans, such as 90, 180 and 360 days, between turning points. See [market cycles](/market-cycles/).

**Worked example.** A stock rises from 100 to 140. The 50% level of that range is 120. The eighths of the range are 5 points apart (40 divided by 8), giving levels at 105, 110, 115, 120, 125, 130 and 135. A Gann-style analyst would watch those levels for pauses or reversals, and would also note the calendar spacing from the low.

## How to read it

**Trend filter.** Price above a rising 1x1 angle is read as strength; a break below is read as a warning of a trend change.

**Levels.** The 50% retracement is the most used Gann level. Others come from eighths and thirds.

**Time.** Turns are expected when time and price tools agree, for example a level hit on an anniversary of an earlier turn.

**Honest limits.** Rules vary, charts depend on scale (angles change with how the chart is drawn), and the tools generate many levels. That makes testing hard and confirmation bias easy.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** Angles and fans are mainly used to judge whether a trend is intact.

**Reversals (moderate).** Retracement and time levels are used to look for turning points. Hits are easy to find after the fact.

**Ranges (weak).** Angles drawn from highs and lows have no clear role in sideways markets.

**Volatility.** Very volatile markets cross many levels quickly, which makes any single level less meaningful.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Scale | Fix the chart scale before drawing angles; angles change with scale |
| Anchor | Use clear swing highs and lows |
| Agreement | More than one tool pointing to the same level |
| Price action | A reversal signal at the level, not just arrival |
| Testing | Check your rules on past data before relying on them |
| Stop | Beyond the level or angle that proves the idea wrong |

## Common mistakes

- Assuming there is one agreed Gann method. There are many versions.
- Drawing angles on a chart with the scale changed after the fact.
- Counting hits and ignoring misses.
- Using so many levels that price is always near one.
- Treating a time cycle as certain.
- Expecting academic support. There is none.

## FAQ

**Who was W. D. Gann?**
William Delbert Gann (1878 to 1955) was an American trader and author who wrote on markets from the 1920s. He is known for angle lines, the square of nine and time-based ideas. Many later authors expanded his work, sometimes in conflicting ways.

**Does Gann theory work?**
There is no academic evidence that it does. Some traders use it as a framework for drawing levels and judging trends. Many Gann levels exist, so price is often near one, which can give a false impression of accuracy.

**What are Gann angles?**
They are lines drawn from a swing point at fixed slopes of price against time. The 1x1 line is the best known. They depend on the chart scale, so the same angle looks different on different charts.

**What is the square of nine?**
It is a spiral arrangement of numbers that some traders use to find possible support and resistance. It is covered on its own page at [Gann square of nine](/gann-square-of-nine/).

**Is Gann theory a good place to start?**
Probably not. The core tools are less standardised than moving averages, support and resistance or momentum indicators. If you try it, treat it as an experiment and keep careful records of how it performs.
