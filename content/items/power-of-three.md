The Power of Three, also called AMD, is an ICT and smart money model that describes a period such as a day as three phases: accumulation, manipulation and distribution. The manipulation phase moves against the real direction, and the distribution phase carries price the other way. It is a trader-education idea with no academic evidence base.

## What is the Power of Three?

The model splits a candle into three parts:

1. **Accumulation.** Price moves in a quiet range near the open, often during the Asian session in foreign exchange. Large participants are said to be building positions.
2. **Manipulation.** Price makes a false move away from the open, against the direction of the real move. This is sometimes called a Judas swing, and is said to sweep stops and trap traders.
3. **Distribution.** Price reverses and carries through in the real direction, finishing the period.

The model comes from the ICT (Inner Circle Trader) teaching community and is part of [smart money concepts](/smart-money-concepts/). It resembles the older Wyckoff idea of accumulation followed by a test and a markup, and some teachers draw that link, but the two are separate frameworks. Neither the model nor its claims about large participants have an academic evidence base. This guide presents it as a convention, not a law.

## How it is identified

The pattern is read on a higher-time-frame candle, such as the daily, using a lower time frame to see the phases.

**Bullish version.** The day opens, ranges briefly, then drops below the open (manipulation) before rising above it and closing high.

**Bearish version.** The day opens, ranges briefly, then rises above the open before falling and closing low.

**Worked example (bullish day).** The daily open is 100.

- Accumulation: price trades between 99.5 and 100.5 early on.
- Manipulation: price drops to 96, below the open and below the earlier range low, sweeping stops.
- Distribution: price rallies through the open and closes at 108.

The daily candle has a long lower wick of 4 below the open and a close 8 above it. Traders who follow the model would have looked for longs after the drop to 96, when price was below the open (in "discount") and bias was bullish.

This is a hindsight view. In real time, nobody knows if a drop below the open is a manipulation or the start of a real decline. Teachers add rules, such as waiting for a sweep followed by a [displacement](/displacement/) move.

## How to read it

**Bias.** If your higher time frame view is bullish, you expect a dip below the open, then a rally. You look for longs below the open, not above it.

**Where entries are.** The manipulation phase is expected to reach a high or low, so traders watch [liquidity](/liquidity-sweep/) beyond obvious levels. Entry is planned after the sweep, once price returns through the level.

**Timing.** The phases are often linked to session windows, for example manipulation in the London window and distribution in New York. See [kill zones](/kill-zones/).

**Weekly version.** The same idea can be applied to the week, with the weekly open as the reference. See [weekly and monthly open](/weekly-monthly-open/).

**Failure.** If price drops below the open and keeps falling with no recovery, the "manipulation" was a genuine move. The model has no way to tell this in advance.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** The model describes a false move followed by a reverse.

**Ranges.** Opening ranges and false breaks of range edges fit the pattern naturally.

**Strong trend days (weak).** If price trends from the open with no counter-move, no manipulation phase appears, and the model has nothing to say.

**Volatility.** News-driven days can sweep both sides, which makes the phases impossible to read.

## Settings

There are no formal settings. The common choices are:

| Choice | Typical option |
|---|---|
| Period | Daily candle, or weekly for a longer view |
| Reference | The period's open price |
| Time marking | Session lines for Asian, London and New York |
| Lower time frame | 5 minute to 1 hour to see the phases |

The model is flexible enough that almost any candle can be described afterwards as showing it. To test it honestly, write the rules down in advance and check them on past days, including the days where it did not fit.

## Common mistakes

- Seeing the pattern only in hindsight and counting it as a forecast.
- Labelling every dip below the open as manipulation.
- Ignoring the news calendar, where a release explains the move.
- Treating the open as a rule rather than a reference. Price does not have to return to it.
- Using the model with no higher time frame view of direction.
- Confusing it with Wyckoff phases, which are a separate framework.

## FAQ

**What is the Power of Three in trading?**
It is an ICT model that divides a day or week into accumulation, manipulation and distribution. Price ranges, makes a false move against the real direction, and then moves the real way. It is a trader-education concept with no academic evidence base.

**What does AMD stand for?**
AMD stands for accumulation, manipulation and distribution. These are the three phases of the model. The same letters are also the ticker symbol of a chip company, so check context when searching.

**How do you use the Power of Three?**
Traders form a bias from a higher time frame, expect a false move against it near the open, and look for entries after that move is swept. Write the rules down and test them on past days before trading real money.

**Is the Power of Three the same as Wyckoff?**
No. Some teachers compare the two because both describe a build-up, a false move and a trend. Wyckoff is an older and broader framework with its own rules. They are separate methods.

**Does the Power of Three always happen?**
No. Many days trend from the open with no manipulation phase, and others sweep both sides. The model is flexible enough to describe many candles after the fact, so judge it by results on clearly defined rules.
