The Cypher pattern is a harmonic reversal shape drawn with five points, X, A, B, C and D. Unlike most harmonic patterns, point C pushes beyond point A, and the final point D is measured against the whole X-to-C swing. Traders look for a turn at D. It is a trader-education concept with no academic evidence base.

## What is the Cypher pattern?

The Cypher belongs to the family of [harmonic patterns](/harmonic-patterns/). These patterns claim that price swings tend to relate to each other by Fibonacci ratios, such as 0.382, 0.618, 0.786 and 1.272. The Cypher is a four-leg shape. It looks like the [Gartley pattern](/gartley-pattern/) at first, but its third leg breaks past the start of the second leg.

A bullish Cypher starts with an upswing from X to A, then pulls back to B, then pushes above A to C, and finally drops to D. Traders watch D as a possible low. The bearish version is the mirror image.

Harmonic patterns are popular among retail traders, but they have no academic evidence base. Many drawn patterns are fitted after the event, which makes them look more reliable on a past chart than they are in real time. Treat the ratios as areas of interest, not as laws.

## How it is drawn

Take a bullish Cypher. The points run in order:

1. **X to A:** the first leg, an up move.
2. **A to B:** a pullback that retraces 0.382 to 0.618 of XA.
3. **B to C:** a rally that extends beyond A, to 1.272 to 1.414 of XA, measured from X in the direction of the first leg.
4. **C to D:** a fall that retraces 0.786 of the X-to-C swing.

Point D is the potential reversal zone, where traders watch for a low. Ratio ranges differ a little between books and software. The 1.272 to 1.414 range for C is the usual one, but some sources and platforms quote slightly different bounds.

**Worked example.** X is at 100 and A is at 120, so XA is 20 points. B retraces 0.5 of XA, so B is at 110. C extends 1.272 of XA from X: 100 + 1.272 x 20 = 125.4, rounded to 125. The X-to-C swing is 25 points. D retraces 0.786 of that: 125 - 0.786 x 25 = 125 - 19.65 = **105.35**. That is the area where a bullish turn is expected, with a stop below X at 100.

## How to read it

**Reversal zone.** D is not a signal on its own. It marks an area where price may turn. Most traders wait for evidence of a turn, such as a reversal candle, before acting.

**Stop placement.** The common stop sits just beyond X, because a move past X breaks the whole structure. Placing it there gives a clear risk, though it can be wide.

**Targets.** Typical targets are retracements of the C-to-D leg, such as 0.382 and 0.618, or a return to C. These are conventions, not tested rules.

**Invalid shapes.** If B goes beyond A or C falls short of A, the shape is not a Cypher. If D passes X, the pattern is broken.

## Best market states

**Reversal after a stretched move (best).** The pattern assumes the C-to-D leg is an overextended swing ready to reverse.

**Trends (caution).** In a strong trend, D often fails to hold and price carries on through it.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways markets produce many small swings that can be forced into the ratios, so false patterns are common.

**Breakouts (weak).** A breakout can slice straight through D.

| Level | D lines up with [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) |
| Stop | Just beyond X |

## Common mistakes

- Forcing a pattern onto the chart by choosing swing points that fit the ratios afterwards.
- Entering at D without any sign of a turn.
- Treating the ratio ranges as exact when platforms define them slightly differently.
- Ignoring the wide stop beyond X when sizing the trade.
- Mistaking the Cypher for a Gartley or Bat, which have different ratios and a different point C.
- Assuming the pattern works because it looks neat on historical charts.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between a Cypher and a Gartley?**
A Gartley keeps point C below point A and ends at 0.786 of XA. A Cypher pushes C beyond A and measures D against the X-to-C swing. The two shapes look alike early on, then diverge at the third leg.

**Where does a Cypher pattern end?**
It ends at point D, commonly 0.786 of the X-to-C swing. Traders treat that area as a possible turning point and look for a reversal sign before acting.

**Who invented the Cypher pattern?**
It is not attributed to one person here. It grew out of the wider harmonic trading community, and accounts of its origin differ.

**Is the Cypher pattern reliable?**
It has no academic evidence base. Patterns drawn on past charts look tidy because swing points can be chosen after the fact. Real-time results depend on risk control and confirmation.

**Where does the stop go on a Cypher?**
Most traders put it just beyond point X. A move past X breaks the pattern. The stop can be wide, so position size must allow for it.
