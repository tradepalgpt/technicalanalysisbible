The Schiff pitchfork is a variation of [Andrews' Pitchfork](/andrews-pitchfork/). It uses the same three pivot points, but starts the median line from the midpoint of the first two pivots instead of from the first pivot. This changes the slope, which can make it fit steep or very early trends better.

## What is the Schiff pitchfork?

A pitchfork is three parallel lines drawn from three swing points. The middle line is called the median line, and the outer two are drawn parallel to it. Price is expected to spend time between the outer lines and to react at them.

Alan Andrews' original version starts the median line at the first pivot. The Schiff version moves that starting point. The variation is named after Schiff, but reliable detail about who this was, and when it first appeared, is hard to find, so this guide does not give a year. The tool has no academic evidence base; it is a charting convention used by some traders.

Changing the start point changes the slope of all three lines. In a trend that begins with a long, steep first leg, the original pitchfork can slope too steeply to be useful. The Schiff version usually gives a flatter, wider fork in that situation.

## How it is drawn

1. Pick three alternating swing points: pivot 1 (P1), pivot 2 (P2) and pivot 3 (P3). In an uptrend, P1 is a low, P2 is a high and P3 is a higher low.
2. Draw a line between P1 and P2 and find its midpoint. The Schiff median line starts there.
3. Find the midpoint of the line between P2 and P3.
4. Draw the median line from the start in step 2 through the midpoint in step 3, and extend it forward.
5. Draw the upper line through P2 and the lower line through P3, both parallel to the median line.

Platforms differ. Some move the start point in both time and price (the version described here). A "modified Schiff" variant, offered by some platforms, moves it only part of the way or only along one axis. Check the tool you use.

**Worked example.** P1 is at bar 0, price 100. P2 is at bar 10, price 120. P3 is at bar 20, price 110.

- Midpoint of P1 and P2: bar 5, price 110. This is the Schiff start.
- Midpoint of P2 and P3: bar 15, price 115.
- Slope of the median line: (115 - 110) / (15 - 5) = 0.5 per bar.
- At bar 25 the median line is at 115 + 0.5 x 10 = **120**.

The Andrews version would start at bar 0, price 100 and pass through the same midpoint (bar 15, price 115), giving a slope of 1.0 per bar. At bar 25 that line would be at 125.

## How to read it

**Median line.** Price tends to return to the median line, and the traditional teaching is that it often reacts there, or moves on to the opposite outer line.

**Outer lines.** The upper and lower lines act as possible support and resistance. A touch of the lower line in an uptrend can be a place to look for an entry.

**Breaks.** A close outside the fork, especially through the lower line, suggests the trend has changed pace or ended. A close back inside the fork can be a failed break.

**Comparing the two forks.** Some traders draw both the Andrews and Schiff pitchforks on the same pivots. Where lines from both agree, the area is given more weight.

These readings are traditions rather than measured rules. The tool does not tell you how far a move will travel.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The tool assumes price moves in a sloping channel. When pivots form a steady trend, the lines give a visual map.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, three pivots can be joined into a fork almost anywhere, and the lines give little help.

**Reversals.** A clean break of the fork can mark a trend change, but a break is only a warning that needs support from price action.

**Volatility.** Wide swings make pivots hard to choose and the forks unstable.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Pivots | Clear, well-spaced swing points that alternate |
| Fit | Price has respected the outer lines at least once |
| Entry signal | A rejection candle or a close back inside at a line |
| Stop | Beyond the line or the swing that proves the idea wrong |
| Redraw | If pivots change, redraw rather than stretch the old fork |

## Common mistakes

- Choosing pivots after the fact to make the fork fit.
- Using the Schiff version just because it looks better on one chart.
- Treating the median line as a certain target. Price often passes it.
- Forgetting that platforms position the start of the median line in different ways.
- Trading in a sideways market where there is no trend to measure.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between Andrews and Schiff pitchfork?**
Both use three pivots. The Andrews median line starts at the first pivot, and the Schiff median line starts at the midpoint of the first two pivots. This usually makes the Schiff fork flatter and can fit better when the first leg is steep.

**Who invented the Schiff pitchfork?**
It is named after a trader called Schiff, as a variation of Alan Andrews' pitchfork. Reliable details about the origin and date are hard to find, so this guide does not state them.

**Is the Schiff pitchfork better than the Andrews pitchfork?**
Neither has been shown to be better. The Schiff version can fit some charts more naturally, but this is a visual judgement. Test both on the market you trade before relying on either.

**What is a modified Schiff pitchfork?**
It is a further variation that some platforms offer, where the start of the median line moves only partway or along one axis. The details differ between platforms, so check the documentation of the tool you use.

**Does the Schiff pitchfork work on all time frames?**
It can be drawn on any time frame, since it only needs swing points. The same limits apply at all of them: it needs a clear trend and well-chosen pivots, and it has no academic evidence base.
