The Volatility Contraction Pattern (VCP) is a base pattern in which a stock in an uptrend pulls back several times, with each pullback shallower than the one before and volume drying up. It shows selling pressure fading as supply is absorbed. Traders look to buy when price breaks above the high of the last, tightest contraction. It was popularised by Mark Minervini.

## What is the Volatility Contraction Pattern?

Stocks do not usually rise in a straight line. They advance, pause and consolidate. In a VCP, the pause is a sequence of pullbacks that get smaller. For example, the first drop from the high might be 20 per cent, the next 12 per cent, then 6 per cent and finally 3 per cent. The shrinking swings suggest that fewer holders are willing to sell at lower prices, so supply is drying up.

Mark Minervini described the pattern in his book *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard* (McGraw-Hill, 2013). It is a trader-education pattern built on his own method and observations. It is not backed by independent academic research, so treat claims about how well it works with care and test it yourself.

The VCP is a close relative of other base patterns such as the [cup and handle](/cup-and-handle/) and the [ascending triangle](/ascending-triangle/). It is a way of describing a base through its shrinking volatility, so it can look like either.

## How it is identified

A VCP usually has these parts:

1. **A prior uptrend.** The stock is already in a stage of advance, often above its rising 50-day and 200-day [moving averages](/simple-moving-average/).
2. **A first pullback.** A larger decline from the high, often the deepest of the base.
3. **Further pullbacks, each smaller.** Both the depth and often the width in time shrink.
4. **Falling volume.** Volume dries up as the base tightens, especially in the last contraction.
5. **A pivot.** The high of the last contraction, or the overall resistance line across the base.
6. **A breakout.** Price closes or trades above the pivot, ideally on higher volume.

Minervini writes contractions in shorthand such as "3T" meaning three contractions. The number is descriptive, not a rule. Two to six is common.

**Worked example.** A stock reaches a high of 100.
- First pullback: down to 80, a drop of 20 per cent.
- Rally to 98, then second pullback to 86. The drop is (98 - 86) / 98, about 12 per cent.
- Rally to 99, then third pullback to 93. The drop is (99 - 93) / 99, about 6 per cent.
- Price then rises toward 99, the pivot.

The depths run 20, 12 and 6 per cent, each smaller than the last. A close above the pivot of 99 on higher volume would trigger the entry. A stop could go under the last low of 93, a risk of about 6 per cent from 99. The levels and stop are examples, not recommendations.

## How to read it

**Shrinking swings.** The tighter each pullback, the more it suggests sellers are exhausted. A pullback larger than the one before breaks the pattern.

**Volume dry-up.** Low volume in the final contraction suggests few sellers remain. A surge in volume on the breakout is the typical confirmation.

**The pivot.** Entry is above the pivot, not inside the base. Buying before the break means buying a pattern that has not been confirmed.

**Stop placement.** The stop usually sits just below the last, tightest contraction low. Because the final contraction is small, the stop distance is small too, which helps the risk-reward ratio. A tight stop also means a normal wobble can trigger it.

**Relative strength.** Many VCP traders also want the stock to be outperforming the market, so they check its relative strength against an index. This is part of the method, not part of the pattern.

**Failure.** If price breaks below the last low or the breakout fails and returns into the base, the setup is over. See [false breakout](/false-breakout/).

## Best market states

**Breakouts (best).** The VCP is a breakout setup. It aims to catch the move from a tight base into a new advance.

**Strong uptrends in the market as a whole (important).** The method assumes a healthy general market. In a broad sell-off, even good-looking bases tend to fail more often. This is the usual view among practitioners, not a measured figure here.

**Volatility.** The pattern is defined by shrinking volatility, so it needs a drop in swing size before the trigger.

**Ranges (weak).** In a flat sideways market with no prior advance, a series of shrinking swings is just a range.

**Reversals (weak).** The VCP is a continuation pattern in an uptrend. It is not a bottoming pattern.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear advance before the base |
| Contractions | Two or more, each smaller than the last |
| Volume | Falling through the base, lowest near the end |
| Pivot | A clear resistance level at the top of the last contraction |
| Breakout | Price moves above the pivot, ideally on above-average volume |
| Stop | Below the low of the last contraction |
| Market backdrop | The broad market is not in a downtrend |

## Common mistakes

- Buying inside the base before the pivot breaks.
- Counting contractions loosely, so any bumpy base is called a VCP.
- Ignoring the volume dry-up, which is a core part of the idea.
- Taking breakouts in a falling market.
- Setting a stop so tight that ordinary noise triggers it.
- Treating the pattern as a promise. A breakout can fail like any other.

## FAQ

**What is a volatility contraction pattern?**
It is a base where a stock pulls back several times in a row, with each pullback smaller than the last and volume drying up. The shrinking swings suggest selling is fading. Traders look to buy a breakout above the final pivot.

**Who created the VCP?**
Mark Minervini popularised the pattern and described it in his 2013 book *Trade Like a Stock Market Wizard*. It builds on older base ideas such as those of other growth-stock traders, but the VCP name and method are his.

**How many contractions should a VCP have?**
Two to six are common. There is no fixed number. What matters is that each pullback is smaller than the one before and that volume falls as the base tightens.

**Where do you put the stop on a VCP?**
Many traders put it just below the low of the last contraction. That keeps the risk small. It also means normal volatility may hit it, so size the position to the stop and plan for a failed breakout.

**Is the VCP the same as a cup and handle?**
No, though they can look alike. A cup and handle is a rounded shape, with one pullback at the end. A VCP is defined by a sequence of shrinking pullbacks and can take several shapes, sometimes including a cup with a handle.
