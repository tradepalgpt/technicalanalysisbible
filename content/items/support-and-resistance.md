Support is a price area where falling prices have often stopped or bounced. Resistance is an area where rising prices have often stalled or turned down. Traders mark these levels from past turning points and use them to plan entries, stops and exits. They are areas of interest, not exact lines, and they can fail.

## What is support and resistance?

Markets rarely move in a straight line. Price rises, stalls, falls back and tries again. The places where it stalled before are remembered by traders, and many place orders near them. That is the usual explanation for why levels seem to "hold": a cluster of buyers waits near support and a cluster of sellers waits near resistance.

Support sits below the current price. Resistance sits above it. Both are best thought of as zones a little wider than a single price. A level that has been tested several times is generally seen as more significant, though each test also uses up some of the orders waiting there, so a level can eventually give way.

No single person invented the idea. It appears in the earliest books on chart reading, including Edwards and Magee, and in nearly every later textbook. Academic work has also looked at it. Carol Osler's study of published levels in currency markets found that they predicted short-term trend interruptions better than chance would suggest.

## How it is identified

1. **Find swing points.** Mark clear highs and lows where price turned. See [swing highs and lows](/swing-highs-lows/).
2. **Look for repeats.** Two or more turns near the same price make a level more believable.
3. **Draw a zone, not a hair-thin line.** Use the cluster of wicks and closes. A zone about the size of a typical bar's range is common.
4. **Add other markers.** Round numbers, the [previous day's high and low](/previous-day-high-low/) and [pivot points](/pivot-points/) often line up with the same areas.

**Worked example.** A share turns down at 50.00, then at 50.20, then at 49.90. Those three highs sit within about 30 pence of each other, so the resistance zone is roughly 49.90 to 50.20. If price later closes at 51.00, the zone has been broken, and 50.00 may now act as support.

## How to read it

**Bounce.** Price reaches a level and turns away. Range traders often enter near the edge, with a stop just beyond it.

**Break.** Price closes clearly beyond the level. A break with strong volume is more convincing than a drift through on thin trade.

**Retest.** After a break, price often returns to the old level to test it from the other side. This is the idea behind [role reversal](/role-reversal/).

**False break.** Price pokes through a level, then closes back inside. See [false breakout](/false-breakout/). These are common and are why many traders wait for a close rather than a touch.

**Strength of a level.** More touches, a longer history, a bigger reaction and a link to a higher time frame all add weight. Levels from daily and weekly charts usually matter more than levels from a one-minute chart.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** When price is boxed between a floor and a ceiling, the edges are clear and the risk can be small, because a stop sits just outside the zone.

**Breakouts (good).** The level is the line that price must clear. Traders watch the close, volume and the retest.

**Reversals (good, with confirmation).** A turn at a major level, with a reversal candle or divergence, is a common setup. The level alone is not the signal.

**Strong trends (weak).** In a fast trend, price may run through level after level. Buying support in a strong downtrend is a well-known way to be caught.

**Volatility.** In wild markets levels become wide zones. Stops need more room, or the trade is skipped.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Touches | At least two clear turns at the level |
| Time frame | Higher time frame levels outrank lower ones |
| Reaction | Price moved away strongly after earlier touches |
| Break | Close beyond the zone, not just a wick |
| Volume | Higher on a real break, lighter on a weak bounce |
| Stop | Just beyond the zone, sized with [ATR](/atr/) so normal noise does not trigger it |

## Common mistakes

- Drawing too many levels until the chart is covered in lines and every price is "near" one.
- Treating a level as a single exact price instead of a zone.
- Buying support or selling resistance in a strong trend because "it has held before".
- Entering on a touch without waiting for any sign that the level is holding.
- Ignoring the time frame: a level on a five-minute chart can vanish on the daily.
- Moving levels after the fact so they appear to fit what price did.

## Pairs well with

- **[RSI](/rsi/)**: an oversold reading at support, or a divergence at resistance, adds momentum evidence to the level.
- **[Volume](/volume/)**: a break on heavy volume is more believable than one on thin volume.
- **[Role reversal](/role-reversal/)**: explains why a broken level often flips to the opposite job.
- **[ATR](/atr/)**: sets a stop distance based on how far the market normally moves.

## FAQ

**What is the difference between support and resistance?**
Support is a level below price where falling prices have tended to stop or bounce. Resistance is a level above price where rising prices have tended to stall or turn back. The same level can switch roles once price breaks through it.

**How many touches make a valid level?**
Two touches are the usual minimum, and three or more make a level more convincing. There is no fixed number. What matters more is the size of the earlier reactions and whether the level appears on a higher time frame.

**Does support and resistance always hold?**
No. Levels fail often, especially in strong trends or when a news event arrives. Treat a level as a place where price may react, and always decide in advance where the idea is proven wrong.

**Should I draw levels from wicks or closes?**
Both are used. Wicks show where price was rejected, closes show where traders accepted the price. Many people draw a zone that covers the cluster of both. Pick one method and apply it the same way every time.

**Which time frame is best for finding levels?**
Higher time frames, such as daily and weekly, give levels that more traders watch. Many people mark levels there first, then drop to a lower time frame to time entries around them.

**How is support and resistance different from supply and demand zones?**
Support and resistance are usually thin lines drawn through repeated turns. [Supply and demand zones](/supply-and-demand-zones/) are wider areas drawn around where a sharp move began. See [support and resistance vs supply and demand](/compare/support-and-resistance-vs-supply-and-demand/).
