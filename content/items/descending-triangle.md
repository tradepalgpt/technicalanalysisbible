A descending triangle is a chart pattern with a flat lower line and a falling upper line. Price keeps hitting the same support while making lower highs. It is usually read as a bearish continuation pattern, and a close below the flat line is the usual signal.

## What is a descending triangle pattern?

A descending triangle shows sellers getting more aggressive while buyers defend one price. Price falls to a support level and bounces. Sellers then step in at a lower high than before. Price falls to the same support again and bounces to another, lower high. The highs fall while the lows stay level, so the range narrows into a triangle with a flat base and a falling top.

The reading is that buyers are defending a fixed price, but sellers are willing to accept less each time. Eventually the buyers at that level are used up, and price breaks through.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, describe the descending triangle as a bearish formation and the mirror of the ascending triangle. It most often forms as a pause in a downtrend, though it can appear after an advance.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts:

1. **A flat lower line** touching at least two lows at about the same price.
2. **A falling upper line** touching at least two lower highs.
3. **Converging lines.** The range narrows as the lines approach each other. Edwards and Magee describe a minimum of four reversal points, two on each line.
4. **A breakout:** a close below the flat lower line.

Volume tends to fall as the triangle forms, as the market waits for a decision.

**Measured target.** Measure the height of the triangle at its widest point, which is at the start, between the first high and the first low. Subtract that height from the breakout level.

**Worked example.** The flat base is at 50 and the first high is at 60: a height of 10. If price closes below 50 at 49, the measured target is 49 - 10 = **39**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**Breakout.** A close below the flat line completes the pattern. Because the lower line is horizontal, it is a clear level to watch. Many traders wait for a close rather than a brief wick below it.

**Volume.** Volume usually dries up inside the triangle. Edwards and Magee note that a downside break does not always need a surge in volume to be valid, though a rise adds weight. A break on weak volume should still be treated with care.

**Timing.** Murphy notes that breakouts from triangles usually occur before price runs all the way to the apex, where the lines meet. A break very close to the apex tends to carry less force.

**Retest.** Price sometimes rallies back to the broken line, which may now act as resistance (see [Breakout Retest](/breakout-retest/)). Some traders enter on that retest.

**Failed breakdown and upside breaks.** The pattern is only a bias. Price can break up through the falling line instead, and that is a signal in its own right. A breakdown that closes back inside the triangle is a failed breakdown.

## Best market states

**Downtrends (best).** The pattern is most often treated as a pause in an existing decline, with the prior trend resuming on the break.

**Breakdowns from consolidation.** The flat base gives a clear trigger level, which suits breakout traders.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, a flat floor with slightly lower highs is easy to see by accident. Without a prior trend and some narrowing, it is weak evidence.

**After long declines.** If price has already fallen a long way, a triangle near the bottom can turn out to be accumulation. Context matters.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Touches | At least two on the flat base and two on the falling line |
| Flat base | Lows at about the same price |
| Lower highs | Each high clearly below the one before |
| Volume | Fading inside the triangle, rising when the break is strong |
| Break | Close below the flat line, not just a wick |
| Stop | Above the last lower high (conservative: above the triangle's top) |

## Common mistakes

- Selling inside the triangle on the assumption that it must break down. It can break either way.
- Drawing a "flat" base through lows that are in fact spread over a wide price range.
- Trading a breakdown that happens very near the apex, where momentum is often weaker.
- Ignoring the prior trend. A descending triangle after a long fall may be a base.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Having no plan if price closes back inside the triangle.

## FAQ

**Is a descending triangle bullish or bearish?**
It is usually read as bearish, because sellers push the highs lower while buyers hold a flat floor. But it is not certain. Price can break upward through the falling line, so wait for the actual break.

**How do you calculate the descending triangle target?**
Measure the triangle's height at its widest point, then subtract that from the breakdown price. Treat the result as a guide only. Many patterns fall short of the target.

**Where does the stop go on a descending triangle?**
Many traders place it above the most recent lower high. A more conservative stop goes above the top of the triangle. The tighter the stop, the more likely it is hit by noise.

**Does a descending triangle always break down?**
No. It is a bias, not a rule. Some break upward, especially when they form after a long decline. The pattern only gives a signal once price closes outside one of the lines.

**What is the difference between a descending triangle and a falling wedge?**
A descending triangle has a flat base and a falling top. A falling wedge slopes downward on both lines and is generally read as bullish, so the two are not interchangeable.
