A measured move is a way to set a price target by assuming the next move will be about the same size as an earlier one. In a trend, the second leg after a pause is projected equal to the first. After a pattern, the target is the pattern's height added to or subtracted from the breakout point. It is a rough guide, not a promise.

## What is a measured move?

Markets tend to move in legs: a push, a pause, then another push. The measured-move idea says the second push is often similar in size to the first. If a stock rose 20 points, paused, and then broke higher, a target could be set 20 points above the breakout.

The same idea works with chart patterns. A [Head and shoulders](/head-and-shoulders/) pattern, a [rectangle](/rectangle-pattern/) or a [flag](/bull-flag/) each has a height. The usual target is that height, projected from the point where price breaks out. Edwards and Magee describe these "measuring implications" for many patterns in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*.

It is not a law. Price may fall short, hit the target exactly or run far beyond it. The method gives a reference point to judge whether a trade offers enough reward for its risk. It is closely related to the [AB=CD pattern](/abcd-pattern/) and to the 100% level of [Fibonacci extension](/fibonacci-extension/).

## How it is drawn

**Leg-based version.**
1. Mark the start of the first leg (A) and its end (B).
2. Mark where the pullback or pause ends (C).
3. Project the same distance from C. In an uptrend, target = C + (B - A). In a downtrend, target = C - (A - B).

**Pattern version.**
1. Measure the pattern's height, from its highest to its lowest point.
2. Project that distance from the breakout level in the breakout direction.

**Worked example (leg-based).** A rise from A = 100 to B = 120 is a 20-point leg. Price pulls back to C = 110 and then rises again.
Target = 110 + 20 = **130**.

**Worked example (pattern).** A rectangle runs between 50 and 60, so its height is 10. Price breaks above 60. Target = 60 + 10 = **70**.

## How to read it

**Target zone.** Price often reacts in the area of the target, even when it does not stop exactly there. Treat it as a zone.

**Partial exits.** Some traders take part of a position off near the target and let the rest run with a trailing stop.

**Reward versus risk.** If the measured target is not clearly bigger than the distance to the stop, the trade offers little reward for its risk, and many traders skip it.

**Confluence.** A target that matches a prior high or low or a Fibonacci level carries more weight. See [Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).

**Failure.** If price breaks back through the breakout level before reaching the target, the move has failed and the target is no longer valid.

## Best market states

**Breakouts (best).** After a breakout from a pattern, the pattern height gives a clear and objective first target.

**Trends (good).** In orderly trends with legs of similar size, the leg-based version often gives a sensible reference.

**Ranges (weak).** Without a breakout there is no move to project. The range height only becomes useful once price leaves it.

**Volatility.** In choppy or news-driven markets, legs vary in size and the method is less reliable.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Clear first leg | An obvious, impulsive move, not a slow drift |
| Pause | A reasonable consolidation or pullback before the second leg |
| Breakout | A close beyond the pattern or the prior swing |
| Room | No major resistance between the entry and the target |
| Stop | Beyond the pause low or the pattern, to define risk |
| Update | Re-check if price breaks back through the breakout level |

## Common mistakes

- Treating the target as certain. Many moves fall short and some go further.
- Measuring from a vague first leg.
- Ignoring strong resistance that sits between entry and target.
- Moving the stop away because the target is "still valid".
- Measuring from the wrong point, such as the pattern edge instead of the breakout.
- Using measured moves in markets with no clear legs.

## FAQ

**What is a measured move in trading?**
It is a target set by assuming the next move will be about as large as an earlier one. The earlier move may be a trend leg or the height of a chart pattern, projected from the breakout or pullback end.

**How do you calculate a measured move?**
Measure the first leg or the pattern height. Then add it to the breakout or pullback point in an uptrend, or subtract it in a downtrend. For a 20-point leg ending a pullback at 110, the target is 130.

**How accurate are measured moves?**
They are rough guides. Price may fall short of the target, hit it or overshoot it. Treat the target as a zone and judge the trade by whether the reward justifies the risk.

**Is a measured move the same as a Fibonacci extension?**
The 100% extension equals a measured move. Fibonacci extension also offers other levels, such as 127.2% and 161.8%, while a measured move uses only equal legs.

**Does it work in both directions?**
Yes. In a downtrend, project the first leg downward from the end of the bounce. Chart patterns that break down use the pattern height subtracted from the breakdown level.
