A falling wedge is a chart pattern where price makes lower highs and lower lows, but the two trendlines slope downward and squeeze toward each other. It shows selling pressure fading. It usually ends with a break above the upper line, so it is most often read as bullish, whether it forms after a fall or inside an uptrend.

## What is a falling wedge?

A falling wedge is built from two falling lines that converge. The upper line joins the lower highs. The lower line joins the lower lows. Because the upper line falls faster than the lower one, the range narrows over time.

The meaning is in the squeeze. Each new low pushes price down a little less than the one before. Sellers are still in charge, but with less force. When the upper line breaks, the narrowing ends.

The pattern can appear in two places. After a long fall, it can mark a bottom (a reversal). Inside an uptrend, it can be a pullback that fades (a continuation of the rise). Either way, the break is usually upward.

No one person is credited with the pattern. It appears in the classic chart-pattern texts and in Thomas Bulkowski's pattern studies.

## How it is drawn

1. **Find two swing highs and two swing lows** that each step lower.
2. **Draw the upper line** through the highs and the **lower line** through the lows.
3. **Check the lines converge.** Both slope down, and the upper line is steeper.
4. **Look for at least five touches** in total, though some traders accept fewer.
5. **Wait for the break:** a close above the upper line.

**Measured target.** A common rule is that price returns to where the wedge began, the high at its start.

**Worked example.** The wedge starts at a high of 120. It falls to a trough of 100 before the upper line breaks at 103. The measured target is 120, a rise of 17 from the break. A stop below the last swing low at 100 puts the risk at 3. The target sits at about 5.7R, where 1R is the initial risk including spread. Price often falls short of the full target, so many traders plan partial exits.

## How to read it

**The break.** A close above the upper line completes the pattern. A wick through it is less reliable.

**Retest.** Price often pulls back to the broken line, which can now act as support (see [Role reversal](/role-reversal/)). Some traders enter on this retest rather than on the break.

**Volume.** In classic descriptions, volume fades as the wedge narrows and rises on the break. Heavy volume on the way down without a rebound is a warning.

**Divergence.** A momentum indicator that makes higher lows while price makes lower lows adds weight. See [Divergence](/divergence/).

**Failure.** If price breaks down through the lower line instead, the bullish idea is wrong. Plan for that before entering.

## Best market states

**Reversals after a decline (best).** The pattern is most meaningful at the end of a clear fall, where easing selling matters.

**Uptrends (good).** A falling wedge as a pullback inside a rising market often resolves in the direction of the larger trend.

**Strong downtrends (caution).** In a powerful decline, a falling wedge can resolve downward. The larger trend can overpower the pattern.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways action produces many converging lines that mean little.

**Volatility.** Choppy markets produce loose lines and frequent false breaks, so the lines need more tolerance.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Direction | Both lines slope down, the upper one more steeply |
| Touches | At least two on each line |
| Narrowing | Each swing falls less than the one before |
| Break | Close above the upper line, not just a wick |
| Volume | Fading inside the wedge, rising on the break |
| Stop | Below the last swing low (conservative: below the wedge low) |

## Common mistakes

- Buying inside the wedge before the upper line breaks.
- Forcing a wedge onto a plain downtrend where the lines do not converge.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Ignoring the larger trend. In a strong decline, a downward break is common.
- Moving the trendlines after the fact so the break appears cleaner.
- Using a stop so tight that normal noise at the bottom of the wedge hits it.

## FAQ

**Is a falling wedge bullish or bearish?**
It is usually read as bullish, even though price is falling inside it. The narrowing shows each decline losing force. A break above the upper line is the normal signal. A downward break does happen, so wait for the close.

**How do you trade a falling wedge?**
Many traders wait for a close above the upper line, then enter on the break or on a retest, with a stop below the last swing low. Set the rules before the trade, and test the setup on past data.

**What is the target for a falling wedge?**
A common guide is a return to where the wedge started. Measure from the break up to that high. It is a guide, not a promise, and many patterns fall short, so partial exits are common.

**How is a falling wedge different from a descending triangle?**
A descending triangle has a flat bottom and a falling top, and usually breaks down. A falling wedge has both lines sloping down and usually breaks up. See [Descending triangle](/descending-triangle/).

**Can a falling wedge break downward?**
Yes. In a strong downtrend, price can push through the lower line. That is why a stop below the wedge, and a plan for the failure, matter.
