The Fear and Greed Index combines several market measures into one score from 0 to 100. Low scores mean investors are fearful, high scores mean they are greedy. Traders use it as a sentiment filter, often reading extremes as a sign that the crowd may be leaning too far in one direction.

## What is the Fear and Greed Index?

Markets are driven partly by emotion. When people are scared, they sell at lower prices. When they are greedy, they buy at higher prices. A sentiment index tries to measure that mood in a single number.

Several versions exist. The best known is the CNN Fear and Greed Index for US stocks, which blends seven inputs, including market momentum, stock price strength, breadth, put and call options, junk bond demand, market volatility and safe-haven demand. A separate crypto version exists, with its own set of inputs, such as volatility, momentum and volume, social media activity and Bitcoin dominance. The exact inputs, weights and start dates can change, so check the publisher's current description.

The idea behind it is contrarian, in the spirit of the saying often linked to Warren Buffett: be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful. That is a famous quote, but it is a saying, not evidence that the index predicts returns. Whether the score gives a repeatable edge is not established, and published research on sentiment measures is mixed.

## Formula

Because versions differ, there is no single formula. The common pattern for the CNN version:

1. Each input is measured against its own recent history (for example, volatility versus its 50-day average).
2. Each is converted to a score from 0 to 100.
3. The scores are averaged with equal weights to give the final index.

**Worked example (illustrative only).** Suppose seven inputs score 20, 30, 25, 40, 35, 30 and 20.
Sum = 200; average = 200 / 7 = about **28.6**
That falls in the fear zone. If the market then rallies and the inputs average 80, the index would show extreme greed.

Typical labels are: 0 to 25 extreme fear, 25 to 45 fear, 45 to 55 neutral, 55 to 75 greed, 75 to 100 extreme greed. Exact boundaries vary by publisher.

## How to read it

**Extreme fear.** The crowd is nervous and selling. Some traders treat this as a time to look for buying opportunities, especially near a support level.

**Extreme greed.** The crowd is confident and buying. Some traders treat this as a time to be more careful, tighten stops or avoid chasing.

**Neutral.** No strong view. Little information.

**Change over time.** The direction of the score, such as a sharp move from fear to greed in a week, says something about mood shifts.

**As a filter, not a trigger.** Extremes can last weeks. Markets can rise on high greed readings for a long time, and fall on low ones. Use the score to adjust caution, not to time a trade.

**Combine with price.** An extreme reading near a major level, with a price reversal sign, is a stronger case than the score alone.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best, as context).** Extreme sentiment near major highs or lows is the classic use. It marks stretched conditions, not exact turning points.

**Strong trends (weak).** In a powerful rally, the score can stay in greed for months. Fading it would have meant standing in the way of the trend.

**Ranges (mixed).** Swings between fear and greed inside a range can match the range edges, but the signal is noisy.

**Volatility.** Because volatility is an input, spikes in the VIX push the score toward fear. See [VIX](/vix/).

## Settings

The index itself has no user settings. Choices you make:

| Choice | Effect |
|---|---|
| Version | CNN (US stocks) or crypto version, each with different inputs |
| Threshold | Many use 25 and 75 for extremes; others use 20 and 80 |
| Smoothing | A moving average of the score reduces day-to-day noise |
| Time frame | Daily is standard; weekly views show the broader mood |

## Common mistakes

- Treating extreme fear as a certain buy signal and extreme greed as a certain sell signal.
- Using the US stock version to judge other markets such as crypto or forex.
- Assuming the index leads price. It mostly reflects what price has already done, since several inputs come from price itself.
- Ignoring that inputs and weights can change over time.
- Trading it without price confirmation or a stop.
- Quoting success rates for it. Published figures vary by period and method, and no single result should be treated as typical.

## FAQ

**What does the Fear and Greed Index measure?**
It measures overall investor mood by combining several market indicators, such as volatility, momentum and demand for safe assets, into a score from 0 to 100. Low means fear, high means greed.

**Is the Fear and Greed Index a good buy signal?**
Extreme fear has sometimes come near market lows, but not reliably or on a schedule. It can stay low for weeks while prices fall further. Use it as background context, together with price and risk rules.

**How often is it updated?**
Most public versions update daily, and some more often. Check the publisher's page for the current schedule.

**Is the crypto Fear and Greed Index the same as the stock one?**
No. They are separate products with different inputs. The crypto version uses factors such as volatility, volume, social media and Bitcoin dominance. Do not compare scores between them.

**Can I trade only on the Fear and Greed Index?**
That is not advisable. It is a mood gauge with no timing precision. Most traders use it as a filter alongside price analysis, levels and a defined stop.
