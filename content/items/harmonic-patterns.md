Harmonic patterns are reversal shapes built from price swings whose lengths match set Fibonacci ratios. The usual layout has five points, X, A, B, C and D, and traders look for a turn at D. The family includes the Gartley, Bat, Butterfly, Crab, Shark and Cypher. It is a trader-education approach with no academic evidence base.

## What are harmonic patterns?

Harmonic patterns are a method of reading price as a chain of swings, then checking whether each swing is a specific fraction of the one before. If the ratios fit, the end point (D) is called the potential reversal zone.

The roots go back to H. M. Gartley's 1935 book *Profits in the Stock Market*, which described a pattern without Fibonacci ratios. In the 1990s Larry Pesavento added ratio rules, and Scott Carney later named and popularised several more shapes. No single person owns the method, and names and ratios differ between authors.

Harmonic trading is a trader-education concept. Published academic work does not show that Fibonacci ratios predict turning points better than chance, and the patterns are identified by eye or by software with fixed tolerances.

## How it is drawn

Every pattern follows the same recipe:

1. Pick a swing X to A. This is the reference leg.
2. Measure how far B retraces XA.
3. Measure how far C retraces AB.
4. Project where D should sit, using a retracement or extension of XA, and a ratio of BC.
5. Mark D as a zone. Place the stop beyond a defined point, often X.

**Common patterns and where D sits** (approximate, and authors vary):

| Pattern | B retraces XA | D (versus XA) |
|---|---|---|
| Gartley | about 0.618 | 0.786 retracement |
| Bat | 0.382 to 0.5 | 0.886 retracement |
| Butterfly | about 0.786 | 1.272 extension |
| Crab | 0.382 to 0.618 | 1.618 extension |

**Worked example.** A bullish setup has X at 100 and A at 140, so XA is 40. B pulls back about 62% to roughly 115. C bounces to 128. D is projected at the 78.6% retracement of XA: 140 - 31.4 = 108.6, call it 109. The stop goes just below X at 99. Entry near 109 risks about 10 points.

## How to read it

**Pattern complete at D.** Until price reaches D, the pattern is only a forecast. Traders watch for a reaction there.

**Reaction candle.** Most methods ask for a sign of reversal near D, such as a rejection candle or a momentum divergence.

**Invalidation.** A close beyond the stop point, often X, means the shape has failed.

**Targets.** Typical targets are the 38.2% and 61.8% retracements of the move from A to D. They are habits, not tested rules.

**Judgement.** Two traders can draw different patterns on one chart, and tolerance settings change what the software finds.

## Best market states

**Reversals at clear swings (best).** The method needs distinct, well-spaced swing points.

**Pullbacks in a trend (moderate).** Several patterns are really deep pullbacks inside a larger move.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, many sets of swings can be bent to fit.

**Breakouts and high volatility (weak).** Fast moves run through the projected zone.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Ratios | Each leg within the stated tolerance |
| Swing quality | Obvious, well-spaced swing points |
| Level | D matches older support or resistance |
| Reaction | Rejection candle or divergence near D |
| Stop | Beyond the invalidation point, usually X |
| Context | Larger trend not strongly against the trade |

## Common mistakes

- Treating Fibonacci ratios as proven. They have no academic evidence base.
- Widening tolerances until every chart shows a pattern.
- Entering at D with no reaction, hoping the zone alone will hold.
- Drawing the pattern after the fact. Real-time patterns look messier than textbook ones.
- Skipping a stop because the pattern "must" work.
- Ignoring the larger trend and context.

## FAQ

**What are harmonic patterns in trading?**
They are reversal shapes made of price swings whose lengths match Fibonacci ratios. Traders mark a possible turning zone at the last point, D, and look for a reaction there. The Gartley, Bat, Butterfly and Crab are the best known.

**Do harmonic patterns work?**
There is no academic evidence that they predict reversals better than chance. Results depend on how the swings are picked, the tolerance used and the risk control. Treat them as one way of marking an area to watch.

**What is the difference between a Gartley and a Bat?**
Both are five-point patterns. The Gartley ends at the 0.786 retracement of XA, while the Bat has a shallower B point and ends at the 0.886 retracement. The ratios define the pattern.

**Who invented harmonic patterns?**
No single person did. H. M. Gartley described the original shape in 1935, and later authors such as Larry Pesavento and Scott Carney added Fibonacci ratios and new patterns. Ratios differ between authors.

**Can software find harmonic patterns automatically?**
Yes, many charting platforms scan for them using fixed tolerances. The results depend on those settings, and software can show patterns that look convincing only in hindsight. Check how each tool defines its swings.
