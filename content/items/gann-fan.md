Gann angles, drawn together as a Gann fan, are a set of fixed-slope trendlines that start from a significant swing high or low. The main line, 1x1, rises one price unit for every one time unit. Traders use the lines as dynamic support and resistance. They are a trader-education concept with no academic evidence base.

## What are Gann angles?

William Delbert Gann was an American trader and author of the early 20th century. He taught a body of methods built on geometry, time cycles and price. Gann angles are among the best known and are described in several of his works, including *How to Make Profits in Commodities* (1942).

Each angle is a straight line that rises or falls at a fixed rate of price against time. A 1x1 angle moves one unit of price per one unit of time. A 2x1 moves two units of price per one of time, so it is steeper. A 1x2 moves one unit of price per two of time, so it is shallower. Gann treated the 1x1 line as the key dividing line between a strong and a weak trend.

The idea is that markets move in proportion between time and price. That claim is not backed by independent academic testing, so this guide presents the method neutrally, as a framework some traders use, not as a proven tool.

## How it is drawn

1. Pick a significant swing low (for an uptrend) or swing high (for a downtrend). The choice is subjective, which is a known weakness.
2. Decide the scale: how many price units equal one time unit (one bar). This is the step that changes everything, because a 1x1 line depends on it.
3. Draw the 1x1 line from the swing point at the chosen slope.
4. Draw the other angles from the same point: 2x1, 4x1, 8x1 above the 1x1 (steeper), and 1x2, 1x4, 1x8 below it (shallower).

**Worked example.** Suppose a low at 100 and a scale of 1 point per bar. After 10 bars, the 1x1 line sits at 110. The 2x1 line sits at 120 (two points per bar), the 1x2 line at 105 (half a point per bar) and the 1x4 line at 102.5. If price is at 112 after 10 bars, it is above the 1x1 line but below the 2x1.

Many platforms let you set a "price per bar" ratio, and often draw the fan automatically from two clicks. Platforms differ on how they scale the angles, particularly on log versus linear charts, so the same fan can look different on different software.

## How to read it

**Above the 1x1.** Price holding above the 1x1 line is read as a strong trend. In a downtrend, price below the line is read as strong selling.

**Break of the 1x1.** A close through the 1x1 is read as a sign the trend is weakening. Price is then expected to fall toward the next angle below, such as the 1x2.

**Angle to angle.** In Gann's scheme, price that falls through one line tends to move to the next. Traders watch each angle as a possible support or resistance level.

**Steeper angles.** Fast moves along the 2x1 or 4x1 lines are viewed as hard to sustain. A break back below them is expected.

**Time and price together.** Because the lines slope, the support or resistance level changes with each bar. This is the feature that distinguishes the fan from a horizontal level.

Treat these as hypotheses to test, not rules. Unlike many methods, the number of lines is high and the lines are close together, so price will often touch one of them. That makes the method easy to fit to history after the fact.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The fan needs a clear, sustained move with an obvious anchor point.

**Ranges (weak).** Sideways price repeatedly crosses the lines, and there is no meaningful angle to hold.

**Breakouts.** A strong breakout will often cross several lines in a few bars. The fan adds little over simpler levels.

**Reversals.** A turn is sometimes signalled by a break of the 1x1, but this happens with many other tools and arrives after the move has begun.

**Volatility.** In volatile markets the scale chosen for the lines matters even more, and a small change shifts every level.

## Settings

| Setting | Options | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor point | Major swing high or low | A different anchor gives a completely different fan |
| Scale | Price units per bar (for example 1 point or 1 cent) | Sets the slope of the 1x1; the most important choice |
| Angles shown | Commonly 1x8, 1x4, 1x3, 1x2, 1x1, 2x1, 3x1, 4x1, 8x1 | Fewer lines are easier to read |
| Chart scale | Linear or logarithmic | Angles are straight on one and curved in effect on the other, see [log scale](/log-scale/) |

There is no standard scale. Some traders pick a scale so the 1x1 line fits the visible chart at 45 degrees; others set it from the market's typical move per bar. Either way, the 45 degrees depends on how the chart is zoomed, which is the main criticism of the method.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing the scale to make the lines fit past price, then treating the fit as evidence.
- Anchoring at an arbitrary point and not at a clear swing.
- Reading the 45-degree line on screen as a true 1x1, when it changes with chart zoom.
- Using the fan in a sideways market.
- Treating every touch of any of the many lines as a signal.
- Taking the method as proven. It lacks an academic evidence base and should be tested on your own data.

## FAQ

**What is the Gann 1x1 angle?**
It is a line that rises one unit of price for every one unit of time. Gann regarded it as the dividing line between a strong and weak trend. Its slope on screen depends entirely on the price scale you choose.

**Do Gann angles work?**
There is no academic evidence base showing they predict price. Some traders find them useful as a visual guide. Because many lines are drawn close together, price often touches one by chance, so test any rule before relying on it.

**How do I draw a Gann fan?**
Choose a clear swing low or high, set the price-per-bar scale, and draw the 1x1 line from that point. Then add the steeper and shallower angles. Most platforms have a Gann fan tool that draws the lines after you click the anchor.

**What is the difference between a Gann fan and a Fibonacci fan?**
A Gann fan uses fixed time and price ratios such as 1x1 and 2x1. A [Fibonacci fan](/fibonacci-fan/) draws lines through Fibonacci levels of a price move. Both are fan-shaped sets of trendlines but they are derived differently.

**Does the scale of the chart matter?**
Yes, a great deal. Changing the price-per-bar scale or switching between linear and logarithmic axes changes the slope and position of every line. This is one of the main reasons the method is hard to test objectively.
