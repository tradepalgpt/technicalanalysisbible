The Bat pattern is a four-leg reversal shape drawn with five points, X, A, B, C and D. Each leg is measured against another using Fibonacci ratios. The bullish version ends at point D, a deep 88.6% pullback of the first leg. Traders look for a turn there. It is a trader-education concept with no academic evidence base.

## What is the Bat pattern?

The Bat is one of the harmonic patterns, a family of shapes where each price swing is a set Fibonacci ratio of another. The family traces back to H. M. Gartley's 1935 layout, which later authors gave Fibonacci ratios. Scott Carney introduced the Bat around 2001 as a variation of the [Gartley](/gartley-pattern/) with a shallower point B and a deeper point D.

The difference shows in the ratios. In a Gartley, B is near 0.618 of the first leg and D is near 0.786. In the Bat, B is a smaller retracement (0.382 to 0.50) and D is much deeper, at 0.886. The result is a pattern in which price retraces most of the first leg before the expected reversal at D.

Harmonic patterns are popular with some traders but have no academic evidence base. The ratios are usually applied with tolerance, and which swings count as X, A, B and C depends on the analyst, so results are not objective. See [harmonic patterns](/harmonic-patterns/) for the wider family.

## How it is drawn

A bullish Bat has five points. Bearish is the mirror image.

1. **X to A:** the first leg, a strong move up.
2. **A to B:** a pullback of 0.382 to 0.50 of XA. B stays above X.
3. **B to C:** a rally of 0.382 to 0.886 of AB. C stays below A.
4. **C to D:** a fall of 1.618 to 2.618 of BC. D lands at 0.886 of XA.
5. **D is the completion point.** It must stay above X.

The key rule is that D completes at 0.886 of XA, which also needs to match the BC extension of 1.618 to 2.618 of BC.

**Worked example.** X is at 100 and A at 120, so XA is 20 points. B retraces 0.50, so B = 120 - 10 = 110. D at 0.886 means D = 120 - (0.886 x 20) = 120 - 17.72 = **102.28**. Point C is anywhere between 0.382 and 0.886 of AB, for example 116. For C = 116, BC is 6, and the fall from C to D of 13.72 is about 2.29 times BC, which sits inside the 1.618 to 2.618 range.

If D breaks below X (100), the pattern is invalid.

## How to read it

**The completion zone.** The area around point D, called the potential reversal zone, is where traders look for a reaction. The pattern does not say price will turn there. It says a turn would be consistent with the shape.

**Trigger.** Many traders wait for price action at D, such as a rejection candle or a break of a minor trendline, before acting. Entering at D with no confirmation is risky because patterns can keep running.

**Stop.** A common placement is just beyond X, because a break of X invalidates the pattern. This can be a wide stop, so position size matters.

**Targets.** Typical targets are retracements of the CD leg, such as 0.382 and 0.618, or a return to point A. These are conventions, not tested outcomes.

**Tolerance.** Real charts never match ratios exactly. Different practitioners accept different tolerances, which is one reason the pattern is subjective.

## Best market states

**Reversal (best, with care).** The pattern is designed to mark the end of a pullback or rally.

**Strong trends (weak).** In a powerful trend, a deep retracement of 0.886 can be the start of a trend change, not a pause.

**Range (weak).** In sideways markets the swings are small, and any shape can be fitted to the ratios.

**Breakout.** The pattern is not built for breakouts. A decisive break through D is usually a sign it has failed.

**Volatility.** Fast, volatile markets can overshoot D and fall through X, which invalidates the pattern.

| Stop | Beyond X |

## Common mistakes

- Forcing the ratios onto swings that do not fit, and then trusting the result.
- Entering at D without any confirmation from price.
- Ignoring that the stop beyond X may be very wide.
- Treating the pattern as proven. Evidence for harmonic patterns is limited.
- Mixing up the Bat with the Gartley or Butterfly, which have different D ratios.
- Using different swing points each time to make a pattern appear.

## FAQ

**What is the Bat pattern in trading?**
It is a harmonic reversal shape with five points, X, A, B, C and D. Point B retraces 0.382 to 0.50 of the first leg, and point D completes at the 0.886 retracement. Traders look for a reversal near D.

**What are the Bat pattern ratios?**
B is 0.382 to 0.50 of XA. C is 0.382 to 0.886 of AB. The CD leg is 1.618 to 2.618 of BC. D is at 0.886 of XA. Ratios are usually applied with some tolerance.

**What is the difference between the Bat and the Gartley?**
Both are harmonic patterns. The Gartley has point B near 0.618 and D near 0.786 of XA. The Bat has a shallower B at 0.382 to 0.50 and a deeper D at 0.886 of XA.

**Where do you place the stop on a Bat pattern?**
Many traders place it just beyond point X, since a break of X means the pattern no longer holds. This can be a long distance from the entry at D, so position size is usually reduced to match.

**Is the Bat pattern reliable?**
There is no strong academic evidence that harmonic patterns predict reversals. They are subjective, since the choice of swing points varies. Test the approach on your own market and treat any pattern as one input among several.
