A cup and handle is a continuation chart pattern in which price rises, forms a rounded U-shaped dip (the cup), makes a small pullback below the cup's rim (the handle) and then breaks above the rim. It was popularised by William O'Neil as a base from which strong stocks can start a new advance.

## What is a cup and handle pattern?

A cup and handle is a pause inside an uptrend. Price first rises to a high. It then slips back in a gradual, rounded decline, bottoms out and climbs back up towards the old high. That rounded shape is the cup. Near the rim, price pulls back briefly and drifts lower in a short, quiet handle. When price clears the top of the handle, the pattern is complete.

The idea is that holders who bought at the old high get a chance to sell at break-even as price returns to the rim, and the handle shakes out the last of the impatient sellers. When that supply is absorbed, price has a clearer path upward.

O'Neil introduced the pattern in *How to Make Money in Stocks* (1988), where it is one of several "bases" he uses to find stocks. It is closely related to the [rounding bottom](/rounding-bottom/), but with the handle added. The pattern is used mostly on stocks, though traders look for it in other markets as well.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **A prior uptrend.** The cup is a pause in a rising market, not a bottom after a long fall.
2. **Left rim:** the high from which price begins to fall.
3. **Cup:** a rounded decline and recovery. A sharp V-shaped dip is weaker than a smooth U.
4. **Right rim:** price returns to about the level of the left rim.
5. **Handle:** a short, shallow pullback from the right rim, usually drifting down on lower volume. It should stay in the upper part of the cup.
6. **Breakout:** a close above the handle high (the rim area).

The cup typically takes weeks to months to form, and the handle a week or more. Exact depth and duration guides vary between sources, so treat them as rough guidelines.

**Measured target.** A common method measures the depth of the cup, from the rim down to the lowest point, and adds it to the breakout level. This is a convention used by many chart readers rather than a rule from O'Neil.

**Worked example.** The rim is at 50 and the cup bottoms at 40: a depth of 10. If price breaks above the handle high at 50 on a close, the measured target is 50 + 10 = **60**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/).

## How to read it

**The cup.** A smooth, rounded shape suggests supply being absorbed steadily. A sharp, V-shaped drop and spike back can mean the move is driven by news or panic rather than orderly accumulation.

**The handle.** The handle should be shallow compared with the cup and should not drop far into it. A deep handle that gives back much of the cup's gain weakens the pattern.

**Volume.** O'Neil looks for volume that dries up as the handle forms and then expands on the breakout. Without that rise, the breakout is less convincing.

**Breakout and retest.** After the break, price sometimes returns to the rim to test it from above (see [Breakout Retest](/breakout-retest/)). A hold there is read as confirmation. A close back inside the handle is a warning.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks out and falls back below the handle low, the pattern has failed, and the stop should have been hit.

## Best market states

**Uptrends and base breakouts (best).** The pattern is meant for stocks already in an advance that pause and rebuild before the next move.

**Strong general markets.** O'Neil stresses that breakouts do better when the broad market is healthy. In a falling market, even clean cups tend to fail more often. That is a guideline, not a measured figure here.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, cup shapes are just swings between support and resistance, and the handle has no meaning.

**Downtrends (weak).** A cup after a long fall is a bottoming shape rather than a continuation, and its handle may be a lower high.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | An advance before the left rim |
| Cup shape | Rounded, not a sharp V |
| Right rim | Back near the level of the left rim |
| Handle | Short and shallow, in the upper part of the cup |
| Volume | Drying up in the handle, rising on the breakout |
| Break | Close above the handle high |
| Stop | Below the handle low (conservative: below the cup's midpoint) |

## Common mistakes

- Buying inside the handle before the breakout and hoping.
- Accepting a cup that is too deep, too sharp or too short.
- Allowing a handle that drops into the lower half of the cup.
- Ignoring volume, which is central to how the pattern is taught.
- Forcing the pattern on charts with no prior uptrend.
- Treating the cup-depth target as certain.

## FAQ

**Is a cup and handle bullish?**
Yes. It is a bullish continuation pattern that forms inside an uptrend. A break above the handle high is read as a sign that the advance may resume.

**How long does a cup and handle take to form?**
The cup usually takes weeks to months, and the handle a week or more. Sources differ on exact limits, so use them as rough guides. Very short formations tend to look more like ordinary pullbacks.

**Where does the stop go on a cup and handle?**
Many traders place it just below the handle low. A more conservative approach puts it below the middle of the cup. A tighter stop risks less per trade but is easier to hit with ordinary noise.

**What is the target for a cup and handle?**
A common convention is to measure the cup's depth and add it to the breakout point. It is a rule of thumb used by chart readers, and the result is a guide only.

**What if there is no handle?**
Some cups break out without a handle. O'Neil describes the handle as a normal part of the pattern, but a rounded base that breaks out directly is still a valid base for many traders.
