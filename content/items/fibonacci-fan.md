The Fibonacci fan is a drawing tool that fans three sloping lines out from a swing low (or swing high) through the 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% levels of the move. Traders watch these sloping lines as possible support or resistance during a pullback in a trend. It is a niche tool with no formal test behind it.

## What is the Fibonacci fan?

Most Fibonacci tools draw flat lines at price levels. The fan draws sloping lines instead. The idea is that in a healthy trend, pullbacks tend to stop at rising (or falling) support rather than at one fixed price, so a sloping line may match the pace of the trend better than a flat one.

The ratios come from the Fibonacci sequence and are the same as those used in [Fibonacci retracement](/fibonacci-retracement/): 38.2%, 50% and 61.8%. The fan has a close relative in the [Gann fan](/gann-fan/), which uses fixed angles instead of Fibonacci ratios.

There is no known single inventor, and no academic evidence that these lines hold. Treat them as a way of organising a chart, not as a law of price.

## How it is drawn

1. Pick two points: the start of a move (a swing low in an uptrend) and the end of it (the swing high).
2. Draw an invisible trend line between the two points.
3. From the end point, draw an invisible vertical line straight down.
4. Mark the 38.2%, 50% and 61.8% points along that vertical line, measured from the end point back towards the start price.
5. Draw a line from the start point through each of those marks and extend it to the right.

**Worked example.** An uptrend runs from a low of 100 to a high of 150 (a rise of 50). The vertical line sits at the high. The marks are at:

- 38.2%: 150 - 0.382 x 50 = 130.9
- 50%: 150 - 0.5 x 50 = 125
- 61.8%: 150 - 0.618 x 50 = 119.1

The three fan lines run from 100 through 130.9, 125 and 119.1, and carry on upwards. A pullback that comes down onto the 50% line a few bars later meets that line at a price that is higher than 125, because the line is still rising.

Charting platforms draw this automatically once you click the two points. Some use only the three levels above; others offer 23.6% and 78.6% as extras.

## How to read it

**Support in an uptrend.** Price pulls back to a fan line and stalls or turns. The 38.2% line is the shallowest and shows a strong trend; the 61.8% line shows a deeper pullback.

**Resistance in a downtrend.** Draw the fan from a high down to a low. Rallies may stall at the sloping lines.

**Fan breaks.** When price closes below the 38.2% line, many traders expect the 50% line to come into play, then the 61.8% line. A close below the lowest line suggests the trend has changed pace or ended.

**Slope.** A steep fan means a fast move that is hard to sustain. A gentle fan describes a slower, steadier trend.

Because the lines slope, the price they sit at moves with time. A touch of the 50% line today is at a different price from a touch tomorrow, which makes alerts and orders a little harder to manage than with flat levels.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The fan is built for trends with orderly pullbacks. It needs a clear swing low and swing high to anchor on.

**Ranges (weak).** There is no trend slope to project, so the lines are arbitrary.

**Breakouts and volatility.** Sharp, gappy moves can blow straight through the lines. The tool adds little when price is jumping instead of drifting.

**Reversals.** A broken fan can be one of several clues that a trend is ending, but it is not a reversal signal on its own.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Anchor points | Obvious, widely seen swing low and swing high |
| Trend | Clear direction before the pullback begins |
| Reaction | A rejection candle or stall at the fan line |
| Confluence | A fan line near a flat Fibonacci level or an old support level |
| Break | A close beyond the line, not just a wick |
| Stop | Beyond the next fan line, or beyond the pullback swing |

## Common mistakes

- Choosing anchor points after the fact so the lines appear to fit. Different anchors give different fans.
- Expecting every touch of a line to produce a bounce.
- Using the fan in a range, where there is no trend to slope.
- Treating the three lines as equally important rather than looking for confluence with other levels.
- Forgetting the lines move with time and placing an order at yesterday's price.
- Ignoring the chart scale. On a logarithmic chart the lines look different from a linear chart.

## FAQ

**What is a Fibonacci fan used for?**
It marks sloping support or resistance lines during pullbacks in a trend. Traders watch for price to stall at the 38.2%, 50% or 61.8% lines. It is an alternative to flat retracement levels, not a prediction tool.

**Which levels does the Fibonacci fan use?**
The usual levels are 38.2%, 50% and 61.8%. Some platforms also draw 23.6% and 78.6%. Check your own charting package, as the defaults differ.

**How is a Fibonacci fan different from Fibonacci retracement?**
Retracement draws flat lines at price levels. The fan draws sloping lines from the start of the move. The price at which a fan line meets the market changes as time passes.

**Does the Fibonacci fan work?**
There is no formal evidence that price respects fan lines more often than chance. Many traders use it as one of several clues. Test it on your own market and time frame before relying on it.

**Can I use a Fibonacci fan in a downtrend?**
Yes. Draw it from the swing high to the swing low. The lines slope downwards from the high, and rallies may stall at them.
