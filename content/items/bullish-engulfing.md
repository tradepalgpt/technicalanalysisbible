A bullish engulfing pattern is two candles after a decline. The first is a small falling candle, and the second is a rising candle whose body fully covers the first one's body. It shows buyers took over from sellers in a single period, and it can mark the start of a bounce or a turn.

## What is a bullish engulfing pattern?

The pattern has two parts. On day one, sellers are still in charge and the candle closes lower. On day two, price opens lower still, perhaps continuing the fall, but then buyers push it all the way up to close above the previous day's open. The second candle's body "engulfs" the first.

Nison covers it in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*. The idea is a swing in control: a market that has been falling suddenly shows buyers with more force than sellers had a day earlier.

The pattern is one of the most widely recognised in candlestick charting. That also means it is widely over-read. Many examples appear that lead nowhere, so context matters a great deal.

## How it is identified

A bullish engulfing pattern has:

1. **A prior decline.** The pattern is a reversal pattern, so it needs something to reverse.
2. **A first candle with a falling body** (close below open), usually small.
3. **A second candle with a rising body** (close above open).
4. **The second body fully covers the first.** The second open is at or below the first close, and the second close is at or above the first open.

Strict versions compare bodies only and ignore wicks. Looser versions also look at the highs and lows. State which one you use.

**Worked example.** Day one opens at 52.0 and closes at 51.0 (falling, body of 1.0). Day two opens at 50.8, rallies and closes at 52.6.
- Day two open (50.8) is below day one close (51.0).
- Day two close (52.6) is above day one open (52.0).
- Day two body = 1.8, larger than day one's 1.0.

The second body covers the first, so this is a bullish engulfing pattern.

## How to read it

**Size of the engulfing candle.** A second candle much bigger than the first shows a decisive shift. One that only just covers a tiny first candle is a weaker signal.

**Location.** At a known support level, after a clear fall, the pattern carries more meaning. In the middle of a downtrend with no support nearby, it may just be a bounce.

**The close.** A close near the high of the second candle shows buyers held control through to the end of the period.

**The next candle.** Many traders wait to see whether the following candle holds above the pattern. A drop back below the second candle's open shows the shift failed.

**Stop reference.** Some traders place a protective stop just below the lower of the two lows, as a break under that level cancels the idea. That is a common method, not advice.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** After a long fall into support, the pattern can mark the point where sellers lose control.

**Ranges (weak).** At the bottom of a range it can be useful. Elsewhere in a range, engulfing candles appear often and are mostly noise.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong downtrend, a bullish engulfing candle is frequently a short bounce before the fall resumes.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A clear decline into the pattern |
| Shape | Second rising body fully covers the first falling body |
| Size | Second candle clearly larger than the first |
| Level | Support, a prior low or a trendline nearby |
| Volume | Higher volume on the second candle adds weight |
| Next candle | Holds above the pattern rather than falling back below it |

## Common mistakes

- Calling any big rising candle after a falling one an engulfing pattern without checking the bodies.
- Treating the pattern as a buy signal with no regard for trend or level.
- Using it in sideways markets, where it appears constantly.
- Mixing up body-only and full-range definitions and not noticing the difference between charts.
- Entering late, after the second candle has already made a large move, which leaves a wide stop.
- Expecting every example to work. Many engulfing patterns lead to nothing.

## FAQ

**Is a bullish engulfing pattern reliable?**
It is widely watched but not certain. Many examples fail, especially in sideways or strongly falling markets. It works best after a clear decline at a known support level, with confirmation. Test it on your own market before relying on it.

**Do the wicks need to be engulfed too?**
Nison defines it by the bodies, so the wicks do not have to be covered. Some traders use a stricter rule that requires the whole range to be covered. Choose one and apply it the same way each time.

**What is the difference between a bullish engulfing and a piercing line?**
A piercing line closes above the midpoint of the previous falling body but not above its open. A bullish engulfing closes above the whole previous body, so it is the stronger version. See [Bullish Engulfing vs Piercing Line](/compare/bullish-engulfing-vs-piercing-line/).

**Does the first candle have to be small?**
No, but a small first candle followed by a large second one makes the pattern more striking. If the first candle is large, the second needs to be larger still to cover it.

**What time frame works best?**
The shape means the same on any time frame. Daily and higher charts usually carry more weight because each candle summarises more trading. On very short time frames, engulfing candles appear often and are less meaningful.
