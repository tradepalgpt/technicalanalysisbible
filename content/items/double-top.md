A double top is a reversal chart pattern that forms at the end of an uptrend. Price rises to a high, pulls back, rallies to about the same high and fails again. A close below the low between the two peaks completes the pattern and suggests the uptrend may be over.

## What is a double top pattern?

A double top shows buyers failing twice at the same price area. In an uptrend, price reaches a high and pulls back. It then rallies again but stalls near the first high instead of breaking through. Because the same ceiling has held twice, some traders conclude that demand there has run out.

The pattern is shaped like the letter M. The low point between the peaks is the support level, sometimes called the neckline. Until price closes below it, the pattern is incomplete and price may still break to new highs.

Edwards and Magee, in *Technical Analysis of Stock Trends*, warn that true double tops are much rarer than casual chart reading suggests. Many two-peak shapes are only pauses in a continuing trend. That caution still applies.

## How it is drawn

A valid pattern needs these parts, in order:

1. **A prior uptrend.** Without one, there is nothing to reverse.
2. **First peak:** a high followed by a pullback.
3. **Trough:** the low of the pullback. This is the support level.
4. **Second peak:** a rally back to roughly the same high as the first, which then fails.
5. **Break:** a close below the trough.

The two peaks should be at similar prices. They do not need to match exactly, but a second peak well above the first is a breakout, and one well below it is a lower high, a different signal. Edwards and Magee also look for some time between the peaks. Peaks only a few bars apart suggest a minor blip rather than a pattern.

**Measured target.** Measure the vertical distance from the peaks down to the trough. Subtract that distance from the breakout point.

**Worked example.** Both peaks are at 60 and the trough between them is at 50: a height of 10. If price closes below the trough at 49, the measured target is 49 - 10 = **39**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. See [Measured Move](/measured-move/) for the general idea.

## How to read it

**Trough break.** A close below the trough completes the pattern. Until then, traders who sell at the second peak are guessing that it will fail.

**Retest.** Price often rallies back to the broken trough, which may now act as resistance (see [Role Reversal](/role-reversal/)). Some traders enter on that retest.

**Volume.** A common reading is that volume is lower on the second peak than on the first, which suggests weaker demand. Volume often rises when the trough breaks. This is a tendency, not a rule.

**Momentum divergence.** If an oscillator makes a lower high at the second peak while price matches the first high, that is a bearish [divergence](/divergence/) and adds weight to the idea.

**Failed pattern.** If price breaks the trough and then closes back above it, or breaks above both peaks, the pattern has failed. Failures can lead to fast moves in the other direction.

## Best market states

**Reversals at the end of a clear uptrend (best).** The pattern needs a trend to reverse. A mature uptrend that stalls at a known resistance level is the classic setting.

**Strong trends (caution).** In a powerful uptrend, a retest of the high is often a pause before the next leg up. The M shape alone does not tell you which it is.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, price keeps touching the same highs, so double tops appear constantly and mean little. A range top is just resistance doing its job.

**Volatility.** In volatile markets the peaks are rarely equal and the trough breaks are noisy. Allow for wider tolerances and wider stops.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear uptrend before the first peak |
| Peaks | At similar prices, with enough time between them |
| Break | Close below the trough, not just a wick |
| Volume | Lighter on the second peak, rising on the break |
| Momentum | Optional: lower oscillator high at the second peak |
| Stop | Above the second peak (conservative: above the higher of the two) |

## Common mistakes

- Selling at the second peak before the trough breaks. The pattern is not complete until the close below support.
- Calling any two highs a double top. Without a prior uptrend and a break, it is just two highs.
- Accepting peaks that are too far apart in price, or too close together in time.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Ignoring the higher-timeframe trend. A double top on a five-minute chart inside a strong daily uptrend is weak evidence.
- Having no plan if price closes back above the trough or breaks the peaks.

## FAQ

**Is a double top bullish or bearish?**
It is bearish. It forms at the end of an uptrend when price fails twice at the same level, and a close below the trough between the peaks signals a possible reversal downward.

**How do you calculate the double top target?**
Measure the height from the peaks to the trough, then subtract it from the point where price closes below the trough. This is a guide only, and many patterns fall short or overshoot.

**How is a double top different from a head and shoulders?**
A double top has two peaks at about the same height. A head and shoulders has three, with the middle one highest. Both are confirmed by a break of support. See the comparison page for more detail.

**Do the two peaks have to be exactly equal?**
No. Real peaks are rarely identical. A small difference is normal. If the second peak is clearly higher, price has broken out, and if it is clearly lower, the picture is closer to a lower high.

**Where does the stop usually go?**
Most traders put it above the second peak, where the idea would be proven wrong. A wider stop above the highest peak costs more per trade but gives price more room.
