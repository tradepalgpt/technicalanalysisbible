Separating lines is a two-candle continuation pattern. Two candles of opposite colour open at the same price, and the second moves in the direction of the main trend. In an uptrend a bearish candle is followed by a bullish one at the same open. It shows a brief counter-move that was cancelled out immediately.

## What are separating lines?

The pattern is two candles that start from the same place and then head in opposite directions. The first candle goes against the trend. The second opens at the same price as the first and then moves back with the trend, wiping out the counter-move. The two open prices form a line that "separates" the two candles.

The reading is that the market tried to turn and failed straight away. The trend resumed from the same price. Nison lists it with other continuation patterns.

It is related to the [kicker pattern](/kicker-pattern/) and the [counterattack lines](/counterattack-lines/), which also involve gaps or matching prices, but separating lines indicate continuation and not reversal.

## How it is identified

For the **bullish** version, in an uptrend:

1. **Candle 1:** a bearish candle.
2. **Candle 2:** a bullish candle that opens at the same price as candle 1's open (or very close to it).
3. Candle 2 is usually long, often with little or no lower wick, and it closes well above that open.

The **bearish** version is the mirror image in a downtrend: a bullish candle, then a bearish candle opening at the same price.

Exact matches of two opens are rare. Traders accept an open that is very close, for example within a small fraction of the average range.

**Worked example.** In an uptrend, candle 1 opens at 80 and closes at 77, a bearish candle. Candle 2 opens at 80 and closes at 85, bullish. Both opened at 80, and the second candle recovered the whole drop and more. That is a bullish separating lines pattern.

## How to read it

The first candle suggests a possible turn. The second candle denies it by returning to the starting price and pushing past it. Traders read this as a sign the trend remains intact.

The pattern is more useful when it forms during a trend with some pullback depth, and less useful when it forms in a flat market, where opposite candles from the same open are everyday events.

## Best market states

**Established trends (best).** The pattern needs a clear prior trend, as it only describes a continuation.

**Pullbacks within trends (good).** It can mark the end of a brief counter-move.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, the pattern has no trend to continue.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear trend before candle 1 |
| Open match | Both candles open at or very near the same price |
| Candle 2 | A strong close in the trend direction |
| Follow-through | The next candle extends the move |
| Context | Not at a major opposing level |

## Common mistakes

- Using it in sideways markets.
- Demanding exact open matches and finding none, or accepting loose matches and finding many.
- Confusing it with a reversal pattern because the first candle goes against the trend.
- Acting before candle 2 closes.
- Treating a rare pattern as a dependable edge without testing it.

## FAQ

**Is separating lines bullish or bearish?**
It depends on the version. The bullish version is in an uptrend and expects a rise. The bearish version is in a downtrend and expects a fall. Both are continuation patterns.

**How is it different from counterattack lines?**
Counterattack lines have two opposite candles that close at the same price, and they point to a possible reversal. Separating lines have two candles that open at the same price, and they point to continuation.

**Do the opens have to match exactly?**
In theory yes, in practice not. Traders accept a very small difference. Choose a tolerance, such as a small fraction of the average range, and use it consistently.

**Is it a strong signal?**
Not on its own. It is a minor pattern. Most traders use it as extra evidence that a trend is intact.

**Does it appear often?**
Close matches are not common, so strict versions appear rarely. That also makes them hard to test.
