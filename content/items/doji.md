A doji is a candlestick where the open and close are the same or almost the same, leaving a tiny body or just a line. It shows that buyers and sellers ended the period evenly matched. A doji after a strong move or at a key level can be an early sign that the move is losing force.

## What is a doji?

Each candlestick shows four prices for a period: open, high, low and close. The body is the box between the open and close. A doji has almost no body because price finished where it started, even if it moved a lot in between.

That makes a doji a picture of indecision. During the period one side pushed price away, then the other side pushed it back. On its own, indecision means little. After a long run up or down, though, it can show that the side in control is no longer winning easily.

Doji come from Japanese candlestick charting and were brought to Western traders by Steve Nison in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*.

## How it is identified

A doji has:

1. **An open and close at or near the same price.** A common rule is a body no bigger than about 5 to 10% of the candle's full range (high to low).
2. **Upper and lower wicks**, whose lengths decide the type:
   - **Standard doji:** short, similar wicks on both sides
   - **Long-legged doji:** long wicks on both sides, showing a wide but undecided fight
   - **Dragonfly doji:** long lower wick, little or no upper wick; open and close near the high
   - **Gravestone doji:** long upper wick, little or no lower wick; open and close near the low
   - **Four-price doji:** open, high, low and close all equal (rare, usually very thin markets)

**Worked example.** A candle opens at 1.2500, rises to 1.2560, falls to 1.2440 and closes at 1.2503. Range = 0.0120. Body = 0.0003, which is 2.5% of the range, so it counts as a doji. With wicks of similar length on both sides, it is a long-legged doji.

## How to read it

**After a strong trend.** A doji after several strong candles in one direction suggests momentum is fading. It is a warning, not a reversal on its own.

**At support or resistance.** A doji right at a known level is more meaningful than one in open space, because it shows the level is holding price.

**The next candle matters.** Most traders wait for the following candle to confirm. A doji after a rally followed by a strong bearish candle is far more meaningful than the doji alone. This combination often forms an evening star or morning star.

**Dragonfly and gravestone.** A dragonfly at support shows sellers pushed down and were rejected. A gravestone at resistance shows buyers pushed up and were rejected.

## Best market states

**Reversal points (best).** At the end of an extended move, a doji can be the first sign of exhaustion.

**Edges of a range (good).** At range highs or lows, a doji shows the boundary holding.

**Middle of a range (weak).** Doji are common in quiet, sideways markets and mean almost nothing there.

**Strong trends (weak).** Trends often pause with a doji and then carry on. A single doji is not enough to bet against a strong trend.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Context | A clear move before it, or a key level |
| Next candle | A strong candle in the opposite direction to the prior move |
| Volume | Higher volume adds weight (where volume data is reliable) |
| Time frame | Daily and higher doji carry more meaning than very short time frames |

## Common mistakes

- Treating every doji as a reversal signal.
- Ignoring context: a doji in the middle of a sideways market is noise.
- Acting before the next candle confirms.
- Using a very strict "open equals close" rule and missing near-doji, or a very loose one and calling ordinary small candles doji.
- Reading doji on very short time frames, where they appear constantly.

## FAQ

**Is a doji bullish or bearish?**
Neither on its own. A doji shows indecision. Its meaning comes from where it appears: after a rally or at resistance it can warn of a fall; after a decline or at support it can warn of a rise. The next candle usually decides.

**How small does the body need to be for a doji?**
There is no single rule. Many traders count a body of about 5 to 10% of the candle's full range or less as a doji. Be consistent with whatever rule you choose.

**What is the difference between a doji and a spinning top?**
Both show indecision. A doji has almost no body. A spinning top has a small but visible body with wicks on both sides. See [Doji vs Spinning Top](/compare/doji-vs-spinning-top/).

**What does a dragonfly doji mean?**
It opens and closes near the high after sellers pushed price well lower during the period. At support or after a fall, it shows sellers were rejected and can hint at a turn up.

**Should I trade a doji on its own?**
It is risky. Most traders treat a doji as a warning and wait for the next candle and other evidence, such as a key level or momentum divergence, before acting.
