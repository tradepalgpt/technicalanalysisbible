A stick sandwich is a three-candle pattern. In the bullish form, two bearish candles close at the same price with a bullish candle between them. The matching closes suggest that price found support at that level twice. It is a minor reversal signal that needs confirmation.

## What is a stick sandwich?

The two outer candles are the "bread". The opposite-colour candle is the "filling". The bread candles close at the same price, which marks a level that price touched and held twice.

In the bullish version after a decline, the first bearish candle sets a close. A bullish candle follows. Then another bearish candle falls back and closes at the same price as the first. The market failed to push below that level a second time, which can suggest buyers are defending it.

The idea is a simple double test of a closing level. As with other minor candle patterns, it is a hint, not a proof. Bulkowski's candle studies cover this pattern, and results depend on how strictly the matching close is defined.

## How it is identified

For the **bullish** stick sandwich:

1. **Candle 1:** a bearish candle in a downtrend.
2. **Candle 2:** a bullish candle that trades above candle 1's close.
3. **Candle 3:** a bearish candle that closes at the same price as candle 1's close (or very close to it).

The **bearish** version is the mirror image after an advance: two bullish candles with equal closes around a bearish candle.

Exact matches are rare. Traders use a small tolerance, such as a small fraction of the average range.

**Worked example.** After a fall, candle 1 opens at 30 and closes at 26. Candle 2 opens at 26.5 and closes at 29, bullish. Candle 3 opens at 29.5 and closes at 26. Candles 1 and 3 share a close of 26, so it is a bullish stick sandwich. The level of 26 is treated as support.

## How to read it

The shared close is the key. If price falls back to the same close and does not break it, that level may be holding. A close below it later would cancel the idea.

Traders often wait for the next candle. A move above candle 2's high would support the signal. A close below the shared level would reject it.

## Best market states

**Reversal points after a move (best).** The pattern needs a prior move to reverse.

**At known support or resistance (good).** A level that already matters gives the matching close more meaning.

**Ranges and strong trends (weak).** In ranges it appears by chance. In strong trends, levels often fail.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A clear decline (or advance for the bearish form) |
| Matching close | Equal or nearly equal closes on candles 1 and 3 |
| Next candle | A move away from the level, in the reversal direction |
| Level | A known support or resistance zone nearby |
| Volume | Rising volume on the rebound, where available |

## Common mistakes

- Accepting closes that are clearly different.
- Treating the pattern as a major signal when it is a minor one.
- Ignoring whether a level already mattered.
- Entering before the next candle confirms.
- Reading a stick sandwich after a very small move, where nothing is being reversed.

## FAQ

**Why is it called a stick sandwich?**
Two same-colour candles, the "bread", close at the same price. An opposite-colour candle sits between them as the "filling". The shape looks like a sandwich on the chart.

**Is a stick sandwich bullish or bearish?**
Either. The bullish form comes after a decline and hints at support. The bearish form comes after a rise and hints at resistance.

**Do the closes have to be exactly equal?**
In theory yes. In practice traders allow a small tolerance. The tighter the rule, the rarer the pattern.

**Is it a reliable signal?**
It is a minor one. Treat it as supporting evidence, wait for confirmation and look at the surrounding levels.

**What time frame suits it?**
Daily and higher charts are the usual choice. Short charts produce many weak matches.
