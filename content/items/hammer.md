A hammer is a single candle with a small body near the top and a long lower wick, appearing after a fall. It shows sellers pushed price sharply lower during the period but buyers drove it back up before the close. After a decline, it can be an early sign that selling pressure is easing.

## At a glance

| | |
|---|---|
| Type | Single-candle pattern, bullish reversal |
| Best market states | The end of a decline, at support |
| Weak in | Ranges and without a prior fall |
| Best uses | Entry timing (after confirmation), confirmation |
| Rule | Lower wick about twice the body or more; little or no upper wick; follows a downtrend |
| Origin | Japanese candlestick charting; popularised in the West by Steve Nison, 1991 |
| Mirror pattern | Hanging man (same shape, after a rise) |

## What is a hammer?

The name comes from the shape: a head (the small body) on a long handle (the lower wick). Nison describes it in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*, and it appears in the standard texts by Morris and Bulkowski too.

The story the candle tells is simple. Sellers had control and pushed price well below the open. Then buyers stepped in and pulled price back to close near the top of the range. The sellers were rejected. After a long fall, that rejection can mark a pause or a turn.

The body can be hollow (close above open) or filled (close below open). Many traders prefer a close above the open, since it shows a stronger recovery, but the shape matters more than the colour.

## How it is identified

A hammer has:

1. **A prior decline.** Without a fall to reverse, the shape is not a hammer.
2. **A small real body** in the upper part of the candle's range.
3. **A long lower wick**, commonly at least twice the length of the body.
4. **Little or no upper wick.**

There is no single official ratio. "Twice the body" is the most widely quoted rule of thumb. Pick one rule and apply it the same way each time.

**Worked example.** After a fall, a daily candle opens at 50.0, drops to 46.0, then recovers to close at 49.8. High is 50.1.
- Body = 50.0 - 49.8 = 0.2
- Lower wick = 49.8 - 46.0 = 3.8 (19 times the body)
- Upper wick = 50.1 - 50.0 = 0.1
- Range = 4.1; the body sits at the very top of it.

It meets all four tests, so it is a hammer.

## How to read it

**The rejection.** The long lower wick marks a price zone where buyers overwhelmed sellers. The longer the wick compared with the body, the stronger that message.

**Location.** A hammer at a known support level, or at the lower edge of a recent range, carries more weight than one in open space.

**The next candle.** A hammer is a warning, not a confirmed turn. Many traders wait for the next candle to close above the hammer's body or high. A further drop that breaks below the hammer's low cancels the idea.

**The wick as a stop reference.** Traders often place a protective stop just below the hammer's low, because a move under it means the rejection failed. That is a method some use, not advice.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** After an extended decline, particularly one that has tested support, a hammer can show selling has run out of force.

**Ranges (weak).** In a sideways market, hammers appear often and mean little unless they sit at the range edge.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong downtrend, a hammer is often just a pause before the fall resumes. Nison's own point was that it needs confirmation.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A clear decline into the candle |
| Shape | Long lower wick, small body near the top, tiny upper wick |
| Next candle | A close above the hammer's body or high |
| Level | Support, a prior low or a trendline nearby |
| Volume | Higher volume on the hammer or the next candle adds weight |

## Common mistakes

- Calling the shape a hammer when it appears after a rise. That is a hanging man.
- Acting on the hammer alone without waiting for the next candle.
- Ignoring location. A hammer in the middle of a downtrend with no level nearby is weak.
- Using a very short time frame, where hammer-like candles appear constantly.
- Being too strict or too loose on wick length and then misreading ordinary candles.
- Expecting a large rally. A hammer signals a possible pause or turn, not a target.

## FAQ

**Is a hammer bullish?**
It is a bullish reversal signal, but only when it follows a decline. The long lower wick shows sellers were rejected. Because it is a single candle, most traders wait for a confirming candle before treating it as more than a warning.

**What is the difference between a hammer and a hanging man?**
They look the same. A hammer appears after a fall and is read as bullish. A hanging man appears after a rise and is read as a bearish warning. Context decides the name. See [Hammer vs Hanging Man](/compare/hammer-vs-hanging-man/).

**Does the colour of a hammer matter?**
Slightly. A hollow (rising) hammer closes above its open, which shows a stronger recovery. Nison treats the colour as less important than the shape and the confirmation that follows.

**How long should the lower wick be?**
A common rule is at least twice the body. Some traders ask for three times. There is no official standard, so choose a rule, apply it consistently and test it on the market you trade.

**What if the next candle falls below the hammer's low?**
Then the rejection has failed and the pattern is cancelled. Selling pressure has resumed, and the hammer should not be treated as a turning point.
