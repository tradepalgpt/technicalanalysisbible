Three pushes is a price action pattern where a trend makes three successive pushes to a new high (or low), each one a little weaker, before reversing. Traders read it as a sign of exhaustion. It is often called a wedge when the three pushes converge. It is a rule of thumb, not a tested edge.

## What is the three pushes pattern?

In a trend, price moves in waves: a push, a pullback, a push. Three pushes happens when a trend makes three pushes in a row to a new extreme, with the third push showing less strength. The pullbacks between them are shallow, and each new high is only slightly above the last.

The pattern is read as a sign of trend exhaustion. Each push needs fresh buyers (in an uptrend). By the third, the supply of new buyers is thinning out, so a reversal or a deeper pullback becomes likely. When the three pushes converge into a narrowing shape, the pattern resembles a [rising wedge](/rising-wedge/) or [falling wedge](/falling-wedge/). The Al Brooks school of price action popularised the term "wedge" for this idea.

The count of three is a convention. Some traders accept two pushes as an [ABC](/two-legged-pullback/) and need three to treat the move as exhausted. Similar ideas appear in Elliott wave theory (a fifth wave after three advancing waves) and in harmonic drive patterns, but those are separate methods. There is no formal evidence that three is a special number.

## How it is identified

1. A prior trend, usually a clear one.
2. Push 1: a swing high.
3. A pullback, then push 2: a new swing high above push 1.
4. A second pullback, then push 3: a new swing high above push 2, often by a small margin.
5. Signs of weakness on the third push, such as smaller bars, long upper wicks, or slower momentum.
6. A reversal signal: a strong bar the other way, or a break below the low of the last pullback.

**Worked example.** An uptrend makes highs at 100, 106 and 109. The pullback lows between them are 96 and 101. The third push gains only 3, against 6 for the second. Price then closes below 101, the last pullback low. A trader who enters short on that break might place the stop above 109 and look for a target near the start of the pattern, around 96. The distance from the entry (about 101) to the stop (109) is 8; to the target it is 5, so the reward is less than the risk. A trader would need to judge whether that is acceptable.

The same applies upside down for three pushes down to a low.

## How to read it

**Fading momentum.** Each push covering less ground, or with smaller bars, supports the exhaustion story.

**Divergence.** An oscillator such as RSI making lower highs while price makes higher highs adds weight. See [divergence](/divergence/).

**Climactic third push.** Sometimes the third push is a fast spike that overshoots, then reverses. This is an exhaustion move rather than a slow wedge.

**Outcome.** The usual expectation is a pullback of the whole pattern, with price returning to the start of the wedge or the pattern's lowest point. It may also be a pause before the trend resumes.

**Failure.** If price breaks to a new extreme beyond the third push and holds, the pattern has failed and a fourth push is under way.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** At the end of a long move, where the pattern can flag a turn.

**Trends (caution).** In a strong trend, three pushes may just be a trend that keeps going. Many strong trends have four, five or more pushes.

**Ranges (weak).** In a range, price pushes to the edge several times without any trend to exhaust.

**Volatility.** In choppy markets, three small pushes are easy to find after the fact and are not reliable.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior trend | A clear trend before push 1 |
| Pushes | Three clear swing highs or lows, each beyond the last |
| Weakness | Smaller range or momentum on push 3 |
| Location | Near resistance, a prior high or a target area |
| Trigger | A reversal bar or break of the last pullback low |
| Stop | Beyond the third push |

## Common mistakes

- Counting pushes after the fact and finding a "three" in every trend.
- Entering at the third push before any reversal sign.
- Ignoring the bigger trend. A pullback inside a strong trend often leads to a fourth push.
- Treating the count of three as a law.
- Using the pattern in a range with no trend to exhaust.
- Placing the stop inside the pattern, where normal noise will hit it.

## FAQ

**What are three pushes in trading?**
It is a pattern where a trend makes three successive pushes to a new high or low, each weaker than the last. Traders read it as a sign the trend is running out of steam.

**Is three pushes the same as a wedge?**
They overlap. A wedge usually means the three pushes converge into a narrowing shape. Some traders also call any three-push pattern a wedge, while others use it only for the converging version.

**Why three pushes and not two or four?**
Three is a convention. Two pushes can be a simple pullback; three is taken as showing exhaustion. There is no formal evidence three is special, and many trends make four or more pushes.

**Does the three pushes pattern work?**
It is a rule of thumb. Some traders find it helpful at the end of extended moves, but results depend on context, confirmation and risk control. Test it on your own market.

**How do you trade three pushes?**
Many traders wait for a reversal bar or a break of the last pullback, then place a stop beyond the third push. Entering at the third push before any sign of reversal is riskier.
