Overbought and oversold describe a market that has moved far and fast in one direction, according to an oscillator. Overbought means price has risen a lot and may be stretched. Oversold means it has fallen a lot. They are descriptions of momentum, not sell and buy signals, and they fail often in strong trends.

## What are overbought and oversold?

An oscillator turns recent price moves into a number. Zones near the top and bottom of its scale are labelled overbought and oversold. The idea is that markets cannot rise or fall at a fast pace for ever, so extreme readings suggest a pause or reversal may be near.

The labels are a little misleading. "Overbought" sounds like buyers have gone too far and price must fall. In reality it only says price has risen strongly over the lookback period. A market can stay overbought for weeks in a strong uptrend. For that reason many experienced traders prefer to read these zones as "strong momentum" in trends and "stretched" in ranges.

Nobody is credited with the concept. It is built into oscillators such as [RSI](/rsi/), the [Stochastic Oscillator](/stochastic-oscillator/), [Williams %R](/williams-r/) and the [CCI](/cci/), each with its own standard levels.

## How it is identified

1. **Pick an oscillator and its levels.** The standard levels are in the table above.
2. **Note when the line enters the upper or lower zone.** That is the overbought or oversold reading.
3. **Note when it leaves.** Many traders treat the exit from the zone, not the entry, as the signal.
4. **Check the market state.** Is price in a range or a trend?

**Worked example.** RSI(14) reads 74. That is above 70, so the market is overbought on RSI. Three bars later it falls to 68. A trader using "exit from the zone" notes a possible short-term pullback, but only if price is at resistance and in a range. The same drop in a strong uptrend usually means nothing.

Note: the levels are conventions, not laws. Different platforms and traders use 80/20 or 90/10 for RSI on short periods.

## How to read it

**In a range.** Overbought near resistance and oversold near support can mark good places to look for reversals. A reading that leaves the zone is a common trigger.

**In a trend.** An uptrend often keeps RSI between about 40 and 80. Readings above 70 show strength, not weakness. Pullbacks to the middle of the scale are the usual trend entries, not fades at the extremes.

**At a level.** An overbought reading at a clear resistance level is more useful than one in open space.

**With divergence.** If price makes a new high while the oscillator stays below its earlier peak, the overbought reading is more meaningful. See [Divergence](/divergence/).

**Taking profit.** Some traders in a long position take partial profit when an oscillator reaches overbought, then keep the rest running.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** Swings between support and resistance give the oscillator regular, repeated extremes that match the edges.

**Reversals (fair).** At a major level, an extreme reading plus divergence and a price signal can mark a turning point.

**Strong trends (weak).** Overbought can last for as long as the trend does. Selling because an indicator says "overbought" in a strong uptrend is a classic way to fight the market.

**Breakouts.** A breakout from a range often pushes the oscillator straight to an extreme. That is a sign of strength.

**Volatility.** Volatile markets reach extremes more often, so the zones carry less information.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Market state | Ranging, not a strong trend (an ADX below about 20 to 25 is a common rule of thumb) |
| Level | At known support or resistance |
| Exit from zone | Oscillator moves back out of the zone, not just into it |
| Price trigger | A reversal candle, a failed breakout or a break of a short-term trendline |
| Divergence | Bonus: momentum fails to confirm the latest price extreme |
| Stop | Beyond the recent swing high or low, based on price |

## Common mistakes

- Treating "overbought" as "sell" and "oversold" as "buy" in all conditions.
- Fading a strong trend because the oscillator is at an extreme.
- Using the same levels for every market and every period length.
- Expecting a reversal at an exact reading, such as 70.
- Ignoring the bigger time frame. Oversold on a five-minute chart can sit inside a daily downtrend.
- Stacking three oscillators that all say "overbought" and counting it as three separate signals. They use the same price data.

## FAQ

**Does overbought mean I should sell?**
No. Overbought means price has risen strongly over the lookback period. In a range it may mark a turn. In a strong uptrend it can last for weeks. Use it as an alert and look for a price signal.

**How long can a market stay overbought?**
As long as the move continues. In a strong trend an oscillator can sit in the overbought zone for many bars or weeks. The zone describes momentum, not a countdown to a reversal.

**What are the standard overbought and oversold levels?**
RSI uses 70 and 30, Stochastic uses 80 and 20, Williams %R uses -20 and -80, and CCI uses +100 and -100. They are conventions, and traders adjust them for the market and the period setting.

**Is oversold a good time to buy?**
Not by itself. Oversold says price has fallen a lot. In a downtrend it can keep falling. It is more useful in a range, at support and with a price trigger.

**Which indicator is best for overbought and oversold?**
No single one is best. RSI is the most popular and is smoother, while the Stochastic is faster and noisier. Test your choice on the market and time frame you trade.
