An inverse cup and handle is the upside-down version of the cup and handle. Price rounds over into a dome, falls back, then makes a small rally (the handle) that fails below the dome's high. A close below the handle's low is the usual signal that a further fall may follow.

## What is an inverse cup and handle?

The standard [cup and handle](/cup-and-handle/) is a bullish continuation pattern: a rounded bottom, then a small pullback before a break higher. William O'Neil popularised it for stocks in the late 1980s in *How to Make Money in Stocks*. The inverse cup and handle simply flips the idea upside down.

Price rallies in a rounded, dome-like arc, falls in a mirror arc back to roughly where it started, then makes a small rally, the handle. If the handle fails to reach the dome's high and price drops below its low, sellers are taking control. The shape is closely related to the [rounding top](/rounding-top/), with a handle added at the end.

The inverse version is less well documented than the standard one, and it is rarely discussed in the classic literature. Treat it as a mirror-image idea, not as a pattern with its own body of research.

## How it is identified

The parts, in order:

1. **Left side of the dome.** A gradual rise to a high.
2. **Right side of the dome.** A gradual fall back to roughly the starting level. The dome should look rounded, not like a sharp spike.
3. **Handle.** A short rally of a modest size, often sloping up or sideways, that stays below the dome's high.
4. **Break.** A close below the handle's low, or below the base level at the start of the dome.

**Measured target.** Measure the height of the dome from its high to the base level. Subtract that from the breakout point.

**Worked example.** The dome's high is 120 and its base is 100: a height of 20. The handle forms below 120 and breaks down at 99. The measured target is 99 - 20 = **79**.

The target is a guide, not a promise. Many patterns fall short, and some go further.

## How to read it

**Breakdown.** A close below the handle's low is the standard trigger. Some traders use a close below the base of the dome for a stronger signal.

**Handle depth.** A shallow handle that hugs the dome suggests weak buying. A handle that climbs almost to the dome's high is less convincing, and one that exceeds it means the pattern is dead.

**Volume.** Volume that rises as price falls on the right side of the dome, and again on the breakdown, supports the pattern.

**Retest.** Price often returns to the broken level, which may now act as resistance (see [role reversal](/role-reversal/)).

**Failure.** A close back above the handle's high means the idea has failed.

## Best market states

**Breakdowns after a rise (best).** The pattern is about a rally losing momentum and rolling over.

**Strong uptrends (caution).** A dome-and-handle in a powerful uptrend can resolve upward instead.

**Ranges (weak).** A round-looking move inside a range is often just noise.

**Volatility.** Choppy markets tend to produce ragged domes that do not match the shape.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Dome | Rounded, not a sharp spike or a series of jagged swings |
| Handle | Small, below the dome's high, sloping up or sideways |
| Break | Close below the handle's low, not just a wick |
| Volume | Rising on the break |
| Stop | Above the handle's high |

## Common mistakes

- Selling before the handle's low breaks. Until then, it is only a possible pattern.
- Calling any bump a dome. Genuine domes take time to form and look rounded.
- Ignoring the handle's size. A handle as large as the dome is not a handle.
- Treating the measured target as certain.
- Assuming it has the same research history as the standard cup and handle.
- Fighting a strong uptrend because a shape appears.

## FAQ

**What is an inverse cup and handle?**
It is the upside-down version of the cup and handle. A rounded dome forms, price falls back, then a small handle rally fails. A close below the handle's low signals a possible further fall.

**Is the inverse cup and handle bullish or bearish?**
It is bearish. The standard cup and handle is a bullish continuation pattern. The inverse flips it into a pattern that points to a fall.

**How is it different from a rounding top?**
A rounding top is just the dome. The inverse cup and handle adds a handle at the end, which gives a more specific trigger: a break of the handle's low.

**How do you set the target?**
Measure from the dome's high to its base, then subtract that distance from the breakout point. It is a guide only, and many patterns stop short.

**Is it as well documented as the standard cup and handle?**
No. The standard pattern has a clearer history and wider coverage. The inverse is better treated as a mirror-image idea than as a pattern with its own research.
