A shooting star is a single candle with a small body near the low and a long upper wick, appearing after a rise. It shows buyers pushed price sharply higher during the period but sellers drove it back down before the close. After an advance, it can warn that buying pressure is fading.

## What is a shooting star?

The shape resembles a star streaking upward, with a small body at the bottom and a long tail above. Nison describes it in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques* as a bearish reversal signal that appears at the top of an uptrend.

The story is a failed breakout. Buyers drove price well above the open, perhaps through a recent high. Then sellers took over and pushed price back down to close near the open. The buyers were rejected, and the whole move up was given back.

The candle is the same shape as an [Inverted Hammer](/inverted-hammer/). The difference is context: after a rise it is a shooting star, and after a fall it is an inverted hammer.

## How it is identified

A shooting star has:

1. **A prior advance.** The candle should follow a clear rise.
2. **A small real body** near the bottom of the candle's range.
3. **A long upper wick**, commonly at least twice the body.
4. **Little or no lower wick.**

Nison also notes that the body should ideally gap up from the previous candle's body, as a further sign of a failed push. In markets that trade nearly around the clock, such as forex, true gaps are rare, so many traders drop that requirement.

**Worked example.** After a rally, a candle opens at 120.0, rises to 126.0, then falls back to close at 120.4. Low is 119.9.
- Body = 120.4 - 120.0 = 0.4
- Upper wick = 126.0 - 120.4 = 5.6 (14 times the body)
- Lower wick = 120.0 - 119.9 = 0.1

It meets the tests, so it is a shooting star.

## How to read it

**The rejection.** The long upper wick marks a price zone where sellers overwhelmed buyers. The longer the wick compared with the body, the clearer the message.

**Location.** A shooting star at a known resistance level, or just under a recent high, carries more weight than one in open space. A failed breakout above a level that closes back below it can be a strong version.

**The next candle.** Treat the shooting star as a warning until a following candle confirms it, for example by closing below its body or low. If price instead closes above the star's high, the warning has failed.

**The wick as a stop reference.** Traders who act on the pattern often place a protective stop just above the star's high, because a move beyond it shows buyers have regained control. This is a common method, not advice.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** After a strong advance into resistance, a shooting star can show buyers have run out of steam.

**Ranges (weak).** At the top of a range it can help, but elsewhere in a range it appears often and means little.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong uptrend, a shooting star is often a brief pause. Without confirmation, it is easy to mistake a pause for a turn.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A clear advance into the candle |
| Shape | Long upper wick, small body near the low, tiny lower wick |
| Next candle | A close below the star's body or low |
| Level | Resistance, a prior high or a trendline nearby |
| Volume | Higher volume on the star or the next candle adds weight |

## Common mistakes

- Calling the shape a shooting star after a fall. That is an inverted hammer.
- Entering on the star alone without waiting for the next candle.
- Ignoring location. A shooting star in the middle of a run with no resistance nearby is weak.
- Requiring a gap in a market that rarely gaps, and so missing valid examples.
- Using very short time frames, where long-wicked candles appear constantly.
- Expecting a deep fall. The pattern signals possible exhaustion, not a target.

## FAQ

**Is a shooting star bearish?**
Yes, when it follows a rise. The long upper wick shows buyers pushed up and were rejected. It is a single-candle warning, so most traders wait for a confirming bearish candle before treating it as more than a clue.

**What is the difference between a shooting star and an inverted hammer?**
They have the same shape: small body low, long upper wick. After a rise it is a shooting star and read as bearish. After a fall it is an inverted hammer and read as a possible bullish turn.

**Does a shooting star need a gap?**
Nison describes the body as ideally gapping up from the previous body. In markets that run nearly continuously, gaps are rare, so many traders use the shape and the context without requiring a gap.

**Does the body colour matter?**
A filled body, where the close is below the open, is seen as slightly more bearish than a hollow one. The long upper wick and the confirmation that follows matter more than the colour.

**Where do traders put a stop with this pattern?**
Many place it just above the star's high, since a move past that point shows the rejection failed. That is a common method rather than a rule, and the right distance depends on your market and time frame.
