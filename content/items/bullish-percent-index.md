The Bullish Percent Index (BPI) is a breadth indicator. It shows the percentage of stocks in a group, such as an index or a sector, whose point-and-figure charts are on a buy signal. High readings mean most stocks look strong; low readings mean most look weak. Traders use it to judge market health and watch for turns at extremes.

## What is the Bullish Percent Index?

The BPI answers a simple question: of all the stocks in a group, how many are currently on a buy signal on their point-and-figure chart? If 60 of 100 stocks are, the BPI reads 60.

Point-and-figure charts ignore time and record only price moves of a set size. A stock is "on a buy signal" when its chart has broken above a previous column of Os with a column of Xs. The BPI is built by counting these across a whole group.

It is usually run for a broad market index, and also for individual sectors. Comparing the sector readings shows which parts of the market are healthy and which are not.

Data providers publish BPI readings for common indexes, so most traders read the figure rather than compute it.

## Formula

BPI = (number of stocks on a point-and-figure buy signal / total number of stocks) x 100

**Worked example.** An index has 100 stocks. Of these, 55 are on a point-and-figure buy signal.
BPI = 55 / 100 x 100 = **55**

If the next week 62 are on buy signals, the BPI rises to 62.

Point-and-figure charts need a box size and reversal amount. Providers differ in how they set these, so BPI values can differ between sources.

## How to read it

**Extreme highs.** Readings of about 70 or above mean most stocks are on buy signals. This shows broad strength, but also that few stocks are left to join the rise. Some traders treat it as a caution area.

**Extreme lows.** Readings of about 30 or below mean most stocks are on sell signals. Some traders see this as a sign that selling is broad and may be close to exhausted.

**Direction change.** A common method is to wait for the BPI to turn back from an extreme, for example falling from above 70 or rising from below 30, rather than acting on the level alone.

**Divergence.** If an index makes a new high but the BPI does not, fewer stocks are taking part in the rise. This is a warning of narrowing strength, not a timing tool.

**Sector comparison.** A sector with a high and rising BPI, set beside a sector with a low one, can help rank where to look.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** The BPI is mainly used to spot turns when many stocks are stretched in one direction.

**Trends (useful context).** In a long bull market the BPI can stay high for a long time, so a high reading alone is not a sell signal.

**Ranges (weak).** In sideways markets the BPI tends to hover in the middle and give little guidance.

**Volatility.** Sharp swings can move the BPI quickly through its levels, so read direction changes with care.

## Settings

| Setting | Choice | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Levels | 70 and 30 | Common overbought and oversold marks |
| Group | Broad index or one sector | Wider groups show market health; sector groups show leadership |
| Chart box size | Set by the data provider | Affects how many stocks are on buy signals |

Level choices are conventions rather than fixed rules. Some traders use other thresholds, and the right levels can differ between groups.

## Common mistakes

- Acting on a high reading alone. In strong markets the BPI can stay high for long stretches.
- Assuming a low reading means a bottom. A weak market can stay weak.
- Comparing BPI values from different providers without checking how the point-and-figure charts are built.
- Using a sector BPI built from very few stocks, where one stock changes the reading a lot.
- Ignoring direction. A turn from an extreme tells you more than the level.

## FAQ

**What does a Bullish Percent Index of 70 mean?**
It means about 70% of the stocks in the group are on a point-and-figure buy signal. That shows broad strength. Some traders also read it as a sign that few stocks are left to turn bullish, so they watch for a turn down from that area.

**Is the Bullish Percent Index a leading indicator?**
Partly. Extreme readings and divergences from price can warn that a market is narrowing before it turns. But the signals underneath are built from past prices, so it is best used as a filter, not a forecast.

**What is the difference between BPI and percent above moving average?**
Both count the share of stocks that look strong. The BPI uses point-and-figure buy signals, while percent above moving average counts stocks trading above a chosen moving average. The second is simpler to build on any charting platform.

**Can I use the BPI on a single sector?**
Yes. Many data providers publish sector BPIs. They help compare sectors, but a sector with few stocks can swing sharply on one change, so treat small groups with caution.

**Do I need point-and-figure charts to use it?**
Not to read the figure, since providers publish it. To understand what drives a reading, it helps to know the basics of point-and-figure charting.
