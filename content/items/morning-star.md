A morning star is a three-candle pattern at the end of a decline. A long falling candle is followed by a small candle that shows hesitation, then a long rising candle that closes well into the first candle's body. It suggests selling has run out and buyers are taking over.

## What is a morning star?

The name comes from the morning star (the planet Venus), which rises just before the sun. In the same way, this pattern is read as a sign that a new, brighter phase may be coming after a dark one.

Nison describes it in *Japanese Candlestick Charting Techniques*. It tells a three-act story:

1. **Sellers dominate.** A long falling candle continues the downtrend.
2. **Hesitation.** The next candle has a small body, showing neither side is winning. It is often a spinning top or a [Doji](/doji/).
3. **Buyers take over.** A long rising candle closes well up into the first candle's body, undoing much of the earlier fall.

Because it takes three candles to form, it is a slower signal than a single-candle pattern, but it often carries more weight.

## How it is identified

A morning star has:

1. **A prior decline.**
2. **Candle one:** a long falling body, in line with the trend.
3. **Candle two:** a small body (either colour) that sits below candle one's body. Nison says the star's body should gap below the first body. In markets that rarely gap, many traders accept a small body that simply stays low.
4. **Candle three:** a long rising body that closes beyond the midpoint of candle one's body.

**Worked example.** After a fall:
- Candle one opens at 100.0 and closes at 94.0 (falling, body of 6.0).
- Candle two opens at 93.8 and closes at 93.6 (tiny body, low of 93.0).
- Candle three opens at 93.9 and closes at 98.5 (rising, body of 4.6).

The midpoint of candle one's body is 97.0. Candle three closes at 98.5, above it, so this is a morning star.

## How to read it

**Candle two shows the pause.** The small body means the fall has stalled. If the star is a doji, the indecision is stronger. Some traders count that as a separate pattern, the morning doji star.

**Candle three shows the shift.** A long rising body that recovers much of the first candle's fall shows buyers have taken control. The deeper it closes into candle one, the stronger the reading.

**Location.** At a known support level, after a clear fall, the pattern means more. Three candles that happen to fit the shape mid-way through a downtrend are weaker.

**Stop reference.** Some traders place a protective stop just below the star's low, since a move below it means the turn failed. That is a common method, not advice.

## Best market states

**Reversals (best).** After a sustained decline, it is a well-known sign that sellers are tiring.

**Ranges (weak).** At the bottom of a range it can help. Elsewhere in a range, the shape appears by chance and is mostly noise.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong downtrend, a morning star can be a short pause before the fall continues.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Prior move | A clear decline into the pattern |
| Candle one | Long falling body |
| Candle two | Small body, ideally below the first body |
| Candle three | Long rising body closing beyond the midpoint of candle one |
| Level | Support, a prior low or a trendline nearby |
| Volume | Higher volume on candle three adds weight |

## Common mistakes

- Accepting a first or third candle that is not clearly long.
- Calling it a morning star in a sideways market with no decline to reverse.
- Insisting on a gap in markets that do not gap, and so missing real examples.
- Acting on the first two candles in anticipation. The pattern is not complete until candle three closes.
- Ignoring support and resistance. A morning star under heavy resistance may go nowhere.
- Confusing it with a bullish engulfing pattern, which uses only two candles.

## Pairs well with

- **[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/)**: support under the star gives the turn a reason to hold.
- **[RSI](/rsi/)**: an oversold reading or bullish divergence adds weight.
- **[Volume](/volume/)**: heavy volume on the third candle shows broad participation in the move up.
- **[Trendline](/trendline/)**: a break of a falling trendline alongside the pattern strengthens the case.

## FAQ

**Is a morning star bullish?**
Yes. It is a bullish reversal pattern that forms after a decline. It shows a long selling candle, a pause and then a strong buying candle. It is a clue, not a guarantee, so most traders look for support or other evidence too.

**Does the middle candle need to gap?**
Nison describes the star's body as gapping below the first body. In markets that trade nearly continuously, such as forex, gaps are rare, so many traders accept a small body that stays low without a true gap.

**Does the colour of the star matter?**
Not much. Nison notes that a rising star is slightly more bullish than a falling one, but the small body and the strong third candle matter far more.

**What is the difference between a morning star and an abandoned baby?**
An abandoned baby is a stricter version. Its middle candle is a doji with gaps on both sides, so its wicks do not touch the neighbouring candles. See [Morning Star vs Abandoned Baby](/compare/morning-star-vs-abandoned-baby/).

**How is it different from a bullish engulfing pattern?**
A bullish engulfing pattern uses two candles and relies on the second body covering the first. A morning star uses three and relies on a pause followed by a strong recovery. See [Bullish Engulfing](/bullish-engulfing/).
