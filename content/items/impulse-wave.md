An impulse wave is the five-part move that Elliott wave theory says makes up a trend. Waves 1, 3 and 5 move with the trend and waves 2 and 4 pull back against it. Traders use the count to look for entries at the end of wave 2 or 4, and to guess where a trend might be nearing its end.

## What is an impulse wave?

Elliott observed that trends tend to move in five steps. Three of them (1, 3, 5) push in the trend's direction. Two (2, 4) are pullbacks. That makes a "5-3" structure when the pullback phase (waves A, B, C) is added. The whole five-wave move is called an impulse, or a motive wave.

Ralph Nelson Elliott developed the idea in the 1930s and published *The Wave Principle* in 1938. A. J. Frost and Robert Prechter set out a clear rule set in *Elliott Wave Principle* (1978).

This is a trader-education concept. It has no academic evidence base, and analysts often disagree on the count for the same chart. Use it as a framework for describing trends, not as a forecasting method that works on its own.

## How it is identified

The standard impulse is labelled 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 in an uptrend:

1. **Wave 1:** the first advance. Often small and unclear at the time.
2. **Wave 2:** a pullback. It cannot go below the start of wave 1.
3. **Wave 3:** an advance that usually goes furthest. It cannot be the shortest of waves 1, 3 and 5.
4. **Wave 4:** a pullback. It cannot overlap the price range of wave 1 (diagonals are the exception).
5. **Wave 5:** the final advance, often with weaker momentum.

**Worked example.** Wave 1 rises from 100 to 110, a gain of 10. Wave 2 falls to 105, which holds above 100 (rule met). Wave 3 rises to 130, a gain of 25, which is the longest (rule met). Wave 4 falls to 120, above the 110 top of wave 1 (no overlap, rule met). Wave 5 rises to 135. Wave 3 (25) is longer than waves 1 (10) and 5 (15), so the count is valid. If wave 4 had fallen to 108, it would overlap wave 1 and the count would have to be revised.

## How to read it

**Wave 3 is usually strongest.** Many practitioners look for wave 3 as the largest and fastest wave. Waves 3 and 1 are commonly compared using [Fibonacci extension](/fibonacci-extension/): for example, wave 3 near 1.618 times wave 1.

**Wave 2 and 4 depth.** Wave 2 often retraces 50% to 62% of wave 1. Wave 4 is often shallower, near 38% of wave 3. These are guidelines, not rules.

**Divergence in wave 5.** Wave 5 often shows weaker momentum than wave 3, so a [divergence](/divergence/) on an oscillator is a common clue that the trend is ageing.

**Extensions.** One of waves 1, 3 or 5 is often stretched. Most often it is wave 3.

**Invalidation.** If a rule is broken, the count is wrong, which gives a clear stop level for entries.

## Best market states

**Trends (best).** The pattern describes trends.

**Ranges (weak).** In sideways markets, any five pushes can be labelled as 1-2-3-4-5, which means the label tells you nothing.

**Reversals.** The end of wave 5 is read as a possible turning point, but a fifth wave can extend for a long time, and the count is often corrected after the fact.

**Volatility.** Wave 3 often has the strongest moves and the widest bars.

## Confirmation rules

| Check | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Rule 1 | Wave 2 does not go beyond the start of wave 1 |
| Rule 2 | Wave 3 is not the shortest of waves 1, 3, 5 |
| Rule 3 | Wave 4 does not overlap wave 1 (not a diagonal) |
| Structure | Waves 1, 3, 5 are five-part; 2 and 4 are corrections |
| Momentum | Wave 3 strong; wave 5 often weaker |
| Stop | Beyond the invalidation level of the count |

## Common mistakes

- Labelling waves after the fact, then treating the count as predictive.
- Breaking a rule and keeping the count anyway.
- Calling every move up a wave 3.
- Counting on a timeframe that shows no clear trend.
- Using exact Fibonacci ratios as if they were fixed targets.
- Not having a second count in mind when the first fails.

## FAQ

**What are the three rules of an impulse wave?**
Wave 2 cannot retrace more than 100% of wave 1, wave 3 cannot be the shortest of the three motive waves, and wave 4 cannot overlap the price range of wave 1 (except in diagonals). If one is broken, the count is invalid.

**Which wave is strongest?**
Wave 3 is most often the longest and strongest. This is not certain, and sometimes wave 5 or wave 1 extends instead. Wave 3 simply cannot be the shortest of the three.

**How do I trade an impulse wave?**
Some traders enter near the end of wave 2 or wave 4, with a stop beyond the invalidation level. This is subjective and has no academic support, so test any rules you adopt on past data first.

**Is the Elliott wave theory proven?**
No. It is a trader-education framework without a solid academic evidence base. Counts are subjective, and two practitioners often label the same chart differently. Many traders still find it a useful way to describe trend structure.

**What happens after an impulse wave?**
Elliott theory says a corrective phase follows, labelled A, B, C. See [corrective waves](/corrective-waves/). How deep that correction goes is a guideline rather than a rule.
