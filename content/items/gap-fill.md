A gap fill is when price returns to trade through the empty space left by a gap, so the gap is "closed". The idea that gaps tend to get filled is popular, especially for small gaps in ranging markets. It is a tendency, not a law, and strong gaps often stay open for a long time.

## What is a gap fill?

A gap is empty space on the chart between one bar's range and the next. A gap fill occurs when price later trades back across that space. For an upward gap, the fill is complete when price drops back to the previous bar's closing level or high. For a downward gap, it is complete when price rises back to that level.

The belief that gaps tend to fill has deep roots in chart reading. It is helped by the fact that many gaps are small, driven by short-term order imbalances at the open and soon corrected as the day's trading continues. Larger gaps with real news or a change in trend behave differently and often stay open.

No single person is credited with the idea, and there is no precise standard definition of when a gap is "filled". Some traders require the full gap to be covered, others take a partial fill such as half. State the rule before testing.

Gap types matter. A [common gap](/common-gap/) inside a range tends to fill. A [breakaway gap](/breakaway-gap/) or [runaway gap](/runaway-gap/) is less likely to fill soon, because it reflects a lasting change in demand. An [exhaustion gap](/exhaustion-gap/) usually fills quickly, since it marks the end of the move.

## How it is identified

1. **Find a gap.** On a daily chart, the day's low is above the previous day's high (upward gap) or the day's high is below the previous low (downward gap). Some traders use the previous close and the open instead.
2. **Classify it.** Note whether it is in a range, at a breakout, mid-trend or after a long trend.
3. **Set the fill level.** Usually the previous close, or the far edge of the gap.
4. **Plan the trade.** Trade toward the fill with a stop, or use the fill level as a profit target for another trade.

**Worked example.** A stock in a range closes at 50 and opens the next day at 52 with no news. That is a 2-point gap up, with the fill level at 50.

A trader expecting a fill might sell short near 52 with a stop at 53.5 (risk 1.5) and a target at 50 (reward 2). If price returns to 50 the gap is filled and the trade makes about 1.3 times its risk. If price instead keeps going up, the stop is hit for a loss.

Note that this trade bets against the gap's momentum. If the gap was caused by news that changed the value of the stock, filling it is much less likely.

## How to read it

**Small gaps fill more easily.** A gap of a small fraction of the daily range tends to be covered during the same session or within a few days. Large gaps, particularly on heavy volume and news, do not.

**Context decides.** A gap in a range, back toward the middle of the range, often fills. A gap through a long-standing resistance on strong volume is a breakout and may not fill for a long time.

**As a target.** Even in a trend, price sometimes returns to fill an old gap on a pullback. Open gaps below the current price in an uptrend can act as potential support, and those above in a downtrend can act as resistance.

**Partial fills.** Price often covers part of the gap and then moves on. Half the gap is sometimes used as a target for that reason, but there is no standard.

**Be careful with statistics.** Claims that a certain percentage of gaps fill vary widely with the market, period and definition of "fill". Test it yourself on your market and time frame rather than relying on a quoted number.

## Best market states

**Ranges (best).** In a sideways market, price tends to move back toward the middle of the range, so a small gap at the edge has a reasonable chance of closing.

**Reversals (good).** Exhaustion gaps and island reversals involve a quick return through the gap, which is a fill.

**Trends (weak).** In a strong trend, gaps often act as signs of strength and stay open. Fading them can mean trading against the trend.

**Breakouts (weak).** Gaps out of a base are the ones least likely to fill soon.

**Volatility.** In volatile, news-driven markets, gaps can be large and persistent. Fill trades need wider stops.

| Target | Previous close or far edge, set before entry |
| Stop | Beyond the gap's extreme, sized for the risk |

## Common mistakes

- Treating every gap as certain to fill. Many large gaps stay open for months.
- Fading a breakaway gap that signals a new trend.
- Entering against the gap at the open without any sign that it is stalling.
- Using no stop because the gap "must" fill.
- Not defining what counts as a fill before testing or trading.
- Quoting a fill percentage from a book or website without checking it on your market.

## FAQ

**What does it mean when a gap is filled?**
It means price has traded back through the empty space that the gap left. For an upward gap, price falls back to the previous close or high. For a downward gap, it rises back to the previous close or low.

**Do all gaps get filled?**
No. Many small gaps fill, but large gaps that follow news or start a trend can stay open for a long time, sometimes permanently. How often gaps fill depends on the market, the period and your definition of filled.

**Is it a good idea to trade gap fills?**
It can work in ranges when a small gap has no clear reason behind it. It is risky against strong trends and news-driven gaps. Always use a stop and test the idea on your own market first.

**What is the difference between a gap fill and an exhaustion gap?**
A gap fill describes what happens to any gap when price returns through it. An [exhaustion gap](/exhaustion-gap/) is a specific late-trend gap that usually fills quickly and signals the trend is tiring.

**How is a gap fill related to a fair value gap?**
A [fair value gap](/fair-value-gap/) is a three-bar concept from some trading communities, where price often returns to the area. A gap fill refers to a true price gap closing. They are related ideas but not the same.
