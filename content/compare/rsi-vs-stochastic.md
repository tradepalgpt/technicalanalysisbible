RSI and the Stochastic Oscillator are both momentum oscillators on a 0 to 100 scale, but they measure different things. RSI compares the size of recent gains with recent losses. The Stochastic shows where the latest close sits within the recent high-low range. The Stochastic is faster and gives more signals; RSI is smoother and gives fewer.

## At a glance

| | RSI | Stochastic Oscillator |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Average gains vs average losses | Close relative to the high-low range |
| Created by | J. Welles Wilder Jr., 1978 | George Lane, late 1950s |
| Default settings | 14; levels 70 / 30 | 14, 3, 3; levels 80 / 20 |
| Speed | Smoother, slower | Faster, more signals |
| Uses highs and lows? | No, closes only | Yes |
| Best market state | Ranges, reversals | Ranges |
| Main weakness | Stays overbought in strong trends | Whipsaws more in choppy markets |

## When to use RSI

[RSI](/rsi/) suits traders who want fewer, steadier readings. Because it averages gains and losses with Wilder's smoothing, single wild bars move it less. Its 50 line works as a simple trend filter, and its divergences are among the most watched in technical analysis. It is a good default if you want one momentum gauge across many markets.

## When to use the Stochastic Oscillator

The [Stochastic Oscillator](/stochastic-oscillator/) suits traders who want earlier turns and more frequent signals, often on shorter time frames. Because it uses the high and low of the period, it reacts as soon as price closes near the edge of its recent range. That makes it popular for timing entries inside a clear range, where %K crossing %D near 20 or 80 can line up with the range edges.

## Using both together

Some traders use RSI to judge the bigger momentum picture and the Stochastic for timing. For example, they only take Stochastic buy crosses while RSI is above 50. The two are closely related, so agreement between them is weaker confirmation than agreement between a momentum tool and something different, such as [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) or [volume](/volume/).

## Common mix-ups

- **Same levels for both.** RSI usually uses 70 and 30; the Stochastic usually uses 80 and 20.
- **Treating overbought as a sell signal.** Both can stay high for long periods in a strong uptrend.
- **Comparing fast and slow Stochastic values.** Platforms label the two versions differently, so check which one is plotted.
- **Thinking two oscillators means two confirmations.** They measure related things and often agree for the same reason.

## FAQ

**Which is better, RSI or Stochastic?**
Neither is better in general. RSI is smoother and suits slower trading and trend filtering. The Stochastic is faster and suits timing entries in ranges. Pick the one whose speed fits your time frame, and test it on your market.

**Is the Stochastic more sensitive than RSI?**
Usually, yes. Because it reacts to where the close sits within the recent high-low range, it moves sharply when price closes near an extreme. RSI's averaging makes it calmer.

**Can I use RSI and Stochastic on the same chart?**
You can, and many traders do. Just remember they are both momentum oscillators, so a signal confirmed by both is not as strong as one confirmed by a different kind of evidence, such as a key price level.

**Do RSI and Stochastic use the same overbought levels?**
No. RSI is usually read at 70 and 30. The Stochastic is usually read at 80 and 20.
