On-Balance Volume and the Accumulation/Distribution Line are both running totals of volume used to confirm trends. They split each bar's volume in different ways. OBV gives all of it to one side, based on the close versus the previous close. The A/D Line gives a share, based on where the close sits inside the bar's range.

## At a glance

| | On-Balance Volume | Accumulation/Distribution Line |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Volume added or subtracted by close vs previous close | Volume weighted by close position in the bar's range |
| Created by | Joseph Granville, 1963 | Commonly attributed to Marc Chaikin, 1980s |
| Default settings | None; optional 20-period average | None; a running total |
| Treatment of volume | All or nothing | Partial share |
| Uses high and low? | No | Yes |
| Best market state | Trends, reversals | Trends, reversals |
| Main weakness | Crude; a one-cent rise counts as full buying | Weak in ranges and markets with frequent gaps |

## When to use On-Balance Volume

[On-Balance Volume](/on-balance-volume/) suits traders who want a simple, easy-to-read volume line. It only needs closes and volume, so it is quick to check and hard to misread. It is often used to see whether a breakout or trend has volume behind it, and to look for divergence from price.

## When to use the Accumulation/Distribution Line

The [Accumulation/Distribution Line](/accumulation-distribution/) suits traders who want volume judged by how the bar closed. A bar that closes near its high adds most of its volume; one that closes near its low subtracts most. This gives a finer picture inside wide-range bars. It is also the base for Chaikin Money Flow.

## Using both together

Some traders plot both and look for agreement. If price rises and both lines rise, volume supports the move. If OBV rises but the A/D Line falls, the bars may be closing weakly despite higher closes, which is worth a closer look at the chart. They share the same inputs, so agreement is not independent proof. Pair them with a price level such as [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).

## Common mix-ups

- **Reading the absolute number.** Neither line's value means anything alone. Only direction and divergence matter.
- **Assuming they always agree.** They often differ, because they split volume differently.
- **Using them on forex without care.** Spot forex has only [tick volume](/tick-volume/), so both are approximations.
- **Comparing levels.** The lines depend on where the calculation begins, so compare shapes, not levels.

## FAQ

**What is the main difference between OBV and the A/D Line?**
OBV counts a whole bar's volume as up or down from the close versus the previous close. The A/D Line counts a share of volume based on where the close sits within the bar's high-low range.

**Which one is better?**
Neither is better in general. OBV is simpler. The A/D Line uses more of each bar. Compare how each behaves on your market and time frame.

**Can they give opposite signals?**
Yes. A bar can close higher than the last close yet near its low. OBV counts it as full buying, while the A/D Line counts it as mostly selling.

**Do I need to set a period for either?**
No. Both are cumulative totals. OBV is sometimes shown with a 20-period moving average for smoothing.
