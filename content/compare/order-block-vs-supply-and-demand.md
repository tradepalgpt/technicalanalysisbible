Order blocks and supply and demand zones are close relatives. Both mark an area where price left sharply and where traders watch for a reaction on a return. The main difference is how the zone is drawn. An order block is usually a single candle, the last opposite-coloured one before a structure-breaking move. A supply or demand zone is usually a small base of several candles.

## At a glance

| | Order Block | Supply and Demand Zones |
|---|---|---|
| Drawn from | The last opposite candle before a strong move | The base (pause) before a sharp move |
| Needs a structure break? | Yes, the move should break a swing high or low | Not required; a sharp departure is enough |
| Size | Often narrow, one candle | Often wider, several candles |
| Origin | ICT and Smart Money Concepts teaching | Course teaching, often linked to Sam Seiden |
| Best market states | Reversals, trend pullbacks | Ranges, reversals |
| Weak in | Sideways ranges | Strong trends that run through zones |
| Evidence | No published research showing an edge | Same; mostly taught in courses |

## When to use Order Block

The [Order Block](/order-block/) suits traders who already read market structure and want a precise area. The structure-break rule filters out many candles, so zones are tied to real turning points. Because the zone is a single candle, the stop can be tighter, though a wide candle gives a wide stop. It fits pullback entries in a trend and reversals after a clear break.

## When to use Supply and Demand Zones

[Supply and Demand Zones](/supply-and-demand-zones/) suit traders who prefer areas to single candles. A base of several candles gives a box with a clear top and bottom, and the method works well at the edges of a range. Zones are drawn from the origin of a strong move, so they can be valid after one visit. Many traders prefer fresh zones that price has not returned to.

## Using both together

Many traders treat the two as cross-checks. If an order block sits inside a wider demand zone, the area has two reasons to matter. They also share the same failure rule: a close beyond the far side of the zone ends the idea. Both explanations about large institutional orders are theories, not things a chart can prove.

## Common mix-ups

- **Thinking they are identical.** They overlap, but one is a single candle after a structure break and the other is a base.
- **Using the terms loosely.** Many traders do, which makes online examples hard to compare.
- **Marking too many zones.** In a range, nearly every candle can be called either.
- **Trusting retested zones.** After repeated visits, both are often treated with less trust.
- **Taking the "smart money" story as fact.** It is an explanation, not evidence.

## FAQ

**Are order blocks the same as supply and demand zones?**
They are very similar but not the same. Both mark areas where price left sharply. An order block is usually one candle tied to a structure break, while a supply or demand zone is usually a base of several candles.

**Which is more reliable?**
No public research shows either beats plain support and resistance. Reliability depends on how you define and apply the rules. Test your version on your own market and time frame.

**Where does the stop go on each?**
Beyond the far side of the zone, since a close through it means the idea has failed. A wider zone means a wider stop, so position size should be smaller.

**Can a failed zone flip?**
Yes, in both ideas. A failed order block may be treated as a breaker block, and a failed demand zone may turn into supply.
