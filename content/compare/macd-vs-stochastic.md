MACD and the Stochastic Oscillator measure momentum in different ways. MACD tracks the gap between a fast and a slow moving average and has no fixed limits, so it reads trend and momentum shifts. The Stochastic shows where the latest close sits inside the recent high-low range, on a scale of 0 to 100, so it reads stretched moves.

## At a glance

| | MACD | Stochastic Oscillator |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Gap between a 12 and a 26 EMA | Close relative to the high-low range |
| Created by | Gerald Appel, 1979 (histogram added by Thomas Aspray, 1986) | George Lane, 1950s |
| Default settings | 12, 26, 9 | 14, 3, 3; levels 80 / 20 |
| Scale | Unbounded | Fixed, 0 to 100 |
| Uses highs and lows? | No, closes only | Yes |
| Best market state | Trends, reversals | Ranges, reversals |
| Main weakness | Lags, and whipsaws in choppy ranges | Stays overbought in strong trends |

## When to use MACD

[MACD](/macd/) suits traders who want to follow a trend and see when its momentum is building or fading. The zero line shows which moving average is on top, the signal line crossover marks momentum shifts, and the histogram shows whether the gap is widening or narrowing. It is smoother than the Stochastic and gives fewer signals, but it lags more.

## When to use the Stochastic Oscillator

The [Stochastic Oscillator](/stochastic-oscillator/) suits traders who want earlier turns inside a range. It reacts as soon as price closes near the edge of its recent high-low range, so %K crossing %D near 20 or 80 can line up with the edges of a sideways market. It is quicker than MACD, which also means more false signals in choppy conditions.

## Using both together

A common pairing uses MACD for direction and the Stochastic for timing. For example, a trader might only take Stochastic buy crosses from low levels while MACD is above its zero line. Both are momentum tools, so agreement between them is weaker confirmation than a signal from something different, such as [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) or [volume](/volume/).

## Common mix-ups

- **Looking for overbought levels on MACD.** MACD has no fixed scale, so 80 and 20 mean nothing there.
- **Mixing up fast and slow Stochastic.** Platforms label the two versions differently, so check which one is plotted.
- **Reading Stochastic extremes as sell signals.** It can stay above 80 for long periods in a strong uptrend.
- **Treating two momentum tools as two confirmations.** They are both built from past prices and often agree for the same reason.

## FAQ

**Which is better, MACD or Stochastic?**
Neither is better in general. MACD suits trends and momentum shifts; the Stochastic suits timing entries in ranges. Pick the one that fits the market state and your time frame, and test it.

**Which one is faster?**
The Stochastic is usually faster, because it reacts to where the close sits in the recent range. MACD is built from two exponential moving averages, so it lags more.

**Does the Stochastic work in trends?**
It is weaker there. In a strong trend it can sit near an extreme for a long time, which makes overbought and oversold readings misleading.

**Can I put both on one chart?**
Yes. Many traders do, using MACD for direction and the Stochastic for timing, while remembering that both are momentum tools.
