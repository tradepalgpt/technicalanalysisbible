Support and resistance mark price areas where past turning points repeat, so a level gains weight each time price reacts there. Supply and demand zones mark the base before a sharp move, drawn as a box, on the idea that unfilled orders remain there. The two overlap heavily; they differ mainly in how areas are found and drawn.

## At a glance

| | Support and resistance | Supply and demand zones |
|---|---|---|
| Found from | Repeated turning points | The base before a strong move |
| Drawn as | A line or a thin zone | A box around the base |
| Key evidence | Several touches | A sharp, impulsive departure |
| Created by | Not attributed to one person | Not attributed to one person; often linked to the teaching of Sam Seiden |
| Retired when | Price closes clearly through it | Price closes clearly through it |
| Best market state | Ranges, breakouts, reversals | Ranges, reversals |
| Main weakness | Price may slice through in strong trends | Drawing the box involves judgement |

## When to use support and resistance

[Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) suits traders who want levels backed by repeated reactions. A level that has turned price two or three times is more believable than one touched once. It is easy to mark and widely watched, and round numbers, previous highs and lows and pivots often line up with it. In a strong trend, price can pass through levels without pausing.

## When to use supply and demand zones

[Supply and demand zones](/supply-and-demand-zones/) suit traders who focus on where a strong move began. The box covers the pause before the move, and the idea is that price may react when it returns. Zones can be useful after one clear impulse, even before a level has been touched several times. The drawing involves choices, such as whether to use candle bodies or wicks, so two traders may draw different boxes.

## Using both together

When a zone sits on a level that has already held several times, the area has more than one reason to matter. For example, a demand zone sitting on a well-tested support level is a stronger area of interest than either alone. Neither guarantees a reaction, so many traders also check the trend and [volume](/volume/).

## Common mix-ups

- **Thinking the two are unrelated.** Both mark areas where price has reacted or may react.
- **Treating a zone as a line.** A zone has width, and a level should be drawn as an area too.
- **Keeping broken areas on the chart.** Both are retired once price closes clearly through them.
- **Assuming a first return always holds.** A zone can fail on its first retest.

## FAQ

**Which is better, support and resistance or supply and demand?**
Neither is better in general. Support and resistance relies on repeated touches. Supply and demand relies on the strength of the move that left the area. Choose the method you can apply consistently.

**Are supply and demand zones just wider levels?**
Often they overlap with levels, but the evidence differs. A zone comes from one strong departure, while a level builds up from repeated reactions.

**Can a level flip roles?**
Yes. After a close through resistance, that area may act as support, which is called role reversal.

**How do I know a zone has failed?**
A close clearly through it is the usual sign.
