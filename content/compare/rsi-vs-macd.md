RSI and MACD are both momentum tools, but they answer different questions. RSI is a bounded 0 to 100 oscillator that compares recent gains with recent losses, so it suits spotting stretched moves in ranges. MACD plots the gap between two moving averages with no upper or lower limit, so it suits judging trend direction and momentum shifts.

## At a glance

| | RSI | MACD |
|---|---|---|
| Measures | Average gains vs average losses | Gap between a 12 and a 26 EMA |
| Created by | J. Welles Wilder Jr., 1978 | Gerald Appel, 1979 (histogram added by Thomas Aspray, 1986) |
| Default settings | 14; levels 70 / 30 | 12, 26, 9 |
| Scale | Fixed, 0 to 100 | Unbounded, in price units |
| Built from | Closing price changes | Exponential moving averages |
| Best market state | Ranges, reversals | Trends, reversals |
| Main weakness | Stays overbought in strong trends | Lags, and whipsaws in choppy ranges |

## When to use RSI

[RSI](/rsi/) suits traders who want a clear gauge of how stretched a move is. Its fixed scale makes readings easy to compare across markets and time frames. The 70 and 30 levels give a quick view of overbought and oversold conditions, and its 50 line can serve as a simple trend filter. It tends to be most useful when price is moving sideways or when a trend is losing steam.

## When to use MACD

[MACD](/macd/) suits traders who want to follow the trend and see when its momentum is building or fading. The zero line shows whether the fast average sits above or below the slow one, the signal line crossover marks shifts in momentum, and the histogram shows how that gap is changing. Because it is built from moving averages, it fits trending markets better than flat ones.

## Using both together

Some traders let MACD set the backdrop and RSI handle the timing. For example, they might only look at RSI pullbacks toward 40 or 50 while MACD is above its zero line. Both tools are built from past closes, so they often agree for the same reason. Agreement between them is weaker confirmation than a signal from something different, such as [support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/).

## Common mix-ups

- **Treating MACD as bounded.** MACD has no fixed top or bottom, so there are no standard overbought or oversold levels.
- **Comparing MACD values across markets.** The line is in price units, so a reading of 3.0 means different things on different instruments.
- **Calling RSI a trend indicator.** It measures the balance of recent gains and losses, and it can stay at extreme levels for a long time in a strong trend.
- **Counting both as separate confirmations.** They are both momentum tools built from past closes.

## FAQ

**Which is better, RSI or MACD?**
Neither is better in general. RSI is built for judging how stretched a move is, mostly in ranges. MACD is built for reading trend and momentum shifts. Choose the one that matches the market state you are trading, and test it yourself.

**Does MACD have overbought and oversold levels?**
Not in a standard way. MACD is unbounded, so any "extreme" depends on the instrument and its history. RSI has fixed levels, usually 70 and 30.

**Can I use RSI and MACD together?**
Yes, many traders do. Keep in mind that both measure momentum from past closes, so their agreement is not as strong as agreement with a different kind of evidence.

**Which one lags more?**
MACD usually lags more, because it is built from two exponential moving averages. RSI is also based on past closes, but its divergences can warn of fading momentum earlier.
