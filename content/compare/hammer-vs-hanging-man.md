The hammer and the hanging man are the same candle shape: a small body near the top and a long lower wick. The only difference is where they appear. A hammer follows a fall and is read as a possible bullish turn. A hanging man follows a rise and is read as a bearish warning. Both need confirmation from the next candle.

## At a glance

| | Hammer | Hanging Man |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | Small body at the top, long lower wick | Identical |
| Appears after | A decline | A rise |
| Read as | Possible bullish reversal | Bearish warning |
| Best location | Support, end of a fall | Resistance, end of an advance |
| Strength on its own | Modest | Weaker, since the close is near the high |
| Confirmation | Next candle closes above the hammer's body or high | Next candle bearish, ideally closing below the body |
| Origin | Japanese candlestick charting; Steve Nison, 1991 | Same |

## When to use Hammer

The [Hammer](/hammer/) is a tool for spotting that selling may be running out of force. After a clear fall, the long lower wick shows sellers pushed price down and buyers took it back before the close. It carries most weight at a known support level or the lower edge of a range. Traders often wait for the next candle to close above it, and treat a drop below its low as a cancelled idea.

## When to use Hanging Man

The [Hanging Man](/hanging-man/) is a caution flag after a rally. The candle itself closes near its high, so it looks fairly bullish. What it shows is that sellers were strong enough to push price far lower during the period. It is more useful near resistance or after a long advance, and it needs a bearish candle afterwards before it means much.

## Using both together

You do not choose between them, because the trend before the candle decides which name applies. Many traders read both with the same checklist: a clear prior move, a known level nearby, and a confirming next candle. [Support and resistance](/support-and-resistance/) and [volume](/volume/) help for either one. If you test them, keep the wick rule the same for both so results can be compared.

## Common mix-ups

- **Treating them as different shapes.** They are identical. Only the prior trend differs.
- **Naming the candle before checking the trend.** The same candle can be a hammer in one place and a hanging man in another.
- **Acting on the candle alone.** Both are warnings. The hanging man is the weaker one.
- **Ignoring location.** Either candle in the middle of a move, far from any level, is easy to dismiss.
- **Expecting a big reversal.** Both point to a possible pause or turn, not a target.

## FAQ

**Is a hanging man the same as a hammer?**
The shape is the same. The meaning is not. A hammer follows a fall and is read as bullish. A hanging man follows a rise and is read as a bearish warning. The trend before the candle decides the name.

**Which is the stronger signal?**
The hammer is usually seen as slightly stronger, because its close near the high fits the idea of buyers taking control after a fall. The hanging man closes near its high too, which makes it look bullish and is why it needs firmer confirmation.

**Does body colour matter for either?**
A little. A hollow hammer, closing above its open, shows a stronger recovery. A filled hanging man, closing below its open, is seen as slightly more bearish. Shape and confirmation matter more than colour.

**What cancels each pattern?**
A hammer is cancelled if price falls below its low. A hanging man is cancelled if the next candle closes above its high, since bulls are still in control.
