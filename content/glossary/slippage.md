Slippage is the difference between the price a trader expects when placing an order and the price at which the order is actually filled.

## Explained

Prices move between the moment an order is sent and the moment it is executed. If nothing is available at the expected price, the order fills at the next one. Slippage can be positive, giving a better price, but it is more often a cost.

It tends to be larger in fast markets, around news releases, in thinly traded instruments, and on large orders that must be filled across several price levels. Stop orders are especially exposed, because they become market orders once triggered, often during a sharp move. Looking at [depth of market](/depth-of-market/) shows how much volume sits at each price.

Slippage matters for risk. A stop at 98 may fill at 97.50, so the real loss is larger than planned. Allowing for it in [position sizing](/position-sizing/) keeps a bad fill from exceeding the intended loss.

## Example

A trader places a buy stop at 100.00 after a breakout. News hits, and the market jumps. The order fills at 100.40. Slippage is 0.40 per share, so on 500 shares the extra cost is 200. The stop at 99.00 now sits 1.40 away, not 1.00.
